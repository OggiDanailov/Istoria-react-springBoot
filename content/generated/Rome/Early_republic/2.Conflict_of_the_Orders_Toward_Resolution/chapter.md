# Early Republic: The Conflict of the Orders - Toward Resolution (450-367 BCE)

## Patricians, Plebeians and a Puzzling Office {#conflict-of-the-orders}

In the early Roman Republic, the city was divided in two. On one side stood the patricians, a small hereditary aristocracy who owned large estates, lent money and believed that ruling was their birthright. On the other stood the plebeians, the great majority of ordinary Romans.

Their rivalry lasted some two hundred years and is known as the Conflict of the Orders. It shaped Roman politics throughout the Republic's first two centuries. The patricians held power by monopolising the consulship, the two top offices of state filled each year. They also held the seats of the Senate, Rome's powerful council of elders. Many plebeians, especially small farmers, were buried in debt and short of land.

From 444 BC, Rome often replaced its two consuls with a stranger arrangement. In those years it was run by between three and six “military tribunes with consular power”, usually called consular tribunes. The Roman historian Livy, writing centuries later, said this office was created as a concession because it was open to plebeians.

The record tells a different story. Between 444 and 401 BC, only two of about a hundred consular tribunes were plebeians. Around 400 BC this changed, and in some years plebeians made up the majority. Consular tribunes governed Rome in many years up to 376 BC.

## The Fall of Veii {#siege-of-veii}

Veii was a large Etruscan city just 16 km (10 miles) from Rome. The Etruscans were a wealthy people whose cities lay in what is now Tuscany. For years Rome and Veii fought a long, seesawing war, with victories and defeats on both sides.

Around 396 BC, after a string of Roman setbacks, the Romans chose Marcus Furius Camillus as dictator. In the Republic, a dictator was an emergency commander given sweeping powers for six months, not a tyrant. When Camillus took over, Rome was already besieging Veii, and the defenders had destroyed much of the Roman siege equipment.

So the Romans went underground. They dug a tunnel through the soft volcanic rock on which Veii stood. According to Livy, Camillus vowed a tenth of the spoils to the god Apollo. He also invited Juno, Veii's protecting goddess, to move to Rome and receive a worthy temple there.

Camillus then threw his larger army at the walls from every side. The astonished defenders rushed to man them. Meanwhile, picked Roman troops climbed out of the tunnel inside Juno's own temple and overwhelmed the city.

The survivors were enslaved and Romans resettled the site. The destruction of Veii secured Rome's growing place in central Italy.

## Disaster at the Allia {#battle-of-the-allia}

The Senones were a Gallic tribe, one of several Celtic peoples who had recently pushed into northern Italy. They settled on the Adriatic coast around modern Rimini.

According to Livy, a man named Aruns brought the Senones to Clusium, an Etruscan town now called Chiusi. He wanted revenge on a local nobleman whose son had seduced his wife. Alarmed, Clusium appealed to Rome. Rome sent three brothers of the powerful Fabius family as ambassadors. When fighting broke out, the brothers joined in and one killed a Gallic chieftain. This broke the rule that envoys must stay neutral. The Gauls demanded the brothers be handed over. Instead, the Romans elected them consular tribunes for the next year. Enraged, the Gauls marched on Rome.

The armies met where the Allia brook joins the river Tiber, about 16 km north of the city. Livy says the Romans pitched no camp and failed to consult the gods. They also stretched their line dangerously thin. Brennus, the Gallic chief, attacked the Roman reserves on a hill, and panic spread. Many Romans drowned in the Tiber. Most survivors fled to Veii, while others ran back to Rome.

Tradition dates the battle to 390 BC, but modern historians prefer around 387 BC. Its anniversary, 18 July, became an ill-omened day.

## The Gauls in Rome {#sack-of-rome}

According to Livy, the Gauls found Rome's gates open and its walls unguarded. The fighting men and able-bodied senators had withdrawn to the Capitoline, Rome's fortified citadel hill. The Vestal Virgins, priestesses who guarded the sacred hearth of the city, carried off the sacred objects. A passing citizen, Lucius Albinus, gave them a lift on his wagon to Caere, an allied Etruscan city on the coast.

The old men who had held high office stayed behind. They sat outside their houses on ivory chairs, dressed in their ceremonial robes. The Gauls stared at them as if they were statues. Then one Gaul stroked the long beard of Marcus Papirius, who struck him with his ivory staff. The massacre began, and houses were plundered and burned.

One night the Gauls scaled the Capitoline cliff. The guards and dogs slept, but the sacred geese of Juno honked. Their noise roused Marcus Manlius, a former consul, who hurled the first attacker down the slope.

Famine and disease finally forced a deal: a ransom of about 330 kg (a thousand pounds) of gold. When the Romans complained that the Gauls were using rigged weights, Brennus threw his sword onto the scales and declared, “Woe to the vanquished!” In Livy's telling, Camillus arrived just in time, cancelled the deal and routed the Gauls.

## Did Camillus Really Save Rome? {#sack-reliability}

All accounts of the Allia and the sack of Rome were written centuries after the events, so their reliability is doubtful. The biggest suspect is the heroic rescue by Camillus, the conqueror of Veii. According to Livy, Camillus was in exile at Ardea, a town south of Rome, and was recalled as dictator. But the Greek historians Polybius and Diodorus Siculus never mention him. Many modern historians see his role as a later addition.

Other ancient writers told different stories. Diodorus says an Etruscan army defeated the Gauls as they returned from southern Italy. The geographer Strabo claims that Caere, Rome's Etruscan ally, beat them and recovered the ransom gold. The Greek philosopher Aristotle reportedly said Rome's saviour was “a certain Lucius”. That might be Lucius Albinus, the man who carried the priestesses to Caere.

The historian T. J. Cornell doubts the romantic tale of Aruns's revenge. No account mentions Gallic wives or children, so the Senones looked like a warrior band, not migrants seeking land. He thinks they were mercenaries. A few months later, Dionysius I, the ruler of Syracuse in Sicily, hired Gauls for a war in southern Italy.

Archaeology finds no layer of destruction from this date. Scorch marks once linked to the sack have been redated to the fall of the monarchy over a century earlier.

## Recovery and a Lasting Fear {#rome-after-the-sack}

Livy wrote that Rome was burned and then rebuilt in a hurry, leaving it with tangled streets. Cornell thinks the Gauls mainly wanted loot. In his view they left most buildings standing and went home once they were paid. Haphazard street plans, he notes, were common in ancient cities anyway.

Rome bounced back quickly. It bound newly conquered Veii closer by giving its people citizenship without the right to vote. It also strengthened its alliance with Caere. A few years after the sack, Rome began a huge new city wall, 11 km long. The old wall had used crumbly local stone. The new one was built of a better yellow stone quarried in Veii's territory.

The memory of the Gauls cast a long shadow. Gallic raiders struck the surrounding region in 350 and 349 BC. Even after Rome crushed the Senones in 295 BC, fear persisted. In 228, 216 and 114 BC, Romans buried alive a pair of Gauls and a pair of Greeks, although human sacrifice was not a Roman custom.

Later writers treated the anniversary of the Allia as a day of ritual prohibition, when public business stopped. Some scholars caution, however, that this custom is attested only from the 1st century BC and may be a later invention.

## Ten Years of Struggle {#licinius-and-sextius}

In 375 BC, two tribunes of the plebs, Gaius Licinius Stolo and Lucius Sextius Lateranus, launched a campaign that would last a decade. Tribunes were officials elected by the plebeians to protect them, and they could block official business with a veto.

The pair put three bills to the Plebeian Council, the assembly of the plebeians. One eased debt and one limited land holdings. The third abolished the consular tribunes, restored the consuls and required one consul to be a plebeian.

The patricians were furious about the last bill. They persuaded other tribunes to veto it. Licinius and Sextius struck back by blocking the election of consular tribunes for five years. They gave way only in 370 BC, when Velitrae, a town of the Volscians, a people south-east of Rome, attacked Roman territory.

The pair were re-elected year after year. In 368 BC the Senate named Camillus dictator, and he threatened violence before resigning for unclear reasons. The plebeians passed the land and debt bills but rejected the consulship bill. The tribunes insisted on all or nothing.

A second dictator, Publius Manlius Capitolinus, angered patricians by picking a plebeian deputy. Licinius and Sextius won a tenth term, and their whole package went through. In 367 BC, battered by years of strife, the Senate accepted it.

## Debt, Land and Sacred Books {#licinian-laws}

The laws of Licinius and Sextius tackled the plebeians' most painful problems. Debt weighed heavily on small farmers, whose creditors were usually wealthy patricians. Under the new debt law, interest already paid was subtracted from the sum owed. The rest could be paid off in three equal yearly instalments.

The land law targeted public land owned by the Roman state. Ordinary citizens received small plots of about half a hectare (one and a quarter acres), barely enough to feed a family. Rich landowners, meanwhile, grabbed huge swathes of public land for themselves. The law limited any individual to about 120 hectares (300 acres) of it. It also banned grazing more than 100 cattle there, to protect the common pasture poor farmers relied on. Such laws were easy to dodge. Licinius himself was later fined for breaking his own limit.

A fourth law concerned the Sibylline Books, a collection of prophecies. According to tradition, Tarquinius Superbus, Rome's seventh and last king, bought them from a prophetess at Cumae, a Greek city near Naples. Two patrician priests guarded the books and consulted them in times of plague, famine or war. The new law replaced them with ten priests, five of them plebeians. For the first time, the patricians' monopoly on priesthoods was broken.

## The First Plebeian Consul {#first-plebeian-consul}

The most famous of the laws of Licinius and Sextius ended government by consular tribunes. It brought back the two consuls each year and required one of them to be a plebeian.

In 366 BC Lucius Sextius Lateranus, one of the two tribunes who had led the long campaign, became Rome's first plebeian consul. The patricians refused to confirm his election, and riots broke out. The plebeians came close to seceding, that is, staging a mass walkout from the city, as they had done before.

According to Livy, the aged Camillus brokered a compromise. The nobles accepted a plebeian consul. In return, the plebeians agreed that a new official, the praetor, would be a patrician. The praetor served as Rome's chief judge, presiding over criminal trials. He could also command an army. Livy concluded that the two orders were “at length brought into harmony”.

The new arrangement did not end every barrier. Plebeians had to wait thirty years, until 337 BC, before one of them was elected praetor. Other priesthoods opened to plebeians over time, although patricians kept some of the oldest for themselves.

## What Historians Make of It {#modern-verdict}

The historian T. J. Cornell believes very little of Livy's dramatic story can be trusted. However, he considers the actual changes in government reasonably certain.

He also finds the consulship law puzzling. Plebeians had already served as consular tribunes, so why was a plebeian consul such a landmark? And the law did not guarantee sharing. From 355 to 343 BC, both consuls were patricians. Regular sharing began only after the law of Lucius Genucius, a tribune, in 342 BC. That law apparently required one consul to be a plebeian. Two plebeians did not hold the consulship together until 173 BC. Seen this way, the 367 BC law may have been a mere administrative adjustment.

Cornell sees its real importance elsewhere. Earlier plebeian officeholders, he argues, were dependants of patrician patrons with no ties to the plebeian movement. Licinius and Sextius opened top office to the movement's own leaders. These were wealthy, ambitious men who used the cause of the poor to enter the ruling class.

Once inside, they formed a new aristocracy with the patricians and turned their backs on the poor. Ordinary plebeians gained temporary relief but lost control of their organisation. The tribunate became a stepping stone for young nobles, and Livy called some tribunes “slaves of the nobility”.
