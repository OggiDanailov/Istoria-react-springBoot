# Early Republic: Final Conquest of Italy (290-264 BCE)

## Rome After the Samnite Wars {#rome-after-the-samnite-wars}

By 290 BC, Rome had fought its way to the top of central Italy. Its toughest rivals had been the Samnites, a warlike coalition of tribes from the Apennine mountains. Over three Samnite Wars (343–341, 326–304 and 298–290 BC), the two sides traded victories and heavy losses.

The turning point came in 295 BC at Sentinum, a town in Umbria in north-central Italy. There the Romans faced a mixed alliance of Samnites, Gauls, Etruscans and Umbrians. The Gauls were Celtic warriors from the north, and the Etruscans were Rome's old neighbours in Etruria, the region just north of the city. When Rome beat even this combined army, it became clear that nobody could stop it from dominating Italy.

Rome tightened its hold with concrete measures. It founded colonies (*coloniae*), which were settlements of loyal citizens planted on conquered land. It also built the Appian Way (*Via Appia*), a great road running south from Rome toward Capua, which carried armies and influence deep into the south.

In 282 BC, a battle at Populonia, an Etruscan town on the coast, ended the last traces of Etruscan power in the region. Yet the far south of Italy, with its wealthy Greek cities, still lay beyond Rome's control.

## The Hortensian Law of 287 BC {#lex-hortensia}

While Rome was winning wars abroad, it was also settling a long quarrel at home. For two centuries, Roman society had been split between the patricians (*patricii*), a small hereditary elite, and the plebeians (*plebs*), the mass of ordinary citizens. Historians call this struggle the Conflict of the Orders.

The plebeians had a powerful weapon: the secession (*secessio*). They would simply walk out of the city in protest, leaving Rome without workers or soldiers. According to tradition, around 287 BC they did this one more time, gathering on the Janiculum, a hill across the River Tiber.

To end the crisis, Rome appointed Quintus Hortensius as dictator. In Rome this was a temporary emergency office, not a tyrant, and Hortensius was himself a plebeian. His law, the *lex Hortensia*, made every decision (*plebiscitum*) of the Plebeian Council (*concilium plebis*) binding on all citizens, patricians included.

Earlier laws in 449 and 339 BC had moved in this direction. However, they still needed the Senate (*senatus*), Rome's council of elders and ex-officials, to approve plebeian decisions. The new law removed that requirement entirely.

In theory, patricians and plebeians now had equal political rights. The law made the people and their elected champions, the tribunes (*tribuni plebis*), major players in Roman politics, and it brought the Conflict of the Orders to a close.

## Why Did the Plebeians Walk Out? {#why-the-plebs-seceded}

Ancient accounts of the last plebeian secession are not fully trusted today. The Roman tradition, preserved in yearly chronicles and by the historian Livy, blamed debt. In this version, angry city crowds in the Forum, Rome's central square, revolted when called up to fight the Lucanians, a people of southern Italy. Rome needed soldiers, so the protesters had leverage.

Modern historians doubt this picture. Large masses of urban poor probably did not exist in Rome at this time. Voting in the Plebeian Council was also organised by tribal districts (*tribus*), never more than 35. Rural landowners controlled 29 of them, so they, not city crowds, held most of the power.

A likelier explanation, scholars argue, is land. The Third Samnite War had won Rome large tracts of public land (*ager publicus*), and rural plebeians wanted a say in how it was handed out.

Still, the harsh measures taken by Rome's top officials, the consuls (*consules*), suggest there was real unrest in the city too. With both town and countryside demanding change, and the army needing men, reform became unavoidable. Such a well-crafted law probably also needed organisers, most likely the plebeian tribunes. The law did contain one oddity: it banned votes on market days (*nundinae*), which made it harder for country farmers to vote when they came to town.

## Tarentum and the Road to War {#tarentum-calls-for-help}

Southern Italy was home to *Magna Graecia*, or Great Greece, a string of rich Greek cities founded by colonists centuries earlier. As Rome pushed south after defeating the Samnites, it began making alliances with some of these cities.

One ally was Thurii, a Greek city on the Gulf of Taranto. When Thurii came under attack from a Samnite-led alliance, Rome stepped in to help. This alarmed Tarentum, the most powerful Greek city in the south, which stood on the instep of Italy's boot and wanted no Roman meddling in its backyard.

Tarentum struck first. It attacked a Roman fleet sailing in its waters, which an earlier treaty had forbidden. It then marched on Thurii and threw out the city's pro-Roman government.

Rome sent an ultimatum, which the Tarentines rejected in early 281 BC, and war followed. Tarentum knew its own army could not match Rome's in open battle. So it looked abroad for a champion and found one in Pyrrhus, king of Epirus, a Greek kingdom in what is now north-western Greece and Albania. Pyrrhus landed at Tarentum with his troops in the winter of 281/280 BC. It was the first major clash between Rome and one of the great Greek kingdoms that had arisen after Alexander the Great.

## Heraclea, Asculum and the Pyrrhic Victory {#heraclea-and-asculum}

Pyrrhus of Epirus was one of the most famous generals of his age, and at first he lived up to his reputation. In 280 BC he defeated the Romans at Heraclea in Lucania, a region of southern Italy. Some of Rome's allies promptly switched sides.

Pyrrhus then marched quickly north into Latium, Rome's home region, hoping to link up with the Etruscans, who were still fighting Rome. But the Romans won their war in Etruria, and Pyrrhus pulled back to spend the winter in the south.

The Romans refused to talk peace. In 279 BC they met Pyrrhus again at Asculum, in south-eastern Italy, and once more they lost. Yet Pyrrhus's own army had suffered terribly. According to ancient tradition, he exclaimed after the battle: "Another such victory and we are lost!"

That remark gave the English language a lasting phrase. A "Pyrrhic victory" is a win that costs the victor so much that it is almost a defeat.

For the moment, though, Pyrrhus had driven the Romans out of southern Italy. Rather than press on against Rome, he turned his attention to a new prize across the water: the island of Sicily.

## Pyrrhus in Sicily {#pyrrhus-in-sicily}

After his costly victories over Rome, Pyrrhus of Epirus crossed to Sicily. He came to help Syracuse, the leading Greek city on the island, in its struggle against Carthage. Carthage was a wealthy trading city in North Africa, in modern Tunisia, which controlled much of western Sicily. By now Pyrrhus's goals had grown far beyond rescuing Tarentum. He aimed to dominate both southern Italy and Sicily.

At first the campaign went well, and Pyrrhus won over much of the island. But he could not capture Lilybaeum, Carthage's great fortress on Sicily's western tip. The city could be supplied by sea, and Pyrrhus lacked the naval strength to cut it off. The war there ground to a stalemate.

Meanwhile, his Sicilian supporters were growing tired of paying for the war. Back in Italy, Rome had gone on the attack again between 279 and 276 BC, pressing the Greek cities and their allies.

When Tarentum sent a fresh plea for help, Pyrrhus agreed to return. He crossed back to southern Italy in early 276 BC, suffering some losses on the way. His Sicilian adventure had brought him nothing lasting.

## Beneventum and the End of Pyrrhus's War {#beneventum-and-withdrawal}

In 275 BC, Pyrrhus of Epirus faced the Romans one last time, at Beneventum, a town in the Samnite hill country of southern Italy. The outcome is described differently: some accounts call it a Roman victory, others a draw. Either way, it forced Pyrrhus onto the defensive.

The king now saw that the war in Italy could not be won. In the winter of 275/274 BC he sailed home to Epirus, leaving only a small garrison (*praesidium*) of his soldiers in Tarentum.

His campaign had briefly revived the hopes of Rome's enemies in the south. These included the Samnites, the Lucanians, the Bruttians of Italy's toe, and the Italian Greeks. But their gains did not last.

In 272 BC, about two years after Pyrrhus left, Tarentum fell to Rome. The Epirote garrison was allowed to go home in peace. That same year Pyrrhus himself was killed while fighting a different war at Argos, a city in southern Greece.

Rome's victory over a famous Greek king hugely boosted its standing across the Mediterranean world. Its control of the Italian peninsula would not be seriously challenged again until Hannibal invaded at the end of the century.

## How Rome Held Italy Together {#how-rome-ruled-italy}

With Pyrrhus gone, Rome moved quickly to take over southern Italy. Instead of ruling the Greek cities of Magna Graecia directly, it bound most of them through treaties. This gave Rome a kind of indirect control.

The burden on these Greek cities seems to have been light. Even at the start of Rome's next great war in 264 BC, the only recorded help was a fleet of transport ships borrowed from Naples, Tarentum and Locri, a Greek city on Italy's toe. Apart from Roman garrisons in many cities, Rome's grip in the south remained loose for decades.

Across Italy, Rome used several tools to absorb conquered peoples. It confiscated land and planted colonies on it. It granted some communities full Roman citizenship (*civitas*), and others a partial version without the right to vote (*civitas sine suffragio*). It also made military alliances with states, its allies (*socii*), that stayed independent in name but had to supply soldiers.

This system turned Italy into a vast reservoir of fighting men, larger than that of any other state of the time.

## Italy Conquered: 264 BC {#italy-united-264-bc}

The final pieces of Rome's conquest of Italy fell into place in the 260s BC. The last resistance in Etruria, the old Etruscan heartland north of Rome, was crushed in 265–264 BC.

In 264 BC, the same year, Rome went to war with Carthage, the North African trading power that had once been its ally. This conflict, the First Punic War, lasted until 241 BC. "Punic" comes from *Punici*, the Romans' name for the Carthaginians, who were of Phoenician origin. It took Roman armies outside the Italian peninsula for the first time, beginning with the fight for Sicily. Rome's attention had turned south and across the sea partly because the Alps blocked the way north, and because Rome was not yet ready to take on the Gauls who lived beneath them.

In barely a quarter of a century, Rome had changed enormously. It had settled its long internal struggle between patricians and plebeians. It had defeated the Samnites and their allies. And it had driven off Pyrrhus, a famous Greek king.

Rome entered 264 BC as the undisputed master of peninsular Italy, with a fearsome military reputation and an enormous supply of soldiers. The stage was set for its rise from an Italian power to a Mediterranean one.
