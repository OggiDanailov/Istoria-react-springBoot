# Early Republic: The Samnite Wars (367-290 BCE)

## The Samnites and the Road to War {#samnites-and-rome}

In the mid-fourth century BC, Rome's territory reached south as far as the River Liris. This river divided Latium, the Latin homeland around Rome, from Campania, the fertile plain running down to the Bay of Naples. Beyond that plain rose the Apennine Mountains. There lived the Samnites, a confederation of hill tribes and the strongest people in the region.

The Samnites spoke Oscan, an Italian language shared with several neighbours. The Campanians who farmed the plain below were descended from Samnite migrants. By now, though, they had their own identity and their own rich cities, chief among them Capua, just north of Naples.

In 354 BC, according to the Roman historian Livy (*Titus Livius*) and the Greek historian Diodorus, Rome and the Samnites signed a treaty, but neither records its terms. Modern historians suspect it made the Liris a dividing line, with Rome's sphere to the north and the Samnites' to the south. That line would collapse into three wars, fought between 343 and 290 BC, that decided who would dominate Italy.

## How the First Samnite War Began (343 BC) {#first-war-outbreak}

Our main guide to these wars is Livy, a Roman historian writing about three centuries later. According to him, the first war began almost by accident. The Samnites attacked the Sidicini, a small people north of Campania whose stronghold controlled a valuable crossroads. The Campanians came to the Sidicini's aid and were beaten twice, and the Samnites drove them back behind the walls of Capua.

Desperate, the Campanians sent ambassadors to the Roman Senate (*senatus*), the council of leading men who steered Rome's policy. They offered an alliance and their famous wealth. The senators, Livy says, were tempted but refused, since this would betray their treaty with the Samnites. So the envoys played their last card: they surrendered Capua and all Campania unconditionally to Rome, a surrender the Romans called *deditio*.

Now Roman honour was at stake. When Roman envoys asked the Samnites to spare what had become Roman property, Samnite leaders ordered their armies into Campania within earshot. Rome's fetials (*fetiales*), priests who handled formal demands and declarations of war, sought redress, were refused, and war followed.

## Three Victories, a Mutiny and a Peace (343–341 BC) {#first-war-campaigns}

In 343 BC both of Rome's consuls (*consules*), the two chief magistrates elected each year, marched out. Livy credits them with three victories. Marcus Valerius Corvus won the first at Mount Gaurus, near the coastal town of Cumae, with a desperate charge as daylight faded.

In the second, the Samnites trapped the other consul, Aulus Cornelius Cossus, in a mountain pass. A junior officer, Publius Decius Mus, seized a hilltop with a small band and distracted the enemy while the army escaped. In the third, Valerius rushed to Suessula, a town on Campania's eastern edge, captured the Samnite camp and scattered their foragers. Both consuls celebrated triumphs (*triumphi*), Rome's victory parades.

In 342 BC, the sources say, Roman garrison troops seduced by Campanian luxury plotted a coup and mutinied. Valerius, named dictator, an emergency ruler with sweeping powers, talked them down without bloodshed.

In 341 BC the Samnites asked for peace and the old treaty was renewed. Rome left the Sidicini to the Samnites but kept its alliance with Capua, a far greater prize.

## Can We Trust Livy's First Samnite War? {#doubting-livy}

Modern historians treat Livy's account of the first war cautiously. His Campanian ambassadors give a speech closely modelled on a scene in Thucydides, the Greek historian of the Peloponnesian War, where envoys from Corcyra beg Athens for help. The defiant Samnite assembly is also unlikely to rest on any genuine record.

The fiercest debate concerns Capua's surrender. Scholars such as Salmon and Forsythe think it was invented, projected back from Capua's real surrender in 211 BC, when Rome punished it for siding with Carthage, the North African trading power. The tale would clear Rome of treaty-breaking and blame scheming Campanians. Others, such as Cornell and Oakley, accept it, noting that voluntary submission was common diplomacy then.

The battles are doubted too. Decius' hilltop rescue closely resembles a famous episode of 258 BC and may be borrowed from it. Some historians once rejected the whole war, since Diodorus never mentions it. Most now accept it: no Roman would invent a story so embarrassing to Rome.

## The Great Samnite War Begins (326 BC) {#second-war-outbreak}

The peace soon frayed. Through the 330s Rome kept pushing into the region, settling 2,500 colonists (*coloni*) in a new colony (*colonia*) at Cales, a town on key routes towards Capua and the Samnite mountains. Then in 328 BC Rome founded a colony at Fregellae, on the Liris, in an area the Samnites had recently overrun. They saw it as open aggression.

The spark came from Naples, then a Greek city made up of an old town, Paleopolis, and a newer one, Neapolis (Greek for "old city" and "new city"). Livy says Paleopolis attacked Romans living in Campania. Rome demanded redress, was rebuffed, and declared war. Samnite troops helped defend the city.

The siege produced a lasting innovation. Instead of handing over to the next year's consuls, the commanders, including Quintus Publilius Philo, had their military command (*imperium*) extended until the campaign ended, with the title of proconsul (*pro consule*, "in place of a consul"). In 326 BC two leading Neapolitans, fed up with the unruly Samnite garrison, let the Romans in. The Second, or Great, Samnite War would last until 304 BC.

## Humiliation at the Caudine Forks (321 BC) {#caudine-forks}

Rome's most famous disaster came in 321 BC. The Samnite commander Gaius Pontius sent soldiers disguised as shepherds to spread a false rumour that Luceria, a Roman ally in Apulia in south-eastern Italy, was under attack. The two consuls took the quickest road, through the Caudine Forks (*Furculae Caudinae*), two narrow wooded gorges in the Apennines joined by a tight ravine. The Samnites blocked both ends with felled trees and boulders, trapping the army.

According to the story, Pontius asked his elderly father Herennius for advice. Free them all and win Rome's friendship, the old man said, or kill them all and cripple Rome for years. A middle course would neither win friends nor remove enemies. Pontius chose it anyway. The Romans filed out unarmed under the yoke (*iugum*), a frame of spears symbolising subjugation, while the enemy jeered.

Rome went into mourning. Then, Livy says, the Senate refused to honour the agreement, arguing the people had never approved it, and the war resumed.

## From Defeat to Victory (320–304 BC) {#second-war-turning}

The Great Samnite War dragged on with reverses on both sides. Livy reports Roman revenge in 320 BC, when Luceria was taken and Roman hostages freed, though some historians think the truce that followed actually came from the Caudine agreement. When fighting resumed, the Samnites won at Lautulae in 315 BC.

Then a second front opened. In 311 BC the Etruscans, the wealthy city-states north of Rome, besieged Sutrium, which Rome saw as its key to Etruria. Quintus Fabius Maximus Rullianus, Rome's leading general of the era, sent his Etruscan-speaking brother, disguised as a shepherd, through the dreaded Ciminian Forest (*silva Ciminia*), which no Roman had ever crossed. Fabius then marched his army through it. In 310 BC, Livy says, a hard-fought victory at Lake Vadimo first broke Etruscan might.

In Samnium the Romans took Bovianum, capital of the Pentri, the largest Samnite tribe, in 305 BC. The next year the Samnites sued for peace and the old treaty was restored.

## The Battle of Sentinum (295 BC) {#third-war-sentinum}

The Third Samnite War broke out in 298 BC when the Lucanians, a people south of Samnium, asked Rome for protection against a Samnite invasion. The Greek historian Dionysius of Halicarnassus thought Rome acted from fear of Samnite power, not compassion.

The Samnites knew they could not win alone, so their commander Gellius Egnatius took an army north to rally the Etruscans. By 295 BC Rome faced a coalition of four peoples: Samnites, Etruscans, Umbrians from the hills of central Italy, and Senone Gauls, Celtic warriors on the Adriatic coast. Rome elected its best generals as consuls, Quintus Fabius Maximus Rullianus and Publius Decius Mus, son of the hero of 343 BC.

A Roman raid first drew the Etruscans home. At Sentinum, east of the Apennines, Gallic chariots broke Decius' wing. Decius then devoted himself (*devotio*): he prayed to the gods and charged into the enemy, sacrificing his life. His men rallied, Fabius outflanked the Samnites, and the coalition collapsed.

## Aquilonia and the Fall of Samnium (293–290 BC) {#aquilonia-and-the-end}

After Sentinum the Samnites fought on. In 293 BC they raised fresh troops, and some 40,000 men gathered at Aquilonia in north-western Samnium. Elite units, the Linen Legion (*legio linteata*), swore a sacred oath never to flee, on pain of death.

The consuls Lucius Papirius Cursor and Spurius Carvilius Maximus attacked Aquilonia and nearby Cominium at once. Tipped off by a deserter, Papirius intercepted twenty elite Samnite companies and took Aquilonia, while Cominium surrendered to Carvilius. One modern historian says Aquilonia sealed the Samnites' fate.

Livy's surviving books stop here; later events come from brief summaries. According to these, a Samnite commander named Gaius Pontius was captured, paraded in a triumph and beheaded. In 291 BC Rome took Venusia, commanding routes into Lucania, Apulia and Samnium, and founded there its largest colony ever. In 290 BC the last resistance was crushed.

## Why the Samnite Wars Mattered {#roman-alliance-system}

By 290 BC Rome had become the great power of Italy. Its territory now stretched across the Apennines to the Adriatic, and the Samnites were forced into an alliance with Rome on unequal terms (*foedus iniquum*, an "unequal treaty").

The secret was the alliance system. Allied peoples, the *socii* ("allies"), kept their own governments, but every treaty required them to send soldiers for Rome's wars at their own expense. In return they shared the spoils (*praeda*), which could be considerable, and enjoyed Roman protection. Rome also backed allied ruling families when trouble brewed at home.

The result was an enormous pool of soldiers. Allied troops already mattered at Sentinum, and later they often outnumbered the Romans. At the battle of Telamon against the Gauls in 225 BC, the Greek historian Polybius counted about 41,000 Romans alongside 210,000 allies. No other Mediterranean state could match such numbers, and this machine carried Rome to victory over Pyrrhus, a Greek king who invaded Italy, and over Carthage.
