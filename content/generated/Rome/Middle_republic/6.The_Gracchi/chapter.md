# Middle Republic: The Gracchi (133-121 BCE)

## Born to Rome's Elite {#family-of-the-gracchi}

Tiberius Gracchus and his younger brother Gaius Gracchus, the reformers remembered together as the Gracchi, did not come from the margins of Roman society. They were born at its very top. Tiberius arrived in 163 or 162 BC, and Gaius around 154 BC.

Their father, also called Tiberius, had twice been consul, one of the two heads of state elected each year. He had also served as censor, the senior official who kept the list of citizens, and had celebrated two triumphs, the victory parades Rome granted successful generals. One honoured him for bringing twenty years of peace to Spain.

Their mother, Cornelia, was the daughter of one of Rome's most celebrated generals. After her husband died, she devoted herself to raising and educating her children. She even turned down a marriage proposal from the king of Egypt. Her influence lasted: years later, Gaius dropped one of his own bills because Cornelia asked him to.

The family was woven into Rome's great clans. The brothers' sister married a leading general, and Tiberius married the daughter of a former consul from a rival family, perhaps as part of a plan to reconcile the two houses.

## Tiberius at War: Carthage and Numantia {#tiberius-at-war}

Tiberius Gracchus, the elder of the two reforming Gracchus brothers, began his military career in 147 BC. He served under his brother-in-law in the siege of Carthage (*Carthago*), Rome's great rival city in North Africa. According to one ancient biographer, he was among the first Romans to climb its walls.

In 137 BC he went to Spain as a quaestor (*quaestor*), a junior officer in charge of an army's finances. Rome was fighting Numantia, a fiercely independent hill town in north-central Spain. The campaign was a disaster. After losing several skirmishes, the Roman army fled in a confused night retreat and found itself surrounded.

The commanding consul sent Tiberius to negotiate, because the Numantines trusted his family name: his father had governed the region decades earlier. Tiberius saved the army, and even persuaded the enemy to hand back his captured account books.

But Rome refused to accept the treaty, calling it humiliating. The consul was stripped of his citizenship and handed over to the Numantines naked and bound. A proposal to send Tiberius in chains with him was defeated, but his promising career now badly needed rescuing.

## The Land Law of 133 BC {#the-land-law}

In 133 BC Tiberius Gracchus was elected tribune of the plebs (*tribunus plebis*), one of ten officials whose job was to protect ordinary citizens. He chose a burning issue: public land (*ager publicus*), territory Rome had taken in its conquests across Italy.

According to traditional accounts, wealthy families had occupied huge stretches of this land, running vast estates worked by slaves and pushing out small farmers. Tiberius believed that an old, widely ignored law limited how much public land anyone could hold to about 120 hectares (300 acres).

His agrarian law (*lex Sempronia agraria*) would enforce that limit and hand the surplus to poor Roman citizens. He argued that this would help the poor, slow the drift of country people into the city, and increase the number of men who owned enough property to qualify for army service. Tiberius blamed rich landowners for a supposed decline in Rome's population.

Country people flocked to Rome to support him. Many in the elite, who held large amounts of public land, fought the bill hard, though some senators and one of that year's consuls backed it. Rivals also suspected that Tiberius wanted a spectacular success to rebuild his own career after the Spanish fiasco.

## Breaking the Rules {#breaking-the-rules}

To pass his land reform, Tiberius Gracchus repeatedly broke the unwritten rules of Roman politics. He took his bill straight to the Plebeian Council (*concilium plebis*), the assembly of ordinary citizens, without first consulting the Senate (*senatus*), as custom expected.

The senators answered by persuading another tribune, possibly a former friend of Tiberius, to block the bill with his veto (*intercessio*). Tiberius had him physically removed and voted out of office, something without precedent and against the constitution. The bill then passed.

The Senate struck back by giving the new land commission almost no money. Then news arrived that the king of Pergamum, a rich Greek kingdom in western Asia Minor, had died and left his treasury and kingdom to Rome. Tiberius proposed using this windfall for his land scheme; the ancient sources disagree on exactly how. Finance and foreign affairs belonged to the Senate, and a senator accused Tiberius of wanting to make himself king.

Finally, Tiberius ran for a second term in a row. This may have been illegal, and it certainly broke custom: officials could not be prosecuted while in office, so endless re-election meant endless immunity. Historians note that his enemies feared these tactics more than the reform itself.

## Murder on the Capitoline {#death-of-tiberius}

In 133 BC, at the vote for the next year's tribunes, Tiberius Gracchus and his followers took control of the Capitoline Hill, the site of Rome's chief temple, where the voting was being held.

Meanwhile the Senate met. Tiberius's own cousin, Scipio Nasica, was chief priest (*pontifex maximus*), head of Rome's state religion. He demanded that the consul use force to stop the re-election. When the consul refused, Scipio Nasica shouted the old formula for raising troops in an emergency: "Let those who want the state to be safe follow me." Drawing his toga over his head, as a priest did at a sacrifice, he led a mob to the voting place, framing the attack as a religious rite to rid Rome of a would-be tyrant.

Tiberius and his supporters did not fight back. They were beaten to death with stones, wooden chairs and other blunt objects, and their bodies were thrown into the Tiber, the river that runs through Rome.

Nothing like it had happened before: Roman political quarrels had almost never ended in killing, and even men facing death sentences were allowed to flee into exile. Afterwards Scipio Nasica was charged with murder, but was sent on a convenient mission to Pergamum, where he died the next year.

## The Land Commission and the Italian Allies {#land-commission}

Tiberius Gracchus was dead, but his land law survived. The commission it created kept working, staffed by his allies, including his younger brother Gaius Gracchus.

Its progress can still be traced through boundary stones (*cippi*) carved with the commissioners' names, found all over southern Italy. Over a few busy years it distributed some 3,268 square kilometres (1.3 million jugera) of land, enough for somewhere between 70,000 and 130,000 settlers. The number of registered citizens rose in the following decade, perhaps because registering brought a chance of land.

After 129 BC the work slowed. Rome's Italian allies, the peoples of Italy who fought alongside Rome but lacked citizenship, protested that the commissioners were seizing land they had long used. They saw that use as their reward for loyalty. Some complainers were rich men holding too much, but the work was also rushed and inaccurate, and replacement land could be poorer.

The brothers' brother-in-law, the celebrated general, championed the Italians and had disputed cases moved to the consuls, who did nothing. He soon died mysteriously; rumours blamed the Gracchan family, but modern historians reject them. Italian anger over the land helped lead to the Social War, a later revolt of the allies against Rome.

## Gaius Gracchus: The Younger Brother {#gaius-gracchus-rises}

Gaius Gracchus, born around 154 BC, was about nine years younger than his murdered brother Tiberius. He served in the army in Spain in 133 BC and sat on the land commission his brother had created.

In 126 BC he went as quaestor, a junior finance officer, to Sardinia, the large island west of Italy. During a harsh winter he managed to obtain supplies from the islanders for the struggling Roman troops. But when his commander's posting was extended, Gaius simply went home without permission. Questioned by the censors, he argued he had already served longer than most men. He was later accused of helping a revolt in 125 BC at Fregellae, a Latin town south-east of Rome, but cleared himself and was elected tribune for 123 BC.

Gaius was a dazzling speaker, ranked among the greatest Roman orators of his age. He even changed how speeches were given in the Forum: instead of facing the senate house, he turned towards the crowd, in effect turning his back on the Senate.

Gaius presented himself as his brother's heir, and ancient writers followed him. Modern scholars debate how far the two brothers really shared a single programme.

## The Laws of Gaius Gracchus {#laws-of-gaius}

As tribune in 123 and 122 BC, Gaius Gracchus passed a sweeping set of laws designed to win over many groups at once.

One reaffirmed that a citizen facing a death sentence could appeal to the people, and declared courts not set up by the people illegal. It drove into exile a former consul linked to the punishment of Tiberius Gracchus's supporters.

His grain law set a fixed, slightly below-harvest price for each measure of about 8.7 litres (one modius) of grain. The state would buy grain when it was cheap and store it in public granaries. He also renewed his brother's land law and distributed land in Apulia, the south-eastern region of Italy.

Gaius saw that Italy did not have enough land for everyone, so he planned colonies (*coloniae*), new settlements of citizens, including one overseas at Carthage. He also courted the equestrians (*equites*), wealthy men outside the Senate. They received tax-collecting contracts in the province of Asia and seats on the juries that tried corrupt officials.

He was no democrat, but a proud aristocrat who left the Senate in charge under tighter checks. One result was unintended: the equestrian tax companies became a new class exploiting the provinces.

## Citizenship, Defeat and Death on the Aventine {#fall-of-gaius}

In 122 BC Gaius Gracchus, serving a second year as tribune, made his boldest proposal: citizenship for the Latins, Rome's closest allies, and new rights for other Italians. A rival tribune vetoed it and, reportedly at the Senate's prompting, outbid Gaius with grand schemes of his own. A consul who had once been Gaius's ally warned voters that newcomers would "swamp everything". The bills failed, and Gaius lost his bid for a third term.

In 121 BC a new tribune moved to cancel the colony at Carthage. During a sacrifice, an attendant jeered at the Gracchans and was stabbed to death with writing styluses. The consul Opimius, a determined enemy of the reformers, called the Senate together. It passed its first "final decree" (*senatus consultum ultimum*), urging him to defend the state by any means necessary.

Gaius and his close ally armed their followers and seized the temple of Diana on the Aventine Hill, in the south of Rome. Opimius marched on them with armed citizens and Cretan archers, offering rewards for the leaders' heads. Gaius's ally and his sons were killed. Gaius fled across the Tiber, where he was either killed or took his own life.

## Aftermath: A Republic Changed {#turning-point}

After Gaius Gracchus died in 121 BC, the consul Opimius, who had led the attack, set up a court to try the reformers' supporters. Reportedly thousands died, many executed after brief investigations without trial.

Opimius ritually purified the city and built a grand temple to the goddess Concord (*Concordia*), symbol of civic harmony, beside the senate house. Not everyone was convinced. One night someone carved an inscription: "A work of mad discord produces a temple of Concord." Prosecuted under Gaius's own law against executing citizens without appeal, Opimius won by pointing to the Senate's emergency decree, giving such decrees new legitimacy.

Oddly, most of Gaius's laws survived. The grain law lasted until the last decade of the century, and the land commission was closed only in 111 BC, once its work was done.

Both ancient and modern writers see 133 BC, the year of Tiberius Gracchus's murder, as the start of the Republic's decline. A famous Roman orator later wrote that Tiberius had divided one people into two factions. Modern historians warn that the idea of calm agreement before 133 BC is a nostalgic fiction. Still, the killings set a lasting precedent: violence now looked like an alternative to negotiation and compromise.
