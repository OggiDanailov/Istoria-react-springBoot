# Middle Republic: Scipio and the Road to Zama (216-201 BCE)

## A Survivor of Cannae {#survivor-of-cannae}

In 218 BC Rome went to war with Carthage (*Carthago*), the great trading city on the coast of modern Tunisia. The Carthaginian general Hannibal marched an army from Spain, over the Alps and into Italy. This was the start of the Second Punic War. Among the Romans who rode out to stop him was a teenager named Scipio, son of one of that year's consuls (*consules*), the two magistrates elected each year to lead Rome's armies.

According to one ancient account, enemy horsemen surrounded Scipio's father in a cavalry clash on the river Ticinus in northern Italy, and young Scipio saved his life. Other writers gave the credit to an unnamed slave.

In 216 BC Scipio served as a military tribune (*tribunus militum*), a junior officer, at Cannae in southern Italy. There at least 67,500 Romans were killed or captured, among them Scipio's future father-in-law, a consul. Scipio survived and rallied the stragglers. A later story claims he burst in on young nobles who were plotting to flee abroad and forced them at sword-point to swear loyalty to Rome. Modern historians suspect it was invented. In 213 BC voters elected him aedile (*aedilis*), an official who looked after the city's streets and festivals. A tribune objected that he was too young, but the crowd's enthusiasm made the tribune back down.

## An Unprecedented Command in Spain {#command-in-spain}

For seven years Scipio's father and uncle led Rome's armies in Spain, pushing deep into Carthaginian territory there. Then in 211 BC the brothers split their forces to attack three enemy armies at once. Each was beaten separately, and both men were killed. Two of the victorious Carthaginian commanders were Hannibal's own brothers.

In 210 BC the people's assembly gave the Spanish command to the younger Scipio, then about 25. It was an extraordinary choice. Normally only a man who had already held one of Rome's senior offices could lead an army in his own right. Scipio had held none of them, yet he received the full military authority of a consul (*imperium pro consule*). He was the first Roman ever given such power without first serving as consul.

A Roman historian writing two centuries later described the Senate (*senatus*), Rome's council of elder statesmen, as dithering over the appointment. Modern scholars suspect instead that the senators picked Scipio themselves, then had the people vote on it to make an irregular command look legitimate. Scipio arrived in Spain in early autumn with about 10,000 reinforcements.

## The Storming of New Carthage {#new-carthage}

Scipio's first great stroke came in 209 BC. Rather than chase the three Carthaginian armies, he struck at New Carthage, modern Cartagena on Spain's south-east coast. It was the enemy's main supply base. A lagoon guarded the city's northern side, but at low tide it could be waded. Scipio sent a party across while the walls were under attack. He told his troops that the sea god Neptune had promised him help in a vision. The story fed a growing legend that Scipio was a favourite of the gods.

The capture was brutal. Scipio ordered his men to kill everyone they met. Polybius, a Greek historian of the period, thought the aim was to terrify the region into surrender, and claimed the soldiers even cut dogs in half. Once the garrison in the citadel gave up, Scipio changed course. Citizens were spared, and only non-citizens were enslaved.

He treated the 300 Spanish hostages held in the city generously, promising them freedom if their tribes sided with Rome, and several tribes soon did. A much later writer added a famous tale. In it, Scipio returned a beautiful captive untouched to her Spanish fiancé. Renaissance painters loved the scene and called it the "Continence of Scipio".

## Baecula, Ilipa and the Conquest of Spain {#conquest-of-spain}

In 208 BC Scipio fought one of Hannibal's brothers near Baecula in southern Spain. Scipio won the battle but not the campaign. His opponent slipped away with most of his army, crossed the Pyrenees and headed for Italy to join Hannibal.

The decisive blow fell in 206 BC at Ilipa, north of modern Seville. There Scipio routed the remaining Carthaginian forces and drove them from the peninsula. The mopping-up was harsh. One town that had killed Roman refugees was severely punished, and the people of another killed themselves rather than surrender. Soon Gades, modern Cádiz, Carthage's last stronghold in Spain, gave up too. Around this time Scipio also founded Italica, about 9 km northwest of Seville, which later became the birthplace of three Roman emperors.

Scipio was already thinking about Africa. He courted two rulers of Numidia, a kingdom of horsemen west of Carthage. One, an established king, promised friendship but later sided with Carthage. The other was Masinissa, a young prince who had fought for Carthage in Spain. When his rival drove him from his kingdom, Masinissa joined Rome and became its most valuable ally.

## The Metaurus: Hannibal's Brother Falls {#battle-of-the-metaurus}

While Scipio was winning Spain, the brother of Hannibal who had escaped him crossed the Alps in spring 207 BC. His army had been swollen by Gaulish recruits, and he brought war elephants. If the two brothers joined forces, Rome faced catastrophe. Then the Romans captured his messengers and learned of his plan to meet Hannibal in central Italy.

The letters reached Claudius Nero, the consul who was shadowing Hannibal in the far south. Without waiting for the Senate's approval, Claudius Nero took 7,000 picked men and marched north in secret, hundreds of kilometres, gathering volunteers and supplies as he went. He joined his fellow consul by night. Next morning the Carthaginian commander noticed the Roman army had grown. He remembered hearing a second trumpet, the signal that a second commander had arrived, and he retreated in the dark. His guides deserted him, and dawn found his weary army trapped beside the Metaurus, a river in north-east Italy.

In the battle, Claudius Nero led half his men behind the Roman lines and smashed into the enemy flank. Seeing all was lost, Hannibal's brother charged into the Roman ranks and died. About 15,400 of his men were killed or captured. Claudius Nero had the dead man's head thrown into Hannibal's camp. Hannibal soon withdrew to Bruttium, the "toe" of Italy.

## Consul at Thirty-One {#consul-at-thirty-one}

Scipio came home from Spain in 206 BC and was elected consul for 205 BC amid wild enthusiasm. He was 31, while the normal minimum age for the office was 42. He wanted to carry the war to Africa, and many senators thought this reckless. Hannibal was still in Italy. His youngest brother would soon land in northern Italy with another army. And Rome's last invasion of Africa, during the First Punic War, had ended in disaster.

The leader of the Senate (*princeps senatus*), an elderly general famous for his cautious strategy, fought Scipio's plan hard. He may have been partly driven by jealousy of the young man's popularity.

A compromise followed. Scipio received Sicily as his province, with permission to cross to Africa if he judged it best. But he could not call up soldiers in the usual way, only ask for volunteers. The core of his force was two legions (*legiones*) made up of survivors of Cannae who had been posted to Sicily. Scipio drilled his army for about a year, from units of 80 men up to the whole force. Meanwhile he gathered food, transport ships and warships to escort them. Modern historians estimate the army at 25,000 to 30,000 men.

## Fire in the Night: The Invasion of Africa {#invasion-of-africa}

In summer 204 BC Scipio's fleet landed about 20 km (12 miles) north of Utica, a major Carthaginian port. Masinissa, the Numidian prince allied to Rome, joined him. Scipio besieged Utica, but it held out, and two enemy armies camped nearby. One was Carthaginian, the other led by Masinissa's Numidian rival.

Over the winter Scipio held peace talks. His envoys used each visit to study the enemy camps, their layout, their exits and their huts of reeds. One evening in 203 BC he marched out, split his army, and set the Numidian camp ablaze. In the chaos his men also fell on the Carthaginian camp, and thousands died in the dark.

Carthage raised a new army, but at a place called the Great Plains its shaken troops broke at the first charge. Masinissa and Scipio's second-in-command then hunted down the rival Numidian king and captured him. Masinissa took over much of his kingdom.

Carthage opened talks and recalled Hannibal from Italy. He landed with 15,000 to 20,000 veterans but few horses. His arrival restored Carthage's nerve, and it rejected a draft treaty that Rome's Senate had already approved.

## The Battle of Zama, 202 BC {#battle-of-zama}

In 202 BC, near Zama in what is now Tunisia, Scipio and Hannibal met to parley. Scipio refused Hannibal's terms, and battle followed. Hannibal probably had about 40,000 men, including only 4,000 cavalry, plus 80 war elephants. Scipio had around 29,000 to 30,000 men but more than 6,000 cavalry, many of them brought by Masinissa, the Numidian king allied to Rome.

Scipio made an unusual arrangement. Normally his infantry companies, the maniples (*manipuli*), stood staggered like a chessboard. Instead he lined them up one behind another, leaving open lanes filled with light skirmishers (*velites*). When the elephants charged, the Romans blared trumpets and banged their shields. Some elephants panicked and fled back through their own cavalry. Most ran down the lanes, where they were cut off and killed. The Roman horsemen on both wings seized the moment and chased the Carthaginian cavalry off the field.

The infantry fought it out line by line. The Romans broke Hannibal's first two lines in savage fighting, but his third line, his veterans from Italy, waited fresh. After a pause, the two sides clashed again "with the greatest fire and fury", as Polybius put it. Neither gave way until the Roman cavalry returned and struck the Carthaginian rear. Polybius counted 20,000 Carthaginians killed and as many captured, against 1,500 Roman dead. Hannibal escaped.

## Peace, Triumph and a New Name {#peace-and-africanus}

After Zama, Hannibal had only about 6,500 men left. He told Carthage's leaders to make peace on whatever terms they could get. Rome's terms, accepted in spring 201 BC, were harsh. Carthage lost its lands outside Africa and some within it. It had to pay 10,000 silver talents over 50 years, gave up its war elephants and kept only ten warships. It could not wage war outside Africa at all, nor inside Africa without Rome's permission. Masinissa's Numidian kingdom was confirmed. Carthage was now plainly subordinate to Rome, and in 146 BC Rome destroyed the city altogether.

Scipio returned home to celebrate a triumph (*triumphus*), the grand victory parade through Rome. He took the honorary surname (*agnomen*) Africanus, "the African". He deposited about 40 tonnes (123,000 Roman pounds) of silver in the treasury and gave each of his soldiers a cash bonus.

His popularity with ordinary Romans was enormous, and it made many aristocrats jealous and wary. Yet when admirers urged him to become consul for life and dictator, he refused. Later Roman writers praised this restraint, which stands in sharp contrast with the generals who seized power in the Republic's final century.

## How Do We Know? Sources and the Scipio Legend {#sources-and-legend}

Scipio wrote memoirs in Greek, but they are lost, along with a history by his son and a biography by a later Greek author. What survives comes mainly from Polybius, a Greek historian born around 200 BC who was sent to Rome as a hostage in 167 BC. He interviewed participants on both sides and is generally considered reliable. But he was a close friend of Scipio's relatives, and his main informant about Scipio was Scipio's own friend and second-in-command, so his picture may be flattering.

A Roman historian writing two centuries later relied heavily on Polybius but is trusted less. Modern scholars note his weak grasp of warfare and call his account of Zama bizarrely at odds with Polybius. Other later writers are thought less reliable still.

A legend grew around Scipio in his own lifetime. Tales spread of visions from the gods and dreams that foretold the future, and of Spaniards hailing him as king and god. Later versions even made him a son of Jupiter, Rome's chief god. Polybius disagreed, arguing that Scipio succeeded through careful planning and clear thinking. A famous story has Hannibal later ranking the greatest generals in conversation with Scipio, but historians consider the meeting apocryphal.
