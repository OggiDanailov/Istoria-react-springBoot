# Middle Republic: The Fall of Carthage and Corinth (167-133 BCE)

## Carthage Under Rome's Thumb {#carthage-under-rome}

By the middle of the 2nd century BC, Rome was the dominant power in the Mediterranean. Its great rival, Carthage (*Carthago*), a wealthy trading city in what is now northern Tunisia, had lost two long wars to Rome. These were the First Punic War (264–241 BC) and the Second (218–201 BC).

The peace of 201 BC was harsh. Carthage lost its overseas lands and some of its African ones. It had to pay 10,000 silver talents over 50 years. It was also forbidden to wage war at all without Rome's express permission.

That last clause proved poisonous. The king of the Numidians, the native people who controlled much of modern Algeria and Tunisia, was Rome's ally. For half a century he raided and seized Carthaginian land, and every time Carthage appealed to Rome, Rome backed him.

In 151 BC Carthage finally struck back. It raised a large army under Hasdrubal, a general previously unknown to history. The campaign ended in disaster at the Battle of Oroscopa: the army surrendered, and many soldiers were massacred by the Numidians. Hasdrubal escaped home, where his own city condemned him to death in a bid to placate Rome. It was too late. Rome now had its pretext for war.

## "Carthage Must Be Destroyed" {#carthago-delenda-est}

By 151 BC Carthage had paid off its war debt and was prospering, but it posed no military threat to Rome. Even so, a faction in the Senate (*senatus*), Rome's governing council of senior statesmen, had long wanted to finish it off.

Its most famous voice was Cato, the elderly senator whose hatred of Carthage became legendary. Around 153 BC he visited the city as an envoy and was alarmed by its growing wealth and strength. Since the 18th century he has been credited with ending every speech, whatever its subject, with the words "Carthage must be destroyed" (*Carthago delenda est*). The phrase is a later tradition rather than a quotation recorded by ancient writers.

Not every senator agreed. A leading opponent argued that fear of a strong enemy kept the common people in check and Rome united.

Why did Rome want war so badly? Modern historians have offered many answers. Perhaps Romans feared Carthaginian trade. Perhaps they wanted to head off chaos once the Numidian king, then 89, died. Perhaps Carthage was simply a useful political bogeyman, or a tempting source of glory and loot. No consensus exists.

In 149 BC Utica, a large port 55 km (34 miles) north of Carthage, defected to Rome. With a harbour now available, Rome declared war.

## The Ultimatum of 149 BC {#the-ultimatum}

In 149 BC a large Roman army landed at Utica, a port just north of Carthage, the great North African trading city Rome now meant to crush. It was led by both of that year's consuls, the two chief magistrates Rome elected annually to command its armies. One ancient account puts the force at 84,000 men; modern historians estimate 40,000 to 50,000.

Carthage still hoped to buy peace. Its envoys were told to hand over every weapon in the city, and they reluctantly obeyed. Convoys carried away, according to surviving records, 200,000 sets of armour and 2,000 catapults. Carthage's warships sailed to Utica and were burned in the harbour.

Then came the real demand. The Carthaginians must abandon their city and resettle 16 km (10 miles) from the sea, and Carthage itself would be destroyed. For a people who lived by sea trade, this was a death sentence. They broke off talks and prepared to fight.

They had much to fight with. Carthage was huge, with population estimates from 90,000 to 800,000. Its walls ran more than 35 km (20 miles). On the landward side the main wall was 9 metres (30 feet) thick and up to 20 metres (70 feet) high, with barracks for 24,000 soldiers built inside it. Slaves willing to fight were freed, and Hasdrubal, the general recently condemned to death, was released to lead a field army.

## Two Years of Roman Blunders {#a-faltering-siege}

The Roman war on Carthage, launched in 149 BC, began badly. The Roman army tried to scale the city's walls, failed, tried again and was driven back. It settled down to a siege, while Hasdrubal, the Carthaginian general leading an army of at least 20,000 from a fortified base at Nepheris, 25 km (16 miles) south of the city, harassed Roman supply lines.

One officer kept saving the day. Scipio Aemilianus, adopted grandson of the general who had won the Second Punic War, was serving as a military tribune (*tribunus militum*), a middle-ranking post. When a failed assault turned into a rout, he held his men back and used them to beat off the pursuers.

The Romans' troubles mounted. A badly sited camp became so unhealthy it had to be moved, and Carthaginian fireships damaged the Roman fleet. A rash attack on Hasdrubal's camp at Nepheris, launched against Scipio's advice, ended in heavy losses. Scipio led 300 cavalry in disciplined charges that covered the retreat, then rode back at night to rescue trapped men. Visiting senators took note.

In 148 BC a new commander did no better, failing to take several towns loyal to Carthage. Inside the city, Hasdrubal overthrew the civilian leaders and took command himself. Carthage even allied with a pretender who had seized the throne of Macedonia, the kingdom north of Greece, and was fighting Rome there.

## Scipio Takes Command {#scipio-takes-command}

By 147 BC Romans were tired of failure at Carthage and wanted Scipio Aemilianus, the young officer whose courage had shone through the siege. He planned to stand for aedile (*aedilis*), a junior office overseeing the city of Rome. At 36 or 37 he was too young to be consul: a law on minimum ages (*lex Villia annalis*) set the bar at 41. But public demand was so strong, helped by his famous family name, that the Senate suspended the age rules for the year. Scipio was elected consul and given sole command in Africa.

He dismissed slack soldiers, tightened discipline and led a night attack that broke into the city with 4,000 men before withdrawing. Hasdrubal, the general now ruling Carthage, answered by torturing Roman prisoners to death on the walls in full view of their comrades. From then on surrender was unthinkable. Councillors who protested were executed too.

Supply ships kept slipping into Carthage, so Scipio built a huge mole (*moles*), a stone barrier across the harbour approach. The Carthaginians secretly dug a new channel to the sea and sailed out with a newly built fleet. They held their own, but as they withdrew many ships were trapped against the sea wall and lost.

The Romans then built a brick platform as high as the city wall, letting 4,000 men shoot down onto the defenders. Late in 147 BC Scipio stormed the Carthaginian field camp at Nepheris, and most towns still loyal to Carthage opened their gates.

## Six Days of Destruction {#fall-of-carthage}

In the spring of 146 BC Scipio Aemilianus, the Roman consul besieging Carthage, launched his final assault from the harbour and broke through the walls. What followed was one of the grimmest episodes in ancient warfare.

For six days the Romans fought street by street through the residential districts, killing everyone they met and burning the buildings behind them. Only on the last day did Scipio agree to take prisoners. About 900 Roman deserters who had fought for Carthage knew they could expect no mercy. They held out in the Temple of Eshmoun, a Carthaginian god of healing, and finally burned it down around themselves.

At that moment Hasdrubal, the general who had ruled Carthage during its last stand, surrendered to Scipio in exchange for his life and freedom. His wife, watching from a rampart, blessed Scipio, cursed her husband for his cowardice, and walked into the burning temple with their children.

Around 50,000 survivors were sold into slavery. Sacred statues that Carthage had looted from Sicilian cities over the centuries were ceremonially returned.

One famous detail is almost certainly false. The story that the Romans sowed the ground with salt so nothing would grow is probably a 19th-century invention.

## Carthage After the War {#after-carthage}

After Carthage fell in 146 BC, Rome was determined the great North African city would stay in ruins. A ten-man commission arrived from the Senate, more demolition was ordered, and a curse was laid on anyone who tried to resettle the site. The land was confiscated as public land (*ager publicus*), the property of the Roman state.

Scipio Aemilianus, the general who had taken the city, celebrated a triumph (*triumphus*), Rome's victory parade. He also took the honorary surname (*agnomen*) "Africanus", just as his adoptive grandfather had done. Hasdrubal, the defeated Carthaginian leader, vanishes from history, though he had been promised retirement on an Italian estate.

Carthage's lands became the Roman province (*provincia*) of Africa, governed from Utica, a nearby port that had sided with Rome. It became a major source of grain. Local towns kept much of their own government and culture, and the Punic language of Carthage was still spoken in North Africa until the 7th century AD.

In 123 BC Roman reformers voted to found a new colony on the site. Opponents spread rumours that wolves had dug up its boundary markers, a terrible omen, and the plan collapsed. Only in 29 BC did the man who became Rome's first emperor complete a new Roman Carthage, which grew into a leading city of the empire. In 1985 the mayors of Rome and modern Carthage signed a symbolic peace treaty.

## The Sack of Corinth {#sack-of-corinth}

In the same year that Carthage fell, 146 BC, Rome destroyed another famous city: Corinth, a rich Greek port on the narrow isthmus joining southern Greece to the mainland.

Corinth belonged to the Achaean League, an alliance of city-states in the Peloponnese, the peninsula of southern Greece. The League had been Rome's ally for half a century, but relations soured. Rome had taken thousands of Achaean hostages during its war with Macedonia, and it kept blocking the League's ambitions, above all its wish to absorb Sparta. In 148 BC the League conquered Sparta anyway. The next year a Roman embassy announced that the League must shrink back to its original core; the envoys were nearly mobbed. War followed.

The Greeks knew they were fighting a hopeless war. One Roman army, fresh from crushing the Macedonian revolt, beat the League's main force at Scarpheia. Then Mummius, the consul, arrived with 23,000 infantry and 3,500 cavalry and routed a much smaller Greek army outside Corinth.

Most Corinthians fled. Those who remained suffered terribly: the men were massacred, the women and children enslaved, and the city destroyed. Mummius was famously ignorant of art. Shipping priceless statues and paintings to Italy, he warned the contractors that if they lost any, they would have to supply new ones.

Mummius took the honorary surname "Achaicus". Greek independence was over, and the Greek states came under the Roman province of Macedonia.

## Numantia: The Last Stand in Spain {#numantine-war}

While Rome was destroying Carthage and Corinth, it was also fighting a long, bloody war in Spain (*Hispania*). Its enemy was the Celtiberians, Celtic-speaking tribes of the Spanish interior, and above all the hilltop town of Numantia on the Douro river.

Numantia revolted in 154 BC. Fighting died down in 151 BC but flared again in 143 BC. Rome then sent a string of commanders, and one after another they failed. One general, badly beaten, secretly made peace and later denied it. Another was surrounded and forced to sign a treaty, negotiated by a young officer whose family the Numantines trusted. The Senate refused to honour either deal, and simply handed the beaten general over to the Numantines as a prisoner.

In 134 BC Rome turned to Scipio Aemilianus, the consul who had destroyed Carthage. He raised 20,000 men and 40,000 allies, including Numidian cavalry and 12 war elephants led by a Numidian prince who would later become a dangerous enemy of Rome.

Instead of attacking, Scipio built a ring of seven forts around Numantia and starved it out. After disease and famine, most surviving Numantines killed themselves rather than surrender. Numantia fell in 133 BC, and Spain stayed largely at peace for more than half a century.

## How Do We Know? Polybius and the Sources {#polybius-and-the-sources}

Our main guide to Rome's wars with Carthage is Polybius, a Greek historian who lived from about 200 to about 118 BC. He came to Rome in 167 BC as one of the many hostages taken from the Achaean League, the alliance of southern Greek cities that Rome distrusted.

In Rome he became a friend of Scipio Aemilianus, the general who would later destroy Carthage, and he was with Scipio in Africa during the final war. That gave him an eyewitness view, but it also coloured his writing. Normally a careful and reliable historian, Polybius tends to show his patron in a flattering light. Worse, large parts of his account of the Third Punic War are lost.

Other ancient histories survive only in fragments. The great Roman historian Livy, who relied heavily on Polybius, is known for this period only through a list of contents. The one complete narrative of the war was written by a Greek author in the 2nd century AD, three centuries later. It seems to draw mainly on Polybius, but scholars have found several problems with it.

As a result, the Third Punic War is the least reliably known of Rome's three wars with Carthage. Historians fill the gaps with coins, inscriptions, archaeology and practical experiments. Careful study has also exposed some famous stories, like Romans salting the ground at Carthage, as much later inventions.
