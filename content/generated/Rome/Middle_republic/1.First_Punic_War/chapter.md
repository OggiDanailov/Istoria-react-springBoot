# Middle Republic: The First Punic War (264-241 BCE)

## Two Powers of the Western Sea {#rome-and-carthage}

In the early 3rd century BC, two powers dominated the western Mediterranean. Rome had spent a century conquering its neighbours. By 272 BC it controlled the Italian peninsula south of the River Arno, which flows past Pisa.

Across the sea stood Carthage (*Carthago*), a great trading city in what is now Tunisia. Its empire stretched along the North African coast and into southern Spain. It also held the islands of Sardinia and Corsica and the western half of Sicily.

The two cities had long been friends. They signed treaties in 509 BC, 348 BC and around 279 BC, and traded busily. When a Greek king from Epirus, in north-western Greece, fought them both in the 270s BC, Carthage supplied Rome and once ferried Roman troops in its ships.

The war that ended this friendship lasted 23 years, from 264 to 241 BC. It was the longest continuous conflict and the greatest naval war of the ancient world. Romans called the Carthaginians *Punici*, meaning Phoenicians, after their ancestors. That is why it is known as the First Punic War.

## Messana: The Spark of War {#messana}

The war began over Messana, today's Messina, a city on Sicily's north-eastern tip facing Italy. In 289 BC Italian mercenaries called the Mamertines had seized it. By 265 BC they were hard-pressed by Syracuse, the strongest Greek city on Sicily, and begged both Rome and Carthage for help. Carthage acted first and put a garrison in the city.

According to Polybius, the Greek historian who is our main source, Rome debated long and hard. Helping meant risking war with Carthage, and many Romans disliked aiding soldiers who had stolen a city. Others saw the gains of a foothold on Sicily. The Senate (*senatus*) was deadlocked, so the question went to the popular assembly (*comitia*). One of the two consuls (*consules*), the magistrates elected each year to lead Rome's armies, urged war and promised rich plunder (*praeda*). The people voted yes.

In 264 BC two legions (*legiones*), Roman army units of about 4,500 men each, crossed the strait. The Mamertines had already expelled the Carthaginians. In 263 BC, facing 40,000 Romans, the king of Syracuse switched sides and agreed to feed Rome's army.

## The Siege of Akragas {#siege-of-akragas}

Carthage made Akragas, a port halfway along Sicily's south coast that the Romans called Agrigentum, its main base on the island. In 262 BC a Roman army of about 40,000 besieged it. Feeding so many men was hard while Carthage ruled the sea, and at harvest time the troops scattered to gather crops. The garrison seized the moment, burst out and broke into the Roman camp before being driven back.

Carthage, which relied mostly on hired foreign soldiers, shipped over a relief army of 50,000 infantry, 6,000 cavalry and 60 war elephants. Its general camped on high ground for two months, then attacked in spring 261 BC. He was heavily defeated in one of only two full-scale pitched battles fought on Sicily in the whole war. The garrison slipped away that night, and the Romans sold 25,000 inhabitants into slavery.

Yet victory brought no breakthrough. Sicily's rugged hills favoured defenders. Carthage's fortified coastal towns could be supplied by sea, and years of raids and sieges followed.

## Rome Takes to the Sea {#rome-builds-a-fleet}

The Romans saw they could not win while Carthage ruled the waves. Yet Rome had almost no navy, having always borrowed small squadrons from its allies (*socii*). In 260 BC it began building a fleet, copying a shipwrecked Carthaginian warship.

That ship was a quinquereme, meaning "five-oared", the standard warship of the age. It was a galley about 45 m (150 ft) long, rowed by 280 oarsmen. It also carried 20 deck crew and usually 40 marines, rising to 120 before battle. At the waterline sat a bronze ram (*rostrum*) weighing up to 270 kg (600 lb), used for smashing enemy hulls. Rowing in unison, let alone manoeuvring in battle, demanded long training.

Rome built 120 warships, but its novice shipwrights produced copies that were heavier and slower than the originals. The first test was a fiasco. A consul sailed with 17 ships to seize the Lipari Islands, off Sicily's north-east coast. Carthaginian ships trapped them in harbour at night. The raw crews panicked, and every ship was captured, along with the consul himself.

## The Corvus and the Victory at Mylae {#corvus-and-mylae}

To make up for poor seamanship, the Romans fitted their ships with a boarding bridge nicknamed the raven (*corvus*). It was about 1.2 m (4 ft) wide and 11 m (36 ft) long, raised by pulleys on a pole at the prow, and it could swing from side to side. Under its far end hung a heavy iron spike shaped like a bird's beak. Dropped onto an enemy deck, it pinned the two ships together, and Roman soldiers charged across. A sea battle became a land battle, the kind Rome's legions excelled at.

Its great test came in 260 BC off Mylae, on Sicily's north coast. Gaius Duilius, the consul who gave Rome its first naval victory, faced about 130 Carthaginian ships. Sure of their skill, the Carthaginians rushed in without keeping formation. The ravens grappled the first 30 ships, including the flagship, whose commander escaped in a small boat. When the rest swung round to strike from the side, Duilius's men captured 20 more. The faster survivors fled, and Rome grew bolder.

## Regulus Invades Africa {#regulus-in-africa}

Encouraged, Rome decided to strike Carthage at home. In 256 BC a fleet of 330 warships sailed under that year's consuls. One was Regulus, the general who would carry the war to Africa and end up a prisoner. Carthage met them with 350 warships off Cape Ecnomus, on Sicily's south coast. With some 680 ships and up to 290,000 crew and marines, it was possibly the largest naval battle in history by number of combatants. After a long, confused day, Carthage had lost 94 ships sunk or captured, Rome 24.

Regulus landed near Cape Bon, in north-eastern Tunisia. Most of the fleet then sailed back to Sicily, leaving him 15,000 infantry and 500 cavalry. He routed a Carthaginian army in a surprise dawn attack and took Tunis, just 16 km (10 miles) from Carthage. Carthage asked for peace, but his terms were so harsh that it fought on.

Carthage hired a Spartan mercenary commander to retrain its army. In 255 BC the retrained army crushed the Romans, and Regulus was captured. A rescue fleet beat Carthage's navy, but a storm on the way home sank 384 of 464 ships, drowning about 100,000 men.

## Storms, Elephants and Defeat at Drepana {#drepana-and-stalemate}

Rome rebuilt fast, adding 220 ships, and in 254 BC stormed Panormus, today's Palermo, on Sicily's north coast. Another storm sank 150 Roman ships in 253 BC, and again Rome rebuilt. In 251 BC a Carthaginian army marched on Panormus with war elephants. The Roman commander showered the beasts with javelins from behind old siege earthworks. Panicked, they stampeded back through their own infantry, and the Carthaginians fled.

By 249 BC Carthage held only Lilybaeum and Drepana, two strongholds on Sicily's western tip. Rome besieged Lilybaeum by land and sea, but swift Carthaginian blockade runners kept slipping through. A Roman consul then tried a night surprise attack on the enemy fleet at Drepana. His ships scattered in the dark, were pinned against the shore and were badly beaten. It was Carthage's greatest naval victory of the war. A second defeat off Phintias, on Sicily's south coast, all but swept Rome from the sea for seven years.

In 247 BC Hamilcar Barca, the Carthaginian general who kept up the fight in western Sicily, took command. From the mountain of Eryx, near Drepana, he waged a guerrilla war that tied down the Roman legions.

## The Battle of the Aegates Islands {#battle-of-the-aegates}

By 243 BC both sides were exhausted. Egypt refused Carthage a loan, Rome was nearly bankrupt, and its number of adult male citizens had fallen by 17 percent. So the Senate asked the richest Romans each to fund a warship, to be repaid from Carthage's future war payments. About 200 quinqueremes were built at no cost to the state, modelled on a fast captured blockade runner. This time there was no corvus; Romans would have to win as sailors.

Catulus, the consul who won the war's final battle, brought the fleet to Sicily in 242 BC. He sealed off the last Carthaginian strongholds and drilled his crews relentlessly. Carthage took nine months to gather 250 warships, short of trained rowers and loaded with grain for its starving garrisons.

On 10 March 241 BC, near the Aegates Islands just west of Sicily, the Romans left masts and sails ashore and rowed into a strong headwind to intercept. Catulus was wounded, so his deputy led the fight. Nimble and well drilled, the Romans rammed their laden opponents, sinking 50 ships and capturing 70, for a loss of 30.

## Peace and Its Bitter Aftermath {#peace-and-aftermath}

Rather than build another fleet, Carthage ordered Hamilcar Barca, its commander in Sicily, to make peace. Under the Treaty of Lutatius in 241 BC, Carthage left Sicily, freed all prisoners and agreed to pay 3,200 talents of silver over ten years. Catulus, the consul whose fleet won the war, celebrated a triumph (*triumphus*), a victory parade through Rome.

Sicily became Rome's first province (*provincia*), an overseas territory run by a Roman governor, and a vital source of grain. Rome had built over 1,000 galleys during the war, laying the foundation for 600 years of mastery at sea.

Carthage's troubles continued. It tried to underpay its foreign soldiers, who rebelled and were crushed with great savagery. When Carthage moved to recover Sardinia in 237 BC, Rome cynically called it an act of war. It forced Carthage to surrender Sardinia and Corsica and pay 1,200 more talents. That year Hamilcar led his veterans to Spain to build a new power base. Resentment smouldered, and in 218 BC the Second Punic War began.

## How Do We Know? Polybius, the Corvus and the Seabed {#sources-and-debates}

Almost everything we know about this war comes from Polybius, a Greek taken to Rome as a hostage in 167 BC. He wrote about a century after the fighting, interviewing participants where he could. Carthage's own records perished when the city was destroyed in 146 BC. His accuracy has been debated for 150 years, but most modern historians accept his account largely at face value.

The corvus, Rome's spiked boarding bridge, sparks the liveliest debate. It is clearly mentioned only at Mylae and Ecnomus, then vanishes. Some think its weight made ships top-heavy and worsened the great storm disasters. A naval architect, however, calculated that a bridge of about one tonne would not seriously upset a quinquereme. In 1907 one historian argued it never existed as described. Others see it as one tool in a bolder naval strategy than Polybius admits.

Since 2010, divers off western Sicily have found more than two dozen bronze rams from the Aegates battle, plus helmets and hundreds of storage jars. Their location supports Polybius, and the jars confirm Carthage's ships carried supplies. But the rams' size suggests smaller ships than the quinqueremes he describes.
