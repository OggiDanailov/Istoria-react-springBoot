# Middle Republic: Hannibal in Italy (241-216 BCE)

## A Bitter Peace {#after-the-first-war}

In 241 BC Rome and Carthage (*Carthago*), a wealthy trading city in what is now Tunisia, ended twenty-three years of war. The First Punic War, fought mostly on and around Sicily, had been the longest continuous conflict and the greatest naval war of the ancient world. Carthage lost, and under peace terms dictated by Rome it gave up its possessions on Sicily.

Worse followed. Carthage was soon fighting for its life against its own rebellious mercenaries and African subjects, a struggle known as the Truceless War. Rome exploited the distraction. In 238 BC it broke the treaty and seized Carthaginian Sardinia and Corsica. Many Carthaginians were deeply embittered by this.

Yet Carthage was far from broken. It remained the most prosperous port of its day and paid its war debt to Rome with ease. One leading family, the Barcas, began planning how Carthage could one day stand up to Rome again.

## The Barca Family Builds a Base in Spain {#barcas-in-spain}

Hannibal's father, a Carthaginian general nicknamed Barca, meaning 'lightning', crushed the mercenary revolt in 237 BC. He drew a clear lesson: if Carthage was to face Rome again, it needed a stronger base. That same year he led his army to Iberia (*Hispania*), the peninsula that is now Spain and Portugal.

Carthage had held only a few coastal towns there. Through years of hard mountain fighting, he carved out a near-royal domain in the south and east. It gave Carthage silver mines, rich farmland, shipyards and a deep pool of recruits.

He was killed fighting local tribes around 228 BC, reportedly before the eyes of his young son Hannibal. Command passed to his son-in-law. In 226 BC Rome and Carthage agreed the Ebro Treaty, which made the Ebro, a river in north-east Spain, the northern limit of Carthage's sphere. When the son-in-law was murdered in 221 BC, the army's officers chose Hannibal, then just 26, as their commander.

## Saguntum and the Road to War {#saguntum}

Some time after the Ebro Treaty, Rome made a separate agreement with Saguntum, a city on Spain's east coast lying well south of the river. To Carthage, this looked like Roman meddling in its own sphere.

Hannibal spent his first two years in command conquering the tribes south of the Ebro, until only Saguntum stood apart. According to one ancient account, he tried to goad the Saguntines into striking first. When that failed, he besieged the city in 219 BC. It held out for eight months, yet Rome sent no help. When it fell, Hannibal sold its people as slaves and shared the loot with his soldiers and the citizens of Carthage.

Rome sent an embassy to Carthage led by Fabius Maximus, the senator later famous as Rome's most patient general. Its demands were curt and were rejected. In spring 218 BC Rome declared war.

Meanwhile, in Cisalpine Gaul (*Gallia Cisalpina*), the Po valley lands of Celtic tribes in northern Italy, Rome was founding new colonies (*coloniae*) of settlers on the best land. The Gauls seethed, and Hannibal's envoys were already courting them.

## Hannibal Crosses the Alps {#crossing-the-alps}

Rome planned to fight in Spain and Africa. Hannibal instead carried the war to Italy by land, avoiding Rome's control of the sea. In May or June 218 BC he marched north from New Carthage, his main base, now Cartagena.

At the Rhône, a hostile Gallic tribe waited on the far bank. Hannibal sent a detachment about 40 km (25 miles) upstream to cross and fall on them from behind as his main army rowed over. His elephants were coaxed onto rafts covered with earth, so they believed they were still on land. A Roman army that landed at Massalia, a Greek city now Marseille, missed him.

Hannibal reached the Alps in late autumn and crossed them in 15 days. Mountain tribes ambushed his columns and rolled boulders down on them, and pack animals plunged off cliffs. Most of his men, from Africa and Spain, had never known such cold. On the descent a landslide had broken the path for about 275 m (300 yards), and the exhausted troops had to rebuild it.

In November he entered northern Italy with about 20,000 infantry, 6,000 cavalry and the few survivors of 37 elephants.

## The Mystery of Hannibal's Pass {#which-pass}

Exactly where Hannibal crossed the Alps has been argued over since the decades after the war. Polybius, the most trusted ancient historian of the period, gives just three place names, and no weapons or camps have been found. French scholars coined a word, 'Hannibalism', for chasing a question that may have no answer.

Candidates include the Little St Bernard Pass, the Col de Clapier and the Col de la Traversette. Recently the Traversette, on the modern French-Italian border, has gained ground. Researchers studying peat bogs on both sides of its summit found soil heavily churned, they argue, by thousands of animals and people. It also held unusual levels of bacteria from the guts of horses and mules. Radiocarbon dating put this around 218 BC.

The texts fit too. Polybius wrote that Hannibal took the highest pass, and the Traversette is the highest in the western Alps. Polybius also says Hannibal cheered his men by pointing to the plains of the Po below. That view is visible from the Traversette, but not from the Little St Bernard. Sceptics still argue the evidence is too thin to be certain.

## Disasters at the Trebia and Lake Trasimene {#trebia-and-trasimene}

Hannibal struck almost at once. In late November 218 BC his cavalry routed Roman horsemen at the Ticinus, a river in the Po valley, and most Gallic tribes declared for him.

Rome rushed a second army north from Sicily, abandoning its planned invasion of Africa. In December the combined force was lured into battle at the Trebia, a tributary of the Po, on ground of Hannibal's choosing. The Carthaginians surrounded the Romans, and only about 10,000 of 40,000 fought their way out. Gauls flocked to the winner, swelling his army to some 60,000.

In spring 217 BC Hannibal crossed the Apennines, the mountain spine of Italy, by an unguarded route. He ravaged the countryside to goad the Roman commander, one of the two consuls (*consules*) elected yearly to lead Rome's armies, into a hasty pursuit. At Lake Trasimene in central Italy he sprang an ambush. Some 15,000 Romans died, the consul among them, and 10,000 were captured.

Roman prisoners were treated harshly, but Rome's Italian allies (*socii*) were freed and sent home to praise Carthage. Hannibal hoped to pull Rome's alliance apart.

## Fabius the Delayer {#fabius-the-delayer}

News of Trasimene caused panic in Rome. The voters turned to Fabius Maximus, the senator who had carried Rome's ultimatum to Carthage, and made him dictator, an emergency office giving one man supreme command for a limited term.

Fabius refused to fight the battle Hannibal wanted. He avoided pitched battles, harassed the invaders and cut their supply lines, wearing them down while Rome rebuilt its strength. This became known as the 'Fabian strategy'.

It was deeply unpopular. Hannibal marched through Italy's richest farmland, plundering as he went, to shame Fabius into battle. Fabius would not budge. Soldiers, senators and ordinary citizens mocked him as the Delayer (*Cunctator*). Many feared that Rome's unprotected allies would defect to save themselves.

When his term ended, his powers were not renewed. For 216 BC the voters elected two consuls: Varro, a man of reportedly humble origins who demanded an aggressive war, and an aristocratic colleague who favoured a middle course. Events would soon vindicate Fabius, who was elected consul in 215 and again in 214 BC.

## The Battle of Cannae {#battle-of-cannae}

In spring 216 BC Hannibal seized a large Roman supply depot at Cannae, a village on the plain of Apulia in south-east Italy. The Senate (*senatus*) answered with the biggest army Rome had ever raised: eight Roman legions (*legiones*) plus as many allies, about 86,000 men. The two consuls took turns in command day by day, and tradition says Varro held it on 2 August 216 BC, the day of battle.

The Romans packed their infantry unusually deep, hoping to smash Hannibal's centre by sheer weight. Hannibal laid a trap. He placed Gauls and Spaniards in the centre, bulging toward the enemy in a crescent, and stood among them himself. Veteran African infantry waited at either end.

On one wing his Spanish and Gallic horsemen crushed the Roman cavalry, then rode behind the Roman army to rout the allied horse on the other wing. Meanwhile Hannibal's centre gave ground step by step, as planned. The Romans crowded in so tightly they could barely swing their swords. Then the Africans turned inward on both flanks, and the cavalry struck the rear.

This 'double envelopment', or pincer movement, has been studied by generals ever since.

## Cannae's Toll and Rome's Defiance {#after-cannae}

What followed was slaughter. Polybius, the most trusted ancient historian, put losses at 70,000 infantry killed and 10,000 captured. Livy, a Roman historian writing two centuries later, gave about 48,000 dead. Varro's fellow consul died, along with about 80 senators or men eligible to be senators. Varro escaped with 70 horsemen. Hannibal lost between 5,700 and 8,000 men.

In just 20 months Rome had lost perhaps 150,000 men, a fifth of its male citizens over 17. Priests consulted the Sibylline Books (*Libri Sibyllini*), sacred prophecies kept for emergencies, and people were buried alive in Rome's cattle market as a sacrifice. Capua, Italy's second city, and the port of Tarentum went over to Hannibal.

His cavalry commander urged a march on Rome and, it is said, cried: 'You know how to win a victory, Hannibal; you do not know how to use it.' Modern historians note that his battered army was in no state to assault Rome.

Hannibal offered moderate peace terms instead. The Senate refused to talk, banned the word 'peace' and enlisted even slaves. Rome fought on for fourteen more years.

## Who Tells the Story, and Who Was to Blame? {#sources-and-blame}

Most of what we know about these years comes from two writers. Polybius was a Greek taken to Rome as a hostage in 167 BC, who wrote after 146 BC. He interviewed participants from both sides, and modern historians generally trust him. But his account survives only in fragments after 216 BC, and his patron was the grandson of the consul killed at Cannae.

Livy, writing in Rome under the emperor Augustus, relied heavily on Polybius but was openly patriotic. Modern scholars find his battle descriptions often inaccurate.

These biases shape the story of Cannae. Livy blames the low-born Varro, painted as reckless, while his aristocratic colleague appears wise and cautious. Some historians suspect Varro was a scapegoat, since his colleague's powerful family guarded its reputation. Tellingly, the Senate greeted Varro warmly after the battle, unlike other defeated generals. And the Roman commander normally stood on the right wing, where Varro's colleague fought, so no one can be sure who was in charge.

Even the casualty figures are doubted. One modern writer asks whether 50,000 Carthaginians could really kill up to 70,000 Romans in one afternoon.
