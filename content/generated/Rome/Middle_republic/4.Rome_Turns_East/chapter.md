# Middle Republic: Rome Turns East (200-167 BCE)

## Why Rome Went East {#rome-looks-east}

In 204 BC the king of Egypt died and left his throne to a six-year-old boy. Two ambitious neighbours saw their chance. Philip V, king of Macedon, the warrior kingdom on Greece's northern border, and Antiochus III, ruler of the Seleucid Empire, the vast realm governed from Syria, made a secret pact to carve up Egypt's overseas possessions.

Philip moved fast. He seized Greek cities along the Dardanelles, the narrow strait between Europe and Asia, and in Asia Minor (modern Turkey). This alarmed two smaller powers: Rhodes, a rich island trading state, and Pergamon, a kingdom in western Asia Minor. Although they were holding their own, they feared Philip enough to appeal to Rome, which had just beaten Carthage in the Second Punic War. Athens soon joined them after Macedonian troops helped raid its territory.

Philip's actions hardly touched Rome directly. In 200 BC one of the consuls asked the Centuriate Assembly (*comitia centuriata*), the citizen body that alone could declare war, to vote for war. It refused almost unanimously, an unprecedented rejection blamed on exhaustion after years of fighting Carthage. At a second meeting the consul changed their minds, and that autumn a Roman army crossed the Adriatic Sea to Greece.

## Flamininus Takes Command {#flamininus-takes-command}

Rome's war against Philip V, king of Macedon, began slowly. Roman troops raided Macedon's western borders and won over neighbouring peoples, while a small Roman force helped relieve Athens. Philip took revenge by burning the shrines and tombs outside the city and wrecking the sanctuaries of the Athenian countryside so thoroughly that they were never used again. One Roman commander even faced a mutiny by 2,000 veterans who simply wanted to go home.

In 198 BC command passed to Flamininus, a consul not yet thirty who proudly called himself a lover of Greek culture. Until then Rome had only told Philip to stop attacking Greek cities. Flamininus raised the price: Philip must compensate every city he had harmed and pull his garrisons out of everywhere beyond Macedon, even Thessaly, the broad plain of north-central Greece that Macedon had held since 353 BC. Philip stormed out of the meeting.

The two armies faced each other across the Aous river, where it runs through a narrow gorge in the mountains of north-western Greece. The ground favoured the Macedonians, but Flamininus was shown a hidden path through the mountains. He sent troops round to strike from behind, and Philip fled into Thessaly, leaving about 2,000 men dead or wounded.

## The Battle of Cynoscephalae {#cynoscephalae}

In the winter of 198 BC Philip V of Macedon offered to talk peace with the Roman commander Flamininus. Suspicious, the king refused to step ashore and addressed the Romans from the prow of his ship. The talks went nowhere, and ancient writers claimed this was deliberate. Elections were under way in Rome, and Flamininus did not yet know whether his command would be extended (*prorogatio*). If he was to be replaced, he wanted a quick peace and the credit for it. Once he learned he would stay, his friends in Rome quietly sank the deal.

Philip now called up every man his kingdom could spare, from aged veterans to underage boys: about 18,000 Macedonians, plus Thracian light troops and hired soldiers. In June 197 BC the two armies were camped near each other among the Cynoscephalae hills, whose name means "Dog's Heads", in Thessaly. Small detachments blundered into each other and the skirmish swelled into a full battle.

It was a clash of two ways of war. Macedon relied on the phalanx, a dense block of men armed with very long spears. Rome's legions (*legiones*) were more flexible, and they won. Philip escaped on horseback, gathered his survivors and was forced to ask for peace on Rome's terms.

## The Freedom of the Greeks {#freedom-of-the-greeks}

After his defeat at Cynoscephalae in 197 BC, Philip V of Macedon accepted a harsh peace. Back in Rome the Tribal Assembly (*comitia tributa*), another of the citizen assemblies, voted unanimously for it. Philip had to withdraw every garrison from the Greek cities of Europe and Asia and pay 1,000 talents, a talent being roughly 26 kg of silver. He gave up his entire navy except his flagship, and his army was capped at 5,000 men, with no war elephants and no marching abroad without permission from the Senate (*senatus*).

Then came a piece of political theatre. At the Isthmian Games of May 196 BC, a great festival near Corinth, the Roman commander Flamininus proclaimed freedom (*libertas*) for the Greek communities Philip had ruled. The crowd erupted in joy.

The freedom had limits. Rome kept troops in Corinth, Chalcis and Demetrias, three fortresses known as the "fetters of Greece" because whoever held them could control the country. The legions did not fully leave until 194 BC. The Aetolian League, a federation of central Greek states that had fought on Rome's side, felt cheated of cities it wanted and began claiming that Rome meant to become Greece's new master. Meanwhile, Rome used the slogan of Greek freedom to justify a war against Sparta in 195 BC.

## A Cold War with Antiochus {#cold-war-with-antiochus}

Antiochus III, ruler of the Seleucid Empire, the great kingdom based in Syria, was at the height of his power. He had brought Armenia and Iran back under his control and taken Syria and Judea from Egypt, earning the title "Great King". With Philip V of Macedon beaten by Rome, Antiochus pushed into western Asia Minor and then crossed into Europe, campaigning in Thrace, the land north-east of Greece.

Historians describe the years from 196 BC as a cold war. Rome saw Greece as its own sphere of influence and Asia Minor as a buffer zone; Antiochus saw Asia Minor as the heart of his empire and Greece as the buffer. Rome declared that even the Greek cities of Asia should be free. Antiochus neatly answered by declaring them free himself. In Rome, the victorious general Flamininus publicly promised to liberate the Greeks of Asia, while Roman negotiators told the king's envoys privately to stay on his side of the straits.

The spark came from the Aetolian League, the central Greek federation that now resented Rome. It invited Antiochus to liberate Greece. In 192 BC he landed at Demetrias, a fortress on the Greek coast, announcing he would free the Greeks from Roman rule. The Senate saw this as an intrusion it could not accept.

## Thermopylae and Magnesia {#thermopylae-and-magnesia}

Antiochus III, the Seleucid king who had landed in Greece in 192 BC, got a chilly welcome. Most Greeks found Rome's promises of freedom more convincing than his. In 191 BC a Roman consul arrived with a large army, and towns surrendered without a fight. Heavily outnumbered, Antiochus chose to make a stand at Thermopylae, the narrow coastal pass in central Greece. He was crushed and fled across the Aegean to Ephesus, less than six months after landing.

Rome and its allies then won command of the sea in two major naval battles. One of Antiochus's fleets, stopped by the Rhodians, was led by the old Carthaginian general who had once invaded Italy and now served at the king's court. In 190 BC a new consul, accompanied by his famous brother, the general who had defeated Carthage, crossed into Asia Minor. Antiochus offered to pay half of Rome's war costs; the Romans demanded everything, and he broke off talks.

Around December 190 BC the armies met near Magnesia in western Asia Minor. The king of Pergamon led a cavalry charge that smashed one wing, and Antiochus's own elephants threw his infantry into disorder. Ancient writers gave him 72,000 men against 30,000 Romans and allies; one modern study argues both sides had about 50,000.

## The Peace of Apamea {#peace-of-apamea}

Beaten at Magnesia in 190 BC, Antiochus III, king of the Seleucid Empire, accepted the terms he had earlier rejected. They were confirmed in 188 BC at Apamea, a town in inland Asia Minor. He gave up all his lands north and west of the Taurus Mountains, the range running along the south coast of Asia Minor. He agreed to pay 15,000 talents over twelve years, to keep only ten large warships, to give up war elephants and never to sail west of a certain cape. Twenty hostages (*obsides*), including his youngest son, went to Rome, and he had to hand over Rome's enemies. The great Carthaginian general at his court fled instead, and years later killed himself rather than be surrendered.

Rome took no land for itself. The territory went to its loyal allies, Pergamon and Rhodes. Rome's Greek enemies, the Aetolians, were reduced to a dependent state required to serve "the power and empire of the Roman people".

In practice Rome enforced only the clauses on land and money; the last payment arrived, slightly late, in 173 BC. The military limits were tied to Antiochus personally and lapsed when he died in 187 BC. His successors rebuilt their navy and fielded elephants again, and Rome largely looked the other way.

## Perseus and the Road to War {#perseus-road-to-war}

In 179 BC Philip V of Macedon died and was succeeded by his ambitious son Perseus. The new king built alliances by marriage, taking a Seleucid princess as his wife and engaging his daughter to the king of Bithynia, a rival of Pergamon. His agents courted Greeks who did not want to live at Rome's mercy, and he drove out a Thracian king who was Rome's ally.

The king of Pergamon, Rome's closest friend in the region, travelled to Rome and accused Perseus before the Senate of preparing for war ever since he took the throne, warning that he might even invade Italy. Soon afterwards that king was nearly murdered on a visit to Delphi, the great shrine of Apollo, and the plot was blamed on Perseus. Rome declared him a public enemy (*hostis*), and war began in 171 BC.

The early years went badly for Rome. Near a hill called Callinicus in Thessaly, Perseus's cavalry and light troops won a clear victory, killing 2,000 Roman infantry and 200 cavalry for small losses of their own. He then offered ever larger sums for peace, but Rome demanded total surrender. Roman commanders made matters worse by plundering friendly Greek cities, and one was fined back home. In 169 BC a consul finally forced his army over the Olympus mountains into Macedon, building bridges so his elephants could pass.

## The Battle of Pydna {#pydna}

In 168 BC Rome sent Aemilius Paullus, an experienced consul serving for the second time, to finish the war against Perseus, king of Macedon. Aemilius prepared carefully. He sent an officer with several thousand men on a secret march, pretending they were off to raid the coast, but in fact they climbed into the mountains and drove off the Macedonian guards. Outflanked, Perseus fell back to Pydna on the Macedonian coast.

That night the moon went into eclipse. An officer had predicted it, so the Roman soldiers were not frightened. Aemilius, a pious man, offered sacrifice after sacrifice, and only on the twenty-first ox did the omens promise victory if Rome did not strike first.

Ancient writers disagree on how the battle began. Some said Aemilius provoked it by driving a riderless horse toward the enemy. The version favoured by the Roman historian who told the story at greatest length is humbler: a mule broke loose while both sides were drawing water from a river, a scuffle over it grew, and both armies poured in.

At first the phalanx's long spears pierced Roman shields. But on uneven ground gaps opened in its ranks. Aemilius sent small units to slip into them like wedges and strike the exposed flanks, where the long spears were useless. Within about an hour it was over. Ancient writers counted some 20,000 Macedonians dead and 11,000 captured, against about 100 Romans, and the pursuit ran for about 4.5 km (three miles).

## The End of the Macedonian Kingdom {#end-of-macedon}

Perseus, the last king of Macedon, left the field at Pydna in 168 BC early. Ancient writers argued over why: one called it cowardice, another said he had been kicked by a horse the day before. He fled to his capital, Pella, then on to Samothrace, a sacred island in the northern Aegean, where he took refuge in a sanctuary. A Roman fleet blockaded the island. Perseus had the captain of his Cretan mercenaries killed, then tried to slip away by night, but the ship he had hired sailed off with his treasure. Abandoned by everyone except his eldest son, he surrendered.

In Rome, Aemilius Paullus, the victor of Pydna, celebrated a triumph (*triumphus*), the grand victory parade, with Perseus led in chains before it. The former king spent the rest of his life under guard at Alba Fucens, a town in central Italy.

Rome declared the Macedonians free, then dismantled their kingdom. It was split into four republics, centred on Amphipolis, Thessalonica, Pella and Pelagonia, each paying Rome a tribute (*tributum*) half what the king had taken. The national council was abolished, the gold and silver mines were closed and marriage between the districts was banned. Royal officials were deported to Italy. In Epirus, the region of north-western Greece that had backed Perseus, Roman troops sacked seventy towns on a single day and enslaved 150,000 people.
