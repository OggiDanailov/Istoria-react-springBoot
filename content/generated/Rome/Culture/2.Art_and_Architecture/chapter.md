# Empire: Roman Art and Architecture (c. 500 BC-AD 476)

## Borrowing from the Greeks {#greek-roots}

Roman art began as a borrower. Its earliest influences came from the Etruscans, a powerful people of central Italy north of Rome, and from the Greek colonies of southern Italy. The Etruscans had learned much from their Greek trading partners. One of their specialities was near life-size clay figures of the dead, reclining on their coffin lids like guests at a dinner party.

As Rome's armies conquered the Greek world, Greek art flooded into the city as war booty, extortion or trade. Temples were often decorated with secondhand Greek statues. After the sack of Corinth, a wealthy Greek city, in 146 BC, many Greek artists were carried off as slaves. By the 2nd century BC most sculptors working in Rome were Greek, and their names were rarely recorded.

Greek artists had been celebrated as masters, but Roman artists were mostly anonymous tradesmen, and almost no signed works survive. Older historians dismissed Roman art as mere imitation. Modern scholars see a creative blend instead, mixing Greek models with Etruscan, native Italian and even Egyptian ideas.

## Portraits and Ancestors {#portraits-and-ancestors}

If the Romans had one great artistic strength of their own, it was the portrait. It grew from their deep reverence for family. Ancestral busts were displayed in the entrance hall (*atrium*) of an aristocratic house, and at the funerals of great families, masks of the forebears (*imagines*) were worn in procession. None of these masks survives, but many surviving busts probably depict such ancestors.

Under the Republic (*res publica*), the era of elected government that began in 509 BC, it was a mark of good character not to hide physical flaws. Men were shown wrinkled and rugged, unconcerned with vanity. The face was a map of experience.

Things changed under the emperors. Augustus, Rome's first emperor, preferred portraits that were Greek in flavour and highly idealised. Busts of the ruler were sent across the empire to stand in provincial town halls, and his face appeared on coins. Because coins reached even remote villages, they became one of the main tools of imperial propaganda.

## Painted Walls of Pompeii {#painted-walls}

Romans prized painting, yet almost all of it is lost. The most prestigious kind was painted on wooden panels, and wood rots. What survives is mostly wall painting, much of it thanks to a disaster. When Mount Vesuvius erupted in AD 79, ash buried Pompeii and Herculaneum, wealthy towns on the Bay of Naples, preserving their decorated rooms.

The walls show everyday life, animals, myths, erotic scenes and still lifes of fruit, seafood and glass jars of water, later imitated by Renaissance artists. The great Roman innovation was landscape: gardens in flower, views of city buildings and scenes from the Odyssey, the Greek epic of a hero's voyage home, using an early form of perspective. Even tenants of cramped apartments sometimes painted jungle scenes or fake windows.

Rare panel portraits survive from the Fayum, a farming region of Roman Egypt, where realistic painted faces were attached to mummies. Pliny the Elder, the 1st-century AD writer whose encyclopedia is our main ancient source on art, grumbled that true portrait painting had died out through laziness.

## The Concrete Revolution {#concrete-revolution}

Rome's greatest artistic achievement was arguably a building material. Concrete had been used on a small scale in the Near East long before, but the Romans perfected it and made it the backbone of their grandest buildings. Historians call the result the "concrete revolution".

Roman concrete (*opus caementicium*) mixed lime mortar, water and rubble with pozzolana, a volcanic ash that made it unusually strong. Builders poured it into reusable wooden frames, then faced the finished wall with brick, plaster or thin slabs of coloured marble. The earliest known Roman use was at Cosa, a colony on the coast north of Rome, some time after 273 BC. Concrete was cheap and easy to transport.

Combined with the arch, borrowed from Rome's Etruscan neighbours, and with vaults and domes, concrete made possible vast covered spaces. Columns, once essential supports, often became mere decoration in front of load-bearing walls. Most of these techniques are described by Vitruvius, an architect of the 1st century BC whose handbook is the only Roman book on architecture to survive.

## Augustus and the City of Marble {#city-of-marble}

Augustus, Rome's first emperor (27 BC–AD 14), famously boasted that he had found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble. It was an exaggeration: marble is not found near Rome and was mostly used as a facing over brick or concrete. But from his reign the quarries at Carrara in northern Italy were opened up for the capital, and fine coloured marbles were imported from Greece.

The heart of every Roman town was its forum, an open square serving as market, meeting place and stage for political debate. Rome's original Forum was gradually joined by grander imperial forums, including one built by Augustus. Nearby stood basilicas, large halls where business was done and magistrates heard cases. The oldest known was built in 184 BC; centuries later, Christians adopted the shape for their first great churches.

Temples followed an Etruscan layout, with a high platform and steps only at the front, but used Greek column styles: the plain Doric, the scroll-topped Ionic and the leafy Corinthian. The main room (*cella*) housed the god's statue.

## Arenas, Baths and Apartment Blocks {#arenas-baths-apartments}

The amphitheatre, an oval arena ringed by tiered seats, was one of the few building types the Romans invented, along with the basilica and the triumphal arch (*arcus triumphalis*). More than 200 are known. The greatest, the Colosseum, was completed around AD 80. It held over 50,000 spectators, had retractable awnings for shade, and staged gladiator fights and even mock sea battles.

Every Roman city had public baths (*thermae*), where people spent hours bathing, swimming, wrestling and chatting for a tiny, state-subsidised fee. Rooms were warmed by underfloor heating (*hypocaustum*), which sent hot air from a furnace beneath raised floors and through gaps in the walls, a forerunner of central heating.

Most city dwellers lived not in houses but in apartment blocks (*insulae*), up to five storeys high. These were cramped, unhealthy and prone to fire, and the cheapest rooms were at the top, furthest from water and escape. In the early 4th century AD, Rome had 1,781 private town houses (*domus*) against 44,850 apartment blocks.

## The Golden Age of Trajan and Hadrian {#trajan-and-hadrian}

Rome reached its artistic peak under two emperors: Trajan (AD 98–117), the soldier-emperor who expanded the empire to its greatest size, and Hadrian (AD 117–138), his successor and a passionate builder. Both launched huge programmes of baths, aqueducts, palaces and temples.

Trajan's Column, dedicated in AD 113, tells the story of his wars in Dacia, modern Romania, in a carved band spiralling about 200 m (650 feet) around the shaft. More than 2,500 figures march, build and fight, an ancient documentary film. Where Greek sculptors told of war through myth, Romans preferred record, and the column is a priceless source on Roman armour.

Hadrian rebuilt the Pantheon, a temple to all the gods, in its present form. It is the best-preserved temple of the ancient world, crowned by a concrete dome with an open "eye" at its centre. The ceiling's height exactly equals the interior radius, creating a perfect half-sphere. Domes of this size were not surpassed until steel frames arrived in the late 19th century.

## Aqueducts, Roads and Bridges {#aqueducts-roads-bridges}

Roman engineering reached far beyond the capital. Aqueducts carried water to cities by gravity alone, along gentle downward slopes. Most channels ran underground; where valleys intervened, they crossed on arched bridges or through pressurised pipes. Rome's first aqueduct fed a fountain at the cattle market in the 4th century BC. By the 3rd century AD the city had eleven, supporting over a million people, and most of the water went to the public baths. The Pont du Gard in southern France and the aqueduct of Segovia in Spain still stand.

Roads, built from about 500 BC, moved armies, officials and trade; at their peak 29 great highways radiated from Rome. Roman bridges were the first large, lasting bridges ever built, and until the Industrial Revolution only Romans built bridges with concrete. The bridge of Trajan, the soldier-emperor, over the Danube was the longest arch bridge for a thousand years.

New towns followed a standard plan: a grid of straight streets around a central forum, enclosed by a protective wall.

## A New Style for a Christian Empire {#late-roman-style}

In the 3rd century AD, Roman sculpture changed dramatically. Artists abandoned, or perhaps lost the ability to produce, the graceful, lifelike classical style. Figures became stumpy, large-eyed and rigidly frontal, stressing power over beauty. Historians still debate why.

The contrast is plain on the Arch of Constantine, completed around AD 315 near the Colosseum. It was built for Constantine, the emperor under whom Christianity triumphed, and it combines older carved roundels in the full classical style with new panels of squat, hard-edged figures. A famous statue group of four co-ruling emperors, the Tetrarchs, carved around AD 305 and now in Venice, shows the same blocky look.

Christianity soon ended large religious statues; big figures were made only of emperors, like a colossal Constantine whose giant fragments survive. Rich Christians still commissioned carved stone coffins and small ivories. When Constantine moved the capital to Constantinople, the city on the Bosporus once called Byzantium, Roman art absorbed eastern influences and grew into the Byzantine style.
