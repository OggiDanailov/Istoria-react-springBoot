# Roman Religion and Mythology (753 BC-AD 391)

## A Deal with the Gods {#roman-piety}

The Romans thought of themselves as the most religious people on earth. They credited their rise to world power to their piety (*pietas*), the dutiful care with which they kept good relations with the gods. In their eyes, Rome's victories were proof of divine favour.

Yet Roman religion was not about faith or doctrine. It was practical, almost a business contract, summed up in the phrase *do ut des*: "I give, that you might give." A Roman offered a sacrifice, a prayer or a vow, and expected the god to deliver protection, victory or a good harvest in return.

What mattered was doing it correctly. Every sacrifice needed a spoken prayer, and the words had to be exact, down to the god's proper title. Public prayers were recited loudly by a priest on behalf of the whole community, and a single slip could spoil the ritual. A Roman historian tells of a magistrate who forgot to mention "the Roman people" in a festival prayer. The entire festival had to start again from the beginning.

Neglect was dangerous, but so was too much zeal. Fearful grovelling, excessive devotion and prying into divine knowledge were condemned as superstition (*superstitio*). Secret rites were suspect too, as possible magic or conspiracy. Romans believed that any of these could provoke the anger of the gods and bring harm on the whole state.

## Aeneas, Romulus and Remus {#aeneas-and-romulus}

According to legend, Rome's story began at Troy, the city in Asia Minor destroyed by the Greeks. The Trojan prince Aeneas, son of the goddess Venus, escaped and sailed to Italy carrying sacred objects, including the household gods of Troy and the Palladium, a holy statue said to protect a city. Romans believed these treasures were still guarded in their own day by the Vestal priestesses. Aeneas married a local king's daughter, and through a long line of kings at Alba Longa, a Latin town in the hills south-east of Rome, his bloodline led to Rome's founders.

Those founders were the twins Romulus and Remus. Their mother had been forced by her usurping uncle to stay a virgin, but she became pregnant by Mars, the god of war. The king ordered the babies abandoned, yet they survived through a series of miracles. Grown up, they restored their grandfather's throne and set out to build a new city, traditionally in 753 BC, first consulting the gods by watching birds. While the walls were rising, the brothers quarrelled and Romulus killed Remus.

Romulus was credited with Rome's first temple to Jupiter and its first victory parade. He never died a normal death: he was mysteriously carried off and made a god. Romans treated such tales as history, but for Rome's earliest period, history and myth are hard to tell apart.

## Numa, the Priest-King {#numa-pompilius}

Romans believed that most of their religious institutions went back to Numa, the peaceful second king of Rome. He was a Sabine, from a hill people living north-east of Rome. Legend says he dealt directly with the gods and took advice from Egeria, a nymph of springs and prophecy who was his divine companion.

Tradition credits Numa with an astonishing list of foundations: Rome's first calendar, the worship of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus, and several priesthoods. These included the Vestals and the flamens (*flamines*), priests each devoted to a single god. Jupiter's own flamen was bound by such strict rules of ritual purity that he could barely pursue a political or military career.

Numa also founded the Temple of Janus, the two-faced god of doorways. Its doors stood open in wartime and closed in peace. Under Numa they stayed shut. After his death, so the story went, they remained open for centuries, until the reign of Augustus, Rome's first emperor.

Roman historians believed the essentials of their religion were complete by the end of Numa's reign. Later kings added more: an Etruscan king founded the great temple on the Capitoline Hill, and another built a temple to Diana to mark Rome's alliance with its Latin neighbours. These kings, however, are legendary or semi-legendary figures, and their deeds belong to tradition rather than reliable record.

## Jupiter and the Gods of Rome {#gods-of-rome}

Rome had no creation myth of its own and few stories about the personalities of its gods. Instead it had a crowded heaven of gods with practical jobs. Janus guarded the door and Vesta the hearth, Ceres watched over the growing grain and Pomona the fruit. Old farming prayers called on a separate minor god for each stage of ploughing or sowing.

At the top stood Jupiter, god of sky, rain and lightning, and protector of the state. In the earliest era he shared worship with Mars, god of war and farming, and Quirinus, patron of the citizens in peacetime. Modern scholars call this trio the Archaic Triad. It was later replaced by the Capitoline Triad: Jupiter, his wife Juno and his daughter Minerva, goddess of wisdom. One god with two goddesses was an unusual grouping, and it may come from the Etruscans, the powerful people of Etruria north of Rome, who worshipped a similar trio.

The triad's great temple stood on the Capitoline Hill above the Forum, with three separate chambers: Juno on the left, Minerva on the right, Jupiter in the middle. Copies sprang up in towns across Italy and the provinces.

Through contact with Greek settlers in southern Italy, Romans matched their gods with Greek ones and borrowed Greek myths, so that many Roman stories were reworked from Greek models.

## Roman and Greek Gods {#roman-and-greek-gods}

Rome's twelve great gods, six gods and six goddesses, were called the Harmonious Gods (*Dii Consentes*). The poet Ennius listed them in the late 3rd century BC, and their gilded statues stood in the Forum. Each was matched with a Greek god, and over time the Romans took over that god's myths: Jupiter inherited the stories of Zeus, and Venus those of Aphrodite.

A pair was never quite the same god. Mars, father of Romulus and guardian of the fields as well as god of war, was one of the most honoured gods in Rome, while the Greeks thought Ares brutal and liked him little. Apollo, god of light, music and prophecy, is the one great god who kept his Greek name.

The first twelve are the Harmonious Gods. Some Roman gods had no Greek twin at all. Janus, the two-faced god of doorways and beginnings, and Quirinus, patron of the citizens, were purely Roman.

## Priests, Augurs and Sacred Chickens {#priests-and-augurs}

Rome had no separate priestly class. The same aristocrats who were elected to political office also served as priests, married, and led busy public lives. Priesthoods were unpaid and held for life, and were often sought as an honourable retirement after a career in war and politics. At the head stood the chief priest (*pontifex maximus*), who led the college of pontiffs (*pontifices*), Rome's experts in religious law.

The augurs (*augures*) read the will of the gods, above all through the flight of birds. They worked within a sacred space marked out by ritual, called a *templum*, a word that only later came to mean a temple building. Magistrates had to consult the augurs before official acts, and a bad sign could halt public business for the day. Cicero, a leading statesman of the 1st century BC and himself an augur, called them the most powerful authority in Rome.

Another kind of specialist, the haruspex, inspected the entrails of sacrificed animals, especially the liver. This art came from the Etruscans and was seen as essential but slightly foreign.

Ignoring the signs was risky. In 249 BC, during Rome's first war with Carthage, the great North African trading city, the consul Claudius Pulcher found that the sacred chickens refused to eat before a sea battle. He threw them overboard, joking that if they would not eat, they could drink. He lost the battle, and his career with it.

## The Vestal Virgins {#vestals}

The Vestals (*virgines Vestales*) were Rome's most famous priesthood: six women who tended the sacred flame of Vesta, goddess of the hearth of the Roman state. Their fire was the public twin of the fire in every home, and any household could rekindle its own hearth from it. They also guarded the state's household gods and, Romans believed, sacred objects brought from Troy by Aeneas, the legendary Trojan ancestor of the Romans.

A girl chosen as a Vestal gained extraordinary status. She was freed from her father's authority and, unlike other Roman women, needed no male guardian, answering only to the chief priest. The price was a vow of chastity, strictly enforced. A Vestal found to have broken it was buried alive.

Their work touched every official sacrifice. They prepared the *mola salsa*, a salted flour sprinkled on each animal before it was killed, and took part in festivals honouring the dead and the harvest. Legend tied them to Rome's very beginnings: one tradition held that the mother of the founders Romulus and Remus had been a Vestal of royal blood.

Augustus, the first emperor, boosted their funding and gave them seats of honour at the games. The order survived for centuries, well into the Christian era. Then Theodosius, the emperor who made Christianity the state religion, put out Vesta's fire and emptied her temple.

## Religion at Home and in the Calendar {#home-and-festivals}

For ordinary Romans, religion was part of daily life. Every home had a shrine to the Lares, guardian spirits of house and fields, and the Penates, gods of the household stores. The father of the family (*paterfamilias*) acted as its priest, making daily offerings, while his wife looked after the worship of Vesta at the hearth. Offerings were modest: grain, grapes, honey cakes, wine, incense, even food that fell on the floor during a meal.

Roman calendars listed about forty annual religious festivals. Some featured games such as chariot races and plays. Others belonged to particular groups, like the rites of the Good Goddess, from which men were completely barred. Some festivals were so ancient that even learned Romans no longer knew what they meant.

The dead were cared for too. Wealthier families sacrificed a sow at the funeral pyre and shared a last meal with the deceased. Afterwards the dead were believed to join the spirits of the underworld, and tombstones were commonly dedicated "to the spirit-gods" (*Dis Manibus*). Each February the Parentalia, a festival of several days, honoured family ancestors. It was so popular that Christians were still attending it centuries later, until a church council forbade it in AD 567.

## Foreign Gods and Mystery Cults {#foreign-gods}

Rome rarely tried to stamp out other peoples' gods. It absorbed them instead, believing that preserving tradition kept society stable. Gods from conquered Italian towns gained temples in Rome. A general at war might even invite the enemy's god to change sides, a ritual called *evocatio*. During the siege of Veii, a rival Etruscan city just north of Rome, a Roman commander promised Veii's goddess Juno a new home in Rome. After the city fell, her statue was carried there and installed in a temple on the Aventine Hill.

Crises brought new arrivals. In 206 BC, during Rome's desperate second war against Carthage, the North African power that had invaded Italy, Rome's sacred prophecy books advised bringing in the Great Mother (*Magna Mater*), a goddess from Asia Minor. Her eunuch priests and frenzied rites unsettled Romans, and citizens were forbidden to join that priesthood.

Mystery cults, which offered secret initiations and hope for the afterlife, drew growing followings. Isis came from Egypt, and Mithras became a favourite of soldiers, with shrines as far away as Britain. Romans distrusted secrecy, though. In 186 BC the Senate cracked down on the Bacchanalia, the wildly popular rites of the wine god Bacchus, and placed them under strict state control.

## Caesar, Augustus and the Imperial Cult {#emperors-as-gods}

In the late Republic, ambitious politicians began claiming special bonds with the gods. Julius Caesar, the conquering general who became dictator, said the goddess Venus was his ancestress, through her son Aeneas. In 63 BC he was chosen as chief priest (*pontifex maximus*), a sign of his rise. He is the only Roman known to have been treated as a god while still alive, and he was murdered soon after. A comet seen during games after his death was taken as proof that he had joined the gods.

His heir Augustus, Rome's first emperor, justified one-man rule with a sweeping religious revival. He restored old priesthoods, raised up the Vestals and became chief priest himself; later emperors kept the title. Vows once made for the safety of the Republic were now made for the emperor's wellbeing. Shortly before his death in AD 14, sailors at Naples hailed him: "Because of you we are living, because of you we can travel the seas."

In Rome, a living emperor was honoured through his genius, the divine spirit believed to live in every person. After his death, the Senate could vote him a god (*divus*). This imperial cult gave a vast, diverse empire a shared loyalty, and it made refusing to take part look like treason.

## Christianity Takes Over {#christian-triumph}

That expectation set Rome on a collision course with the Christians. To Roman officials, a faith that rejected every other god looked like atheism and dangerous superstition. After the Great Fire of Rome in AD 64, the emperor Nero blamed the Christians, and persecution followed. In the crises of the 3rd century, emperors ordered all subjects to sacrifice to the ancestral gods. In AD 303 new edicts commanded that churches and Christian books be destroyed. Some Christians were imprisoned or executed; others gave in.

Everything changed with Constantine, the emperor who converted to Christianity. In 313 the Edict of Milan proclaimed toleration, and Constantine paid for churches, including St Peter's Basilica. He called the bishops together at Nicaea, a town in Asia Minor, to settle Christian doctrine. Yet at his death in 337 he was honoured both as a Christian and as a deified emperor. His nephew Julian, emperor from 361, tried to revive the old cults, but died in Persia two years later.

In 380, under Theodosius, Christianity as defined at Nicaea became the official religion of the empire. In 391 he ended state support for all other cults and extinguished the Vestals' sacred fire. The old religion did not vanish entirely: many of its customs lived on inside Christian festivals and local traditions.
