# Early Rome & the Kingdom: The Foundation of an Empire (753-509 BCE)

## A City Built From Villages {#origins}

Long before any king, the site of Rome had real advantages. A ford let travellers cross the Tiber River in central Italy, and a cluster of steep, easily defended hills rose from a wide, fertile plain.

Archaeologists have found traces of Bronze Age settlement on the Capitoline Hill, possibly from as early as 1700 BC. By 1000 BC the valley that later became the Roman Forum, the city's great public square, was a cemetery. Huts stood on the Palatine Hill by the 9th or 8th century BC, and around 730–720 BC someone built a wall there.

At about the same time, burials in the Forum stopped and parts of it were paved over, turning it into a shared meeting and worship space. Rome, in other words, did not spring up in a day. It grew from the gradual merging of several hilltop villages, probably inspired by the idea of the city-state then spreading from Greece. By 600 BC the process was complete. Rome had a central forum, monumental buildings and public temples.

## The Legend of Romulus and Remus {#romulus-and-remus}

The Romans told a far more dramatic story of their beginnings. According to legend, Rhea Silvia was a princess of Alba Longa, a Latin town in the hills south-east of Rome. Her uncle Amulius had seized the throne from her father, Numitor, and forced her to become a Vestal Virgin, a priestess sworn to chastity. She nevertheless bore twins, Romulus and Remus, supposedly fathered by Mars, the god of war.

Amulius ordered the babies left to die by the Tiber. Instead a she-wolf suckled them in a cave called the Lupercal, and a shepherd named Faustulus raised them. Grown up, the twins overthrew Amulius and restored their grandfather. They then set out to found a city of their own, but quarrelled, and Remus was killed. Romulus built his city on the Palatine Hill.

The official founding date, worked out by the scholar Varro, was 753 BC, celebrated on 21 April. Later Romans also traced the twins' family back to Aeneas, a hero who fled the fall of Troy. Most modern historians treat all this as myth. Even the name Romulus was probably invented from the city's name, making him, as the classicist Mary Beard put it, "Mr Rome".

## Romulus, the First King {#romulus-the-king}

In Roman tradition Romulus, the legendary founder of Rome, was also its first king, and he was credited with creating its basic institutions. He welcomed newcomers of every kind, including exiles, criminals and runaway slaves. He chose 100 leading men as his advisers. They were called the "fathers", and their descendants became the patricians, Rome's hereditary aristocracy. To show his authority, he was escorted by twelve attendants called lictors.

His new city had too few women. So, the story goes, Romulus invited the Sabines, a hill people living north-east of Rome, to a religious festival, and his men carried off their young women. War followed. It ended only when the captured women threw themselves between the armies. The two peoples then united, and the Sabine king Titus Tatius shared the throne with Romulus.

After 37 years, Romulus vanished during a storm while reviewing his troops. Some whispered that the nobles had murdered him and hidden the pieces of his body. But one respected nobleman claimed Romulus had appeared to him in a vision as the god Quirinus. Romans maintained a hut said to be his in the heart of the city until the end of the empire.

## How the Kings Ruled {#how-kings-ruled}

After Romulus, Rome's kings were not born to the throne. According to tradition, they were chosen for life. When a king died, the Senate, the council of leading men, took charge. One senator became the interrex, or "between-king", serving for five days to find a candidate, then handing over to another if he failed. The chosen man was presented to the Curiate Assembly, the gathering of citizens, which accepted or rejected him.

Two more steps followed. A priest called an augur took the king-elect up to the citadel and looked for signs that the gods approved. Then the assembly granted him imperium, the supreme power to command armies, govern and judge. The king was also Rome's chief priest and controlled the calendar. The Senate could advise him but rarely stop him, though he could not declare war without approval.

Twelve attendants walked ahead of him carrying the fasces, a bundle of rods around an axe that symbolised his power to punish. He sat on a special chair and wore a purple robe embroidered with gold, with red shoes and a white band around his head.

The reality may have been rougher. The historian Arnaldo Momigliano suggested that many kings were really leaders of armed bands who won over the aristocracy by persuasion or force.

## Numa, Tullus and Ancus {#numa-tullus-ancus}

Tradition names seven kings of Rome. After Romulus came three very different rulers.

When Romulus vanished, Rome waited a year before choosing a Sabine named Numa Pompilius, famed for his justice and piety. His 43-year reign was remembered for peace and religion. He closed the doors of the temple of Janus, a god of beginnings, as a sign that Rome was at peace. He also founded the Vestal Virgins and created the office of chief priest. Legend credits him with adding January and February to make a twelve-month calendar.

Tullus Hostilius was the opposite: warlike and scornful of the gods. He fought Rome's neighbours and destroyed Alba Longa, the Latin town said to be Romulus's birthplace, moving its people to Rome. He built a senate house that lasted centuries. According to Livy, when the dying king finally begged Jupiter for help, the god burned him and his house with lightning. He had reigned 32 years.

Numa's grandson Ancus Marcius ruled peacefully for 25 years. He built Rome's first prison and, tradition says, its first bridge over the Tiber. He founded Ostia, a port at the river's mouth, and settled conquered Latins on the Aventine Hill, forming the origins of the plebeians, Rome's common people.

## Tarquinius Priscus, the First Etruscan King {#tarquinius-priscus}

Rome's fifth king, Lucius Tarquinius Priscus, was an immigrant. According to tradition he was born an Etruscan, one of the wealthy, powerful peoples living north of Rome in what is now Tuscany. After settling in Rome he won the favour of King Ancus Marcius, who adopted him as a son.

As king, Tarquinius fought the Sabines and Etruscans, reportedly doubling Rome's size and bringing home great treasure. He added 100 new members to the Senate, raising it to 200.

He spent his loot on ambitious building. The Cloaca Maxima, or "Great Sewer", drained the marshy ground between Rome's hills, and on the reclaimed land he began the Roman Forum. He also laid out the Circus Maximus, a giant stadium for chariot races, and founded the Roman games. Work began on a great temple to Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill, though he did not live to finish it.

Tarquinius is also remembered for introducing the symbols of Roman office and for celebrating the first triumph, the victory parade granted to successful generals. After 38 years on the throne, he was murdered by a son of Ancus Marcius.

## Servius Tullius and the Mystery of Mastarna {#servius-tullius}

According to tradition, Rome's sixth king, Servius Tullius, was the son of a slave who married the daughter of the previous king, Tarquinius Priscus. Like his father-in-law, he was said to be of Etruscan birth, and he fought successful wars against the Etruscans.

His lasting fame rests on reorganising Roman society. He held Rome's first census, a count of citizens, and sorted people into five classes by wealth. These classes became the basis of a new voting assembly, organised so that the rich had the most influence. He also divided the city into four districts by where people lived, and he built the Temple of Diana on the Aventine Hill. Later, though, he was said to favour the poor at the nobles' expense.

After 44 years he was killed in a plot by his own daughter, Tullia, and her husband, the future king Tarquin the Proud.

A puzzle surrounds him. The emperor Claudius, in the first century AD, identified Servius with Mastarna, an Etruscan adventurer who became king of Rome. Mastarna also appears on a painted Etruscan tomb. The historian Momigliano warned, however, that Mastarna is so unlike the traditional Servius that the two are best kept apart.

## Tarquin the Proud and Lucretia {#tarquin-the-proud}

Rome's seventh and last king, Lucius Tarquinius Superbus, "Tarquin the Proud", seized power by murdering his father-in-law, King Servius Tullius. He was the son of the earlier king Tarquinius Priscus, and according to tradition he ruled for 25 years.

He was an energetic ruler. He fought Rome's neighbours, including the Volsci, a hill people to the south-east, and made Rome the leading power among the Latin cities. He finished the great temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill. But Romans remembered him as a tyrant who ruled through violence and fear, ignored custom and the Senate, and even judged death-penalty cases without advisers.

According to the traditional story, the crisis came when the king's son Sextus Tarquinius raped Lucretia, a virtuous noblewoman. She told her husband and father what had happened and then killed herself rather than live with the dishonour. Her death sparked an uprising. The tale later inspired Shakespeare's poem Lucrece and many paintings in the 18th and 19th centuries.

## The Fall of the Kings {#birth-of-the-republic}

According to tradition, the revolt against Tarquin the Proud, Rome's last king, came in 509 BC. It was led by Lucius Junius Brutus, the king's nephew. He was joined by Lucretia's husband Collatinus, her father, and a friend named Publius Valerius Poplicola. While the king was away besieging Ardea, a town south of Rome, they shut the city gates against him and won over the army.

In place of a king, Rome now elected two consuls each year, starting with Brutus and Collatinus. Brutus made the people swear never again to accept a king. When his own sons were caught plotting to bring Tarquin back, he ordered their execution.

Tarquin fought to return. An Etruscan king, Lars Porsenna of Clusium, marched on Rome, but legend says the hero Horatius held a bridge alone until it could be torn down. Rome then defeated an alliance of Latin towns at Lake Regillus. Tarquin died in exile at Cumae, a Greek city near Naples, in 495 BC.

The story echoed through later history. French revolutionaries named boys and towns after Brutus, and the authors of the American Federalist Papers signed themselves "Publius" after Poplicola.

## What Historians Really Know {#how-reliable}

Almost nothing written survives from the age of Rome's kings. Ancient writers blamed the Gauls, invaders from the north who sacked Rome in 390 BC, for destroying many records. The first history of Rome was written by Fabius Pictor around 200 BC, about three centuries after the kings fell. Writers such as Livy relied largely on oral tradition, family stories and even plays.

Modern historians are therefore cautious. Seven kings ruling for 243 years means an average reign of almost 35 years, which seems far too long. Archaeology shows that Rome did have kings, but most scholars doubt the traditional stories about them.

The overthrow is especially suspect. Lucretia's story closely resembles a Greek tale about the fall of the tyrants of Athens, around 510 BC. And the Roman historians Tacitus and Pliny recorded that Lars Porsenna, the Etruscan king, actually captured Rome. The historian Tim Cornell suggests that Porsenna may have ended the monarchy himself.

Others think the kingship faded slowly rather than falling in one dramatic night. Republican Rome kept a priest called the "king of sacred rites", perhaps a last ceremonial echo of the old kings.
