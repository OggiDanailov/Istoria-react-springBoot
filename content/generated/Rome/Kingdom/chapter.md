# The Roman Kingdom (*Regnum Romanum*)

## A City Built From Villages {#origins}

Long before any king (*rex*), the site of Rome (*Roma*) had real advantages. A ford let travellers cross the Tiber River (*Tiberis*) in central Italy (*Italia*), and a cluster of steep, easily defended hills (*colles*, *montes*) rose from a wide, fertile plain.

Archaeologists have found traces of Bronze Age settlement on the Capitoline Hill (*Mons Capitolinus*, or simply *Capitolium*), possibly from as early as 1700 BC. By 1000 BC the valley that later became the Roman Forum (*Forum Romanum*), the city's great public square, was a cemetery. Huts stood on the Palatine Hill (*Mons Palatinus*, or *Palatium*) by the 9th or 8th century BC, and around 730–720 BC someone built a wall (*murus*) there.

At about the same time, burials in the Forum stopped and parts of it were paved over, turning it into a shared meeting and worship space. Rome, in other words, did not spring up in a day. It grew from the gradual merging of several hilltop villages, probably inspired by the idea of the city-state (*civitas*; Greek *polis*) then spreading from Greece (*Graecia*). The later festival of the *Septimontium* ("Seven Hills") may preserve a memory of these early hill communities. By 600 BC the process was complete. Rome had a central forum, monumental buildings and public temples (*templa*).

## The Legend of Romulus and Remus {#romulus-and-remus}

The Romans (*Romani*) told a far more dramatic story of their beginnings. According to legend, Rhea Silvia (also called *Ilia*) was a princess of Alba Longa, a Latin town in the hills south-east of Rome. Her uncle Amulius had seized the throne from her father, Numitor, and forced her to become a Vestal Virgin (*Virgo Vestalis*), a priestess of the goddess Vesta sworn to chastity. She nevertheless bore twins (*gemini*), Romulus and Remus, supposedly fathered by Mars (*Mars*, also *Mavors*), the god of war.

Amulius ordered the babies left to die by the Tiber. Instead a she-wolf (*lupa*) suckled them in a cave called the Lupercal (*Lupercal*), and a shepherd (*pastor*) named Faustulus raised them. Grown up, the twins overthrew Amulius and restored their grandfather. They then set out to found a city (*urbs*) of their own, but quarrelled, and Remus was killed. Romulus built his city on the Palatine Hill.

The official founding date, worked out by the scholar Varro (*Marcus Terentius Varro*), was 753 BC, the year from which Romans counted *ab urbe condita* ("from the founding of the city", abbreviated AUC). It was celebrated on 21 April, the festival of the *Parilia*. Later Romans also traced the twins' family back to Aeneas (*Aeneas*), a hero who fled the fall of Troy (*Troia*). Most modern historians treat all this as myth. Even the name Romulus was probably invented from the city's name, making him, as the classicist Mary Beard put it, "Mr Rome".

## Romulus, the First King {#romulus-the-king}

In Roman tradition Romulus, the legendary founder (*conditor*) of Rome, was also its first king, and he was credited with creating its basic institutions. He welcomed newcomers of every kind, including exiles, criminals and runaway slaves (*servi fugitivi*), offering them refuge in a sanctuary called the *Asylum*. He chose 100 leading men as his advisers. They were called the "fathers" (*patres*), and together they formed the Senate (*senatus*, from *senex*, "old man"). Their descendants became the patricians (*patricii*), Rome's hereditary aristocracy. To show his authority, he was escorted by twelve attendants called lictors (*lictores*).

His new city had too few women. So, the story goes, Romulus invited the Sabines (*Sabini*), a hill people living north-east of Rome, to a religious festival (the *Consualia*, in honour of the god Consus), and his men carried off their young women — the episode known as the "Rape of the Sabine Women" (*raptio Sabinarum*). War followed. It ended only when the captured women threw themselves between the armies. The two peoples then united, and the Sabine king Titus Tatius shared the throne with Romulus.

After 37 years, Romulus vanished during a storm while reviewing his troops at the Goat's Marsh (*Caprae Palus*). Some whispered that the nobles had murdered him and hidden the pieces of his body. But one respected nobleman, Proculus Julius (*Proculus Iulius*), claimed Romulus had appeared to him in a vision as the god Quirinus (*Quirinus*). Romans maintained a hut said to be his, the *Casa Romuli*, in the heart of the city until the end of the empire (*Imperium Romanum*).

## How the Kings Ruled {#how-kings-ruled}

After Romulus, Rome's kings were not born to the throne. According to tradition, they were chosen for life. When a king died, the Senate (*senatus*), the council of leading men, took charge. One senator became the *interrex*, or "between-king", serving for five days to find a candidate, then handing over to another if he failed. The period without a king was called the *interregnum*. The chosen man was presented to the Curiate Assembly (*comitia curiata*), the gathering of citizens (*cives*) organised in thirty *curiae*, which accepted or rejected him.

Two more steps followed. A priest called an augur (*augur*) took the king-elect up to the citadel (*arx*) and looked for signs (*auspicia*, the "auspices") that the gods approved, a ceremony called the *inauguratio*. Then the assembly granted him *imperium*, the supreme power to command armies, govern and judge, through a law known as the *lex curiata de imperio*. The king was also Rome's chief priest and controlled the calendar (*calendarium*). The Senate could advise him but rarely stop him, though he could not declare war without approval.

Twelve attendants (*lictores*) walked ahead of him carrying the *fasces*, a bundle of rods (*virgae*) around an axe (*securis*) that symbolised his power to punish. He sat on a special chair, the *sella curulis* (curule chair), and wore a purple robe embroidered with gold (*toga picta* or *trabea*), with red shoes (*calcei mullei*) and a white band around his head (*diadema*).

The reality may have been rougher. The historian Arnaldo Momigliano suggested that many kings were really leaders of armed bands who won over the aristocracy by persuasion or force.

## Numa, Tullus and Ancus {#numa-tullus-ancus}

Tradition names seven kings (*septem reges*) of Rome. After Romulus came three very different rulers.

When Romulus vanished, Rome waited a year before choosing a Sabine named Numa Pompilius, famed for his justice (*iustitia*) and piety (*pietas*). His 43-year reign was remembered for peace (*pax*) and religion (*religio*). He closed the doors of the temple of Janus (*Ianus*, the shrine known as *Ianus Geminus*), a god of beginnings, as a sign that Rome was at peace. He also founded the Vestal Virgins (*Virgines Vestales*) and created the office of chief priest (*Pontifex Maximus*). Legend credits him with adding January (*Ianuarius*) and February (*Februarius*) to make a twelve-month calendar.

Tullus Hostilius was the opposite: warlike and scornful of the gods. He fought Rome's neighbours and destroyed Alba Longa, the Latin town said to be Romulus's birthplace, moving its people to Rome. He built a senate house (*Curia Hostilia*, named after him) that lasted centuries. According to Livy (*Titus Livius*), when the dying king finally begged Jupiter (*Iuppiter*) for help, the god burned him and his house with lightning (*fulmen*). He had reigned 32 years.

Numa's grandson Ancus Marcius ruled peacefully for 25 years. He built Rome's first prison (*carcer*) and, tradition says, its first bridge over the Tiber, the wooden *Pons Sublicius*. He founded Ostia (from *ostium*, "river mouth"), a port at the river's mouth, and settled conquered Latins (*Latini*) on the Aventine Hill (*Mons Aventinus*), forming the origins of the plebeians (*plebs*, *plebeii*), Rome's common people.

## Tarquinius Priscus, the First Etruscan King {#tarquinius-priscus}

Rome's fifth king, Lucius Tarquinius Priscus (*Priscus*, "the Elder"), was an immigrant. According to tradition he was born an Etruscan (*Etruscus*; the Romans also called these people *Tusci*), one of the wealthy, powerful peoples living north of Rome in what is now Tuscany. Livy says his original name was Lucumo, and that he came from the Etruscan city of Tarquinii. After settling in Rome he won the favour of King Ancus Marcius, who adopted him as a son.

As king, Tarquinius fought the Sabines and Etruscans, reportedly doubling Rome's size and bringing home great treasure (*praeda*, "booty"). He added 100 new members to the Senate, raising it to 200.

He spent his loot on ambitious building. The *Cloaca Maxima*, or "Great Sewer", drained the marshy ground between Rome's hills, and on the reclaimed land he began the Roman Forum (*Forum Romanum*). He also laid out the *Circus Maximus*, a giant stadium for chariot races, and founded the Roman games (*Ludi Romani*, also called *Ludi Magni*). Work began on a great temple to Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill, the Temple of Jupiter Best and Greatest (*aedes Iovis Optimi Maximi Capitolini*), though he did not live to finish it.

Tarquinius is also remembered for introducing the symbols of Roman office (*insignia*) and for celebrating the first triumph (*triumphus*), the victory parade granted to successful generals. After 38 years on the throne, he was murdered by a son of Ancus Marcius.

## Servius Tullius and the Mystery of Mastarna {#servius-tullius}

According to tradition, Rome's sixth king, Servius Tullius, was the son of a slave (*serva*; Roman writers name his mother as Ocrisia) who married the daughter of the previous king, Tarquinius Priscus. Like his father-in-law, he was said to be of Etruscan birth, and he fought successful wars against the Etruscans.

His lasting fame rests on reorganising Roman society. He held Rome's first census (*census*), a count of citizens, and sorted people into five classes (*classes*) by wealth. These classes, subdivided into "centuries" (*centuriae*), became the basis of a new voting assembly (*comitia centuriata*), organised so that the rich had the most influence. He also divided the city into four districts (*tribus*, "tribes") by where people lived, and he built the Temple of Diana (*aedes Dianae*) on the Aventine Hill. Later, though, he was said to favour the poor at the nobles' expense.

After 44 years he was killed in a plot by his own daughter, Tullia, and her husband, the future king Tarquin the Proud.

A puzzle surrounds him. The emperor Claudius (*Tiberius Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus*), in the first century AD, identified Servius with Mastarna (Etruscan *Macstrna*), an Etruscan adventurer who became king of Rome. Claudius's speech survives on a bronze tablet found in Lyon (*Lugdunum*), the *Tabula Claudiana*. Mastarna also appears on a painted Etruscan tomb, the François Tomb at Vulci. The historian Momigliano warned, however, that Mastarna is so unlike the traditional Servius that the two are best kept apart.

## Tarquin the Proud and Lucretia {#tarquin-the-proud}

Rome's seventh and last king, Lucius Tarquinius Superbus (*Superbus*, "the Proud"), seized power by murdering his father-in-law, King Servius Tullius. He was the son of the earlier king Tarquinius Priscus, and according to tradition he ruled for 25 years.

He was an energetic ruler. He fought Rome's neighbours, including the Volsci (*Volsci*), a hill people to the south-east, and made Rome the leading power among the Latin cities (*nomen Latinum*, the "Latin name"). He finished the great temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill. But Romans remembered him as a tyrant (*tyrannus*) who ruled through violence and fear, ignored custom (*mos maiorum*, "the ways of the ancestors") and the Senate, and even judged death-penalty cases (*res capitales*) without advisers.

According to the traditional story, the crisis came when the king's son Sextus Tarquinius raped Lucretia (*Lucretia*), a virtuous noblewoman. She told her husband, Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus, and her father, Spurius Lucretius Tricipitinus, what had happened and then killed herself rather than live with the dishonour. Her death sparked an uprising. The tale later inspired Shakespeare's poem Lucrece and many paintings in the 18th and 19th centuries.

## The Fall of the Kings {#birth-of-the-republic}

According to tradition, the revolt against Tarquin the Proud, Rome's last king, came in 509 BC. It was led by Lucius Junius Brutus (*Lucius Iunius Brutus*), the king's nephew. He was joined by Lucretia's husband Collatinus, her father, and a friend named Publius Valerius Poplicola (also spelled *Publicola*, "friend of the people"). While the king was away besieging Ardea, a town of the Rutuli (*Rutuli*) south of Rome, they shut the city gates (*portae*) against him and won over the army (*exercitus*).

In place of a king, Rome now elected two consuls (*consules*) each year, starting with Brutus and Collatinus. This marked the birth of the Republic (*res publica*, "the public thing"). Brutus made the people swear an oath (*iusiurandum*) never again to accept a king. When his own sons were caught plotting to bring Tarquin back, he ordered their execution.

Tarquin fought to return. An Etruscan king, Lars Porsenna (*Lars Porsena*) of Clusium (*Clusium*), marched on Rome, but legend says the hero Horatius (*Horatius Cocles*, "the one-eyed") held a bridge, the *Pons Sublicius*, alone until it could be torn down. Rome then defeated an alliance of Latin towns at Lake Regillus (*Lacus Regillus*). Tarquin died in exile (*exsilium*) at Cumae (*Cumae*), a Greek city near Naples (*Neapolis*), in 495 BC.

The story echoed through later history. French revolutionaries named boys and towns after Brutus, and the authors of the American Federalist Papers signed themselves "Publius" after Poplicola.

## What Historians Really Know {#how-reliable}

Almost nothing written survives from the age of Rome's kings. Ancient writers blamed the Gauls (*Galli*), invaders from the north who sacked Rome in 390 BC, for destroying many records. The first history of Rome was written by Fabius Pictor (*Quintus Fabius Pictor*) around 200 BC, about three centuries after the kings fell; he wrote in Greek. Writers such as Livy, whose great history is called *Ab Urbe Condita* ("From the Founding of the City"), relied largely on oral tradition, family stories and even plays.

Modern historians are therefore cautious. Seven kings ruling for 243 years means an average reign of almost 35 years, which seems far too long. Archaeology shows that Rome did have kings, but most scholars doubt the traditional stories about them.

The overthrow is especially suspect. Lucretia's story closely resembles a Greek tale about the fall of the tyrants of Athens, around 510 BC. And the Roman historians Tacitus (*Publius Cornelius Tacitus*) and Pliny (*Gaius Plinius Secundus*, "Pliny the Elder") recorded that Lars Porsenna, the Etruscan king, actually captured Rome. The historian Tim Cornell suggests that Porsenna may have ended the monarchy himself.

Others think the kingship faded slowly rather than falling in one dramatic night. Republican Rome kept a priest called the "king of sacred rites" (*rex sacrorum*), perhaps a last ceremonial echo of the old kings.
