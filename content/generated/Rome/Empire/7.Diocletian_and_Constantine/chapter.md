# Empire: Diocletian and Constantine (AD 284-337)

## Diocletian: From Humble Soldier to Emperor {#diocletian-rise}

Diocletian, the soldier-emperor who pulled Rome back from the brink, was born around AD 243 in Dalmatia, a Roman province on the Adriatic coast of modern Croatia. His name was then plain Diocles, and his family was humble. Ancient writers called his father a scribe, or said that Diocles himself had been a senator’s freed slave.

He rose through the army at a desperate time. Since 235 the empire had suffered the Crisis of the Third Century, an age of invasions, revolts and short-lived emperors. By 282 Diocles commanded the cavalry that guarded the emperor's household.

In 284 the young emperor in the East was found dead in his closed coach as the army marched home from Persia. On a hill about 5 km (three miles) outside Nicomedia, a city in north-western Turkey, the troops hailed Diocles as emperor. He swore he was innocent, blamed the dead man's father-in-law, and killed him with his own sword in front of the army. Renamed Diocletian, he defeated the western emperor in 285 near the river Margus in modern Serbia.

## The Tetrarchy: Rule by Four {#tetrarchy}

Diocletian knew no single man could defend an empire stretching from Britain to Syria. In 285 he made his fellow officer Maximian his partner. On 1 April 286 Maximian became a full co-emperor with the title Augustus, ruling the West, while Diocletian remained senior partner in the East. Diocletian styled himself *Iovius*, after Jupiter, king of the gods. Maximian became *Herculius*, after Jupiter's heroic helper Hercules.

In 293 two junior emperors with the title Caesar joined them. Galerius, Diocletian's tough son-in-law, took the East. Constantius, a veteran general married into Maximian's family and father of the future emperor Constantine, took Gaul and Britain. This arrangement is called the Tetrarchy, from a Greek word meaning “rule by four”. When the senior pair retired, the Caesars were to step up.

The emperors also raised themselves above ordinary mortals. Diocletian wore a gold crown and jewels, and visitors had to lie face down before him (*adoratio*). The four ruled from cities near the frontiers, such as Milan and Trier in western Germany, leaving Rome a ceremonial capital.

## Wars on Every Frontier {#wars-and-persia}

The four rulers fought on every border. Diocletian beat the Sarmatians, nomads from the plains beyond the Danube, and lined the river with forts and walled towns. In 297 Egypt rebelled, partly over new taxes, and he besieged Alexandria, its great port, until it fell in 298.

The gravest threat was Persia, ruled by the Sasanian kings, Rome's great eastern rival. In 297 the Persians crushed Galerius, Diocletian's junior emperor in the East, in northern Mesopotamia. Diocletian publicly blamed him, making him walk about 1.5 km (a mile) ahead of the imperial procession in his purple robes.

Galerius struck back in 298 through the mountains of Armenia, where Persian cavalry fought at a disadvantage. He won two great battles, seized the Persian king's treasury and wife, and took Ctesiphon, the Persian capital on the Tigris. The Peace of Nisibis in 299, named after a frontier city that became the only trading point between the empires, brought Armenia back under Roman influence. It proved a lasting peace.

## Bigger Government, New Taxes and the Price Freeze {#reforms-and-prices}

Diocletian built the largest government Rome had ever known. He doubled the provinces, from about fifty to almost a hundred, so that no governor held enough land or soldiers to rebel. They were grouped into twelve regions called dioceses, each run by a deputy (*vicarius*). The army grew from about 390,000 to some 580,000 men.

To pay for it, he turned the old habit of seizing supplies into a regular tax. It was assessed on people, counted in heads (*capita*), and on land, measured in units (*iuga*) that varied with soil and crop. From 296 a census every five years kept the figures current. Italy, long untaxed, now paid too.

Inflation still raged. In late 301 his Edict on Maximum Prices (*Edictum de Pretiis Rerum Venalium*) blamed greedy merchants and set top prices for more than a thousand goods. It ignored supply, demand and transport costs; one modern historian calls it “an act of economic lunacy”. Goods slipped into a black market, and the edict was dropped, perhaps within a year.

## The Great Persecution {#great-persecution}

Diocletian was devoted to Rome's old gods. In 299 at Antioch, priests who read the future in animals' entrails said the signs had failed and blamed Christians at court. Courtiers and soldiers were ordered to sacrifice or be dismissed. According to a Christian teacher living in Nicomedia, Galerius, the junior emperor in the East, pushed for harsher measures than Diocletian wanted.

On 23 February 303 soldiers tore down the new church at Nicomedia and burned its scriptures. The next day an edict ordered churches and holy books destroyed across the empire and banned Christian worship. After fires broke out in the palace, Christians were blamed, tortured and executed.

This Great Persecution was the last, largest and bloodiest attack on Christians by the Roman state, yet it failed. Most Christians escaped punishment and many pagans disliked it. In the West, Maximian and Constantine's father ignored the later edicts. In 311 Galerius, near death, called it off, admitting it had not worked.

## The Emperor Who Retired to Grow Cabbages {#abdication}

In 304 Diocletian fell gravely ill and vanished for months. When he reappeared in March 305 he was barely recognisable.

On 1 May 305, on the same hill outside Nicomedia where he had been proclaimed, he told his troops in tears that he needed rest. He became the first Roman emperor to step down voluntarily, and Maximian resigned the same day in Milan. A Christian writer claimed Galerius bullied him into it, but scholars doubt this. The crowd expected Constantine and Maxentius, Maximian's son, to become Caesars, but two of Galerius's allies were chosen instead, a slight that would wreck the system.

Diocletian retired to a fortified palace on the Dalmatian coast, now the old heart of Split in Croatia. In 308, at Carnuntum, an army town on the Danube near Vienna, he was begged to return. If they could see the cabbages he had planted with his own hands, he replied, they would never ask. He died in 311 or 312 as civil war tore his system apart.

## Constantine Seizes His Chance {#constantine-rise}

Constantine, the emperor who would make Rome Christian, was born around AD 272 at Naissus, now Niš in Serbia. His father became one of the four tetrarchs; his mother, Helena, was a woman of humble birth. As a young man he lived at Diocletian's court, half heir and half hostage for his father's loyalty.

Passed over in 305, he was in danger at Galerius's court. When his father asked for him, Galerius agreed after an evening of drinking. According to Constantine's later propaganda, he fled that night, laming the post horses behind him. He joined his father, and when his father died at York on 25 July 306, the troops proclaimed Constantine Augustus. Galerius grudgingly recognised him only as Caesar.

Three months later Maxentius, Maximian's son, seized Rome. Maximian married his daughter to Constantine in 307, but in 310 he rebelled against his son-in-law, was cornered at Marseille and hanged himself.

## The Battle of the Milvian Bridge {#milvian-bridge}

By 312 Constantine and his brother-in-law Maxentius, ruler of Rome, were at war. Against his advisers' warnings, Constantine crossed the Alps with about 40,000 men and won battles near Turin and at Verona.

Maxentius had twice beaten invaders by holding out behind Rome's walls. This time he came out. The Sibylline Books, ancient prophecies kept in Rome, said that on 28 October “the enemy of the Romans” would die, and he took this as a promise.

He drew up his army with the Tiber at its back, beside the Milvian Bridge, which carried the main road from the north into Rome, and added a temporary bridge of boats. Constantine's cavalry broke his horsemen, and the infantry drove the rest toward the river. The temporary bridge collapsed and Maxentius drowned.

On 29 October Constantine made a grand entry (*adventus*) into Rome. Maxentius's head was paraded through the streets, and his memory was officially condemned (*damnatio memoriae*).

## The Vision: What Really Happened? {#constantine-vision}

The battle became famous for a sign from God that may never have happened. Two Christian writers who knew Constantine told different stories.

Lactantius, a Christian teacher writing soon afterwards, said that the night before the battle, Constantine was told in a dream to paint a heavenly sign of Christ on his soldiers' shields. Eusebius, a bishop who later wrote Constantine's biography, claiming he heard it from the emperor himself, told a grander tale. While marching, Constantine saw a cross of light above the sun with the words “In this sign, conquer”, usually given in Latin as *in hoc signo vinces*. That night Christ told him in a dream to make a battle standard, the *labarum*, bearing the Chi-Rho, the first two Greek letters of Christ's name.

Historians are divided. Some suggest a halo around the sun, noting that his coins honoured the Unconquered Sun for years afterwards. Others think the vision never occurred, though Constantine probably had some deep spiritual experience. The Senate's arch to his victory shows no Christian symbols.

## Constantine, Sole Ruler and Christian Emperor {#constantine-sole-ruler}

In 313 Constantine and his eastern co-emperor, Licinius, agreed the Edict of Milan, granting freedom of worship to all religions and returning property seized from Christians. The alliance soured, and in 324 Constantine defeated his rival and ruled the whole empire alone.

He refounded Byzantium, a Greek city on the strait between Europe and Asia, dedicating it in 330 as Constantinople, “Constantine's City”. It remained a capital for more than a thousand years.

Constantine funded great churches, including the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. In 325 he summoned bishops to Nicaea, a town in north-western Turkey, where they produced the Nicene Creed, a statement of Christian belief. His new gold coin, the *solidus*, kept its weight and purity for seven centuries.

In 326 he had his eldest son, Crispus, and his wife, Fausta, executed, for reasons never explained. Baptised only on his deathbed, he died near Nicomedia on 22 May 337, leaving the empire to his sons and replacing Diocletian's rule of four with rule by family.
