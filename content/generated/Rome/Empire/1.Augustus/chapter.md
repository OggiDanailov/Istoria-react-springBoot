# Empire: Augustus and the Principate (27 BC-AD 14)

## Caesar's Heir {#caesars-heir}

The man who became Rome's first emperor was born Gaius Octavius in 63 BC, into a comfortable but not famous family. His great advantage was his mother, a niece of Julius Caesar, the general who had made himself master of Rome. When Caesar was stabbed to death on the Ides of March (15 March) 44 BC, his will named the 18-year-old as his main heir and adopted son. The young man took the name Caesar. Historians usually call him Octavian for the years from 44 to 27 BC.

He moved with startling boldness. He used Caesar's money and his own to win over the dead dictator's veterans, raised a private army, and in 43 BC marched on Rome, becoming consul at just 19.

He then joined forces with Mark Antony, Caesar's former right-hand man, and a third Caesarian general. The three formed a triumvirate, a three-man board with legal power to rule and to hunt down Caesar's assassins. Their first act was a purge. About 300 senators and wealthy businessmen were declared outlaws, and thousands more lost their property. In 42 BC Antony and Octavian crushed the assassins' armies at Philippi in Macedonia, in northern Greece.

## Actium and the Conquest of Egypt {#actium}

After Philippi the victors carved up the Roman world. Mark Antony took the rich East, where he became the ally and lover of the queen of Egypt. Octavian took the West, including Italy itself.

Octavian was no great general, but he had Agrippa, his loyal friend and gifted commander. In 36 BC Agrippa destroyed the fleet of a renegade admiral whose blockades had starved Italy. The third member of the triumvirate tried to seize Sicily, but his troops deserted him and he was forced into retirement.

That left two men. Octavian waged a fierce propaganda war, accusing Antony of abandoning his Roman wife, Octavian's own sister, for a foreign queen and of giving away Roman lands to her children. In 32 BC the Senate declared war on the Egyptian queen.

The decisive clash came on 2 September 31 BC at Actium, a bay on the western coast of Greece. Agrippa's fleet won, and the queen and then Antony fled. A year later, as Octavian's army reached Alexandria, Antony and the queen both took their own lives. Egypt became Octavian's personal property, and its treasure paid off his war debts. Senators were even forbidden to travel there.

## The First Settlement of 27 BC {#first-settlement}

With no rivals left, Octavian faced a delicate problem. Romans hated the idea of a king, and Julius Caesar had been murdered for looking like one. Yet giving up power might restart the civil wars.

His answer was political theatre. On 13 January 27 BC he announced to the Senate (*senatus*) that he was handing back his provinces and armies. The senators, as expected, begged him to take some of them back. Feigning reluctance, on 16 January he agreed to govern the "troubled" provinces for ten years. These happened to include Spain, Gaul, Syria and Egypt, and with them about twenty legions. The Senate kept only five or six.

The same day the Senate gave him a new name: Augustus (*augustus*), meaning "the revered". It carried a religious glow, hinting that he stood close to the gods. Another suggestion, to call him after Romulus, Rome's legendary founder and first king, was rejected as too royal.

Augustus preferred to be called the leading or first citizen (*princeps*). He refused a crown or sceptre, but a golden shield in the Senate house praised his courage, piety, mercy and justice. The system he created is known as the Principate.

## The Second Settlement and Augustus's Powers {#second-settlement}

By 23 BC the arrangement was showing cracks. Augustus had been elected consul every year, which made his dominance obvious and blocked other ambitious senators. Then he fell gravely ill. He seemed to be dying, but he recovered and, on 1 July 23 BC, gave up the consulship.

In its place the Senate gave him something better. Greater proconsular power (*imperium proconsulare maius*) made him the superior of every governor in the empire. Tribunician power (*tribunicia potestas*), the authority of the ancient tribunes who protected ordinary citizens, was granted for life. It let him summon the Senate and the people's assemblies, propose laws, and veto any decision.

More honours followed. In 12 BC he became chief priest (*pontifex maximus*), head of Rome's state religion. In 2 BC the Senate named him Father of the Fatherland (*pater patriae*).

Augustus himself said he ruled by personal prestige (*auctoritas*) rather than by office. Behind the polite words stood three hard facts: legal powers voted by Senate and people, an enormous private fortune, and a vast network of clients and supporters bound to him by favours.

## Soldiers, Firemen and Tax Collectors {#running-the-empire}

Many of the institutions Augustus created outlived him by centuries. After the civil wars he cut the army from about 60 legions to 28, some 170,000 professional soldiers, backed by auxiliary units of non-citizens. In AD 6 he set up a military treasury (*aerarium militare*), donating 170 million sesterces to pay soldiers and fund their retirement.

In 27 BC he founded the Praetorian Guard, a bodyguard that grew into the emperor's household troops and a powerful force in Roman politics. For the capital he created a combined fire brigade and night police (*vigiles*) in AD 6, with seven units covering fourteen city districts. After food shortages, a permanent official took charge of Rome's grain supply in AD 8.

He also reformed taxes. Under the old Republic, private companies had bought the right to collect taxes and squeezed the provinces mercilessly. Augustus replaced them with salaried collectors, using censuses to fix each province's share. A network of relay stations carried official messages along Italy's roads. In 8 BC the month Sextilis was renamed August in his honour, matching July, named for Julius Caesar.

## A City of Marble {#city-of-marble}

On his deathbed, according to tradition, Augustus boasted that he had found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble. The boast had substance. Marble had been used before, but only under Augustus did it become a standard building material.

He built a new Forum with a Temple of Mars the Avenger, and completed buildings Julius Caesar had left unfinished, including a new Senate house. Near the Field of Mars, a riverside district north of the old centre, rose the Altar of Peace (*Ara Pacis*), its carved panels showing priests, guards and Roman families in procession.

Agrippa, his old friend and admiral, took charge of the water supply, drains, public baths and roads, and built the first Pantheon. Augustus often paid for buildings named after relatives.

He also gave the Romans spectacle. Rome's first stone amphitheatre opened in his time, and in 2 BC he staged a mock naval battle on an artificial lake fed by a new aqueduct more than 30 km (twenty miles) long, recreating a famous Greek sea victory over Persia.

## Conquest and the Roman Peace {#expansion-and-peace}

Augustus called his peace one "born of victories". His armies completed the conquest of Spain, took the Alps and pushed Rome's frontier to the Danube river. By AD 13 his troops had hailed him as victorious general (*imperator*) 21 times.

In the East he relied more on diplomacy. The Parthians, a rival empire in what is now Iran and Iraq, held legionary standards captured in a humiliating Roman defeat a generation earlier. In 20 BC Augustus negotiated their return without a fight. Some Romans who wanted revenge were disappointed, but he presented it as Parthia bowing to Rome, and the standards were housed in the Temple of Mars the Avenger.

On Egypt's southern border, the queen of Kush, a kingdom in modern Sudan, raided Roman territory. After Roman counterattacks she sent envoys, and a treaty fixed a new border and guaranteed trade.

Inside the empire, major wars largely ceased. This era of calm became known as the Roman Peace (*Pax Romana*), and it lasted, with interruptions, for about two centuries.

## Disaster in the Teutoburg Forest {#teutoburg-forest}

Augustus's expansion met its limit in Germany, the lands beyond the Rhine. By AD 9 Roman control seemed secure, and the governor there was a politician rather than a soldier.

His trusted adviser was a Germanic chieftain of the Cherusci tribe who had been raised as a hostage in Rome and served as a Roman officer. Secretly he built an alliance of tribes. He fed the governor false reports of a revolt and persuaded him to march three legions home by an unfamiliar route.

Between 8 and 11 September AD 9, in thick forest and pouring rain, the Germanic warriors struck. The Roman column, strung out over 15 to 20 km (9 to 12 miles), was attacked again and again. Near Kalkriese, in northern Germany, the survivors were trapped in a gap only about 100 metres (330 feet) wide between a hill and a bog, behind a hidden earthen wall. Between 15,000 and 20,000 Romans died; the governor killed himself.

The Romans called it the Varian Disaster (*Clades Variana*). Augustus, it was said, banged his head against the palace walls, crying for the dead commander to give him back his legions. Rome abandoned conquest beyond the Rhine.

## The Search for an Heir {#succession-and-death}

Augustus had no son, and naming an heir openly would have looked like founding a monarchy. His solution was to promote relatives quietly.

His nephew, married to Augustus's only child Julia, died young in 23 BC. Julia was then married to Agrippa, the emperor's loyal general, who received powers close to Augustus's own. Augustus adopted their sons Gaius and Lucius, but Agrippa died in 12 BC, and both boys died young, in AD 2 and AD 4.

That left Tiberius, the elder son of Augustus's wife from her first marriage, a hard-working general who had won victories in Germany and the Balkans. In AD 4 Augustus adopted him, and by AD 13 Tiberius held powers equal to his own.

Augustus died at Nola, near Naples, on 19 August AD 14, aged 75. Two ancient historians claimed his wife poisoned him, perhaps with a fig, but many modern historians dismiss this as slander. His last words were reportedly "The play is over; applaud" (*Acta est fabula, plaudite*). The Senate declared him a god, and his own account of his deeds (*Res Gestae*) was inscribed in bronze before his tomb.

## Hero or Tyrant? The Historians' Verdict {#verdicts}

Romans argued about Augustus from the start. A legal scholar of his own day openly mourned the lost liberty of the Republic. A great Roman historian, writing a century later, accused him of cunningly turning free Romans into slaves, and Tiberius's succession of simply handing them to a new master. Later Romans were kinder: the Senate wished each new emperor to be "more fortunate than Augustus".

In the Middle Ages Christians saw him as a God-sent peacemaker whose reign prepared the world for Christ. A 19th-century German scholar described the Principate as a genuine partnership between the emperor and the Senate. Most modern historians reject that, calling it monarchy in disguise.

A famous British historian, writing in 1939 as fascist dictators borrowed Roman imagery, painted the young Octavian as "sickly and sinister" and his faction as something like a crime gang. More recent writers agree he was in effect a military dictator, but say comparisons with modern tyrants are misleading. Few deny his legacy: a professional army, a dynasty, a rebuilt capital, and titles such as Caesar that survived as kaiser and czar.
