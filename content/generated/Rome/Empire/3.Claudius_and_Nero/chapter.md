# Empire: Claudius and Nero (AD 41-68)

## The Emperor Nobody Expected {#claudius-early-life}

Claudius was born in 10 BC at Lugdunum, modern Lyon in France, the first Roman emperor born outside Italy. He belonged to the very top of Rome's ruling family, but as a child he suffered an illness that left him with a limp, a stammer and a shaking head. His father, a celebrated general, died on campaign in Germany when the boy was one.

His family treated him with contempt. His mother called him a monster and used him as her yardstick for stupidity. A former mule-driver was hired to discipline him, on the theory that his condition came from laziness.

Yet Claudius had a sharp mind. Livy, Rome's most famous historian, tutored him, and he became a serious scholar. He wrote twenty books on the Etruscans, the people who had ruled much of Italy before Rome, eight on Carthage and even one on dice. His early history of Rome's civil wars was too frank for his family, who stopped it and kept him out of public life.

Only in AD 37 did he hold a top office, serving as consul (*consul*), one of the two highest magistrates, alongside his nephew, the reigning emperor, who mocked and humiliated him. Claudius later claimed he had exaggerated his ailments to stay alive. It may have worked: in an age of purges, nobody saw him as a threat. Modern doctors have guessed at polio, cerebral palsy or Tourette syndrome.

## Found Behind a Curtain {#claudius-accession}

On 24 January AD 41, Claudius's nephew, the emperor, was murdered by officers of the Praetorian Guard (*cohortes praetoriae*), the elite bodyguard stationed in Rome, together with several senators. The killers then murdered the dead emperor's wife and daughter, and seemed ready to wipe out the whole imperial family.

In the chaos, German bodyguards cut down innocent nobles, including friends of Claudius. He fled into the palace and hid. According to tradition, a guardsman found him cowering behind a curtain and, instead of killing him, hailed him as emperor. Claudius, now the last adult man of his family, was hurried off to the guards' camp.

Meanwhile the Senate (*senatus*), Rome's council of elder statesmen, debated a change of government but fell to arguing over which of its members should rule. It demanded that Claudius come before it for approval. He wisely refused, and the senators were forced to accept him.

Claudius pardoned most people involved but executed the actual assassins, partly for his own safety. He rewarded each guardsman with 15,000 sesterces. Critics called this the first time an emperor had bought the army's loyalty, although earlier emperors had left similar gifts in their wills. Claudius was the first emperor made by the soldiers rather than the Senate, and his reputation never fully recovered from it.

## Claudius at Work {#claudius-government}

Despite his inexperience, Claudius proved an able ruler. He tried hard to please the Senate, sitting among the senators and scolding them for simply saying "I approve" instead of debating. In AD 47 he revived the old office of censor (*censor*), who kept the lists of citizens and senators. He used it to admit nobles from Gaul into the Senate, a speech still preserved on a bronze plaque found at Lyon. His census of AD 48 counted 5,984,072 adult male citizens, about a million more than a generation before.

To run the empire day to day, he relied on freedmen (*liberti*), former slaves, who headed separate offices for letters, finance and justice. Senators were appalled and whispered that the freedmen ruled their master. In fact his policies stayed consistent whoever was in favour.

Claudius loved law and judged many cases himself. One famous ruling freed sick slaves whom masters had abandoned to die on an island in the Tiber, if they recovered.

He was also a great builder. Two new aqueducts brought water into Rome in AD 52. To end winter grain shortages, he built a new harbour north of Ostia, Rome's port, with a lighthouse, and insured merchants who risked winter voyages to Egypt. One project failed spectacularly: a tunnel to drain the Fucine Lake in central Italy was too small, and when opened its flood swamped a gladiator show, sending the emperor running for his life.

## The Conquest of Britain {#conquest-of-britain}

Under Claudius the empire made its first major conquests in decades, and the greatest was Britain. In AD 43, after a British ally driven from his lands appealed for help, Claudius sent four legions (*legiones*), Rome's heavy infantry divisions of several thousand men each, across the Channel. The island tempted Rome with its mines and potential slaves, and it had sheltered rebels from Gaul.

Once the first fighting was over, Claudius crossed over himself with reinforcements. Ancient writers claimed he brought elephants, a story modern historians doubt. He spent just sixteen days on the island. Camulodunum, modern Colchester, became a colony (*colonia*), a settlement of retired soldiers, and the capital of the new province, with a great temple dedicated to Claudius.

Back in Rome, the Senate voted him a triumph (*triumphus*), the grand victory parade through the city. He was offered the title "Britannicus", conqueror of Britain, but took it only for his baby son, who became known by that name. When Caratacus, a leading British war chief, was captured in AD 50, Claudius spared him, and he lived out his life on land provided by Rome, a rare mercy for a defeated enemy. The conquest also left a legacy of resentment that would explode under Claudius's successor.

## Wives, Heirs and a Poisoned Dish {#claudius-wives-and-death}

Claudius married four times, and ancient writers mocked him as a man ruled by women. His third wife, Messalina, a young cousin, was portrayed by later historians as wildly unfaithful. In AD 48, while Claudius was away at Ostia, she publicly married her lover, a senator. Whether this was a plot to seize the throne is still debated. She was executed.

Claudius then married his own niece, Agrippina, a great-granddaughter of the first emperor. Ancient writers said she won him with her charms. Modern historians see politics: Claudius needed the prestige of her bloodline, and his son Britannicus was still a child. In AD 50 he adopted Agrippina's son, renamed him Nero, married him to his daughter and made him joint heir.

Claudius died on 13 October AD 54, aged 63. Nearly all ancient writers blamed Agrippina, saying she poisoned him with mushrooms, or with a feather pushed down his throat to make him vomit. Her motive, they said, was that Claudius had begun talking about his own son coming of age. Some modern historians doubt the murder. Claudius had been seriously ill, his food taster kept his job under Nero, and AD 54 was an unhealthy year in Rome.

Nero and the Senate declared Claudius a god. Yet Nero's tutor, the philosopher Seneca, soon wrote a cruel satire turning him into a figure of fun.

## Nero's Promising Start and His Mother's Murder {#nero-early-reign}

Nero was born Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus on 15 December AD 37 at Antium, a seaside town south of Rome. Through his mother, Agrippina, he descended from the first emperor. When his stepfather Claudius died in AD 54, the Praetorian Guard and the Senate backed the 16-year-old as emperor.

His first speech, written by his tutor Seneca, a Stoic philosopher, promised respect for the Senate and an end to secret trials and powerful palace favourites. Agrippina meant to rule through her son, and her portrait even appeared on his coins in the place normally reserved for the emperor. But Nero's tutor and the commander of the guard steered government, and these years were later remembered as "Nero's five good years" (*quinquennium Neronis*). Tax collectors were placed under stricter control, and slaves were allowed to complain about mistreatment.

The family was deadly. In AD 55 Claudius's son Britannicus was poisoned after Agrippina threatened to back him. In AD 59 Nero had his mother killed: according to ancient writers, a freedman arranged a shipwreck, and when she swam ashore he murdered her and called it suicide. Tacitus, the senator-historian whose Annals give the fullest account of Nero, blamed Nero's mistress. Modern historians doubt this, since he married her only three years later.

After his mother's death, historians agree, Nero's conduct grew far worse. Treason trials (*maiestas*) returned, and his first wife was exiled and executed.

## Boudica's Revolt {#boudica-revolt}

Boudica was queen of the Iceni, a British tribe in what is now Norfolk in eastern England. Her husband, the king, ruled as a Roman ally and left his kingdom jointly to their two daughters and the emperor Nero. When he died, in AD 60 or 61, Rome ignored his will and seized the kingdom. According to Tacitus, one of two ancient historians who described the revolt, Boudica was flogged and her daughters raped. Another ancient writer blamed Roman loans suddenly called in.

Boudica led the Iceni and their neighbours in revolt. Their first target was Camulodunum, modern Colchester, a colony of Roman veterans hated for its expensive temple to the god-emperor Claudius. Survivors held out inside that temple for two days before being killed, and the town was burned to the ground. A Roman relief force was wiped out.

The governor hurried back from a campaign on the island of Anglesey in Wales, but he was too weak to save Londinium, a trading town just twenty years old. He abandoned it, and the rebels burned it, then Verulamium, modern St Albans. Ancient writers put the dead at 70,000 to 80,000.

The governor gathered almost 10,000 men in a narrow pass with woods behind them. Heavily outnumbered, the Romans hurled javelins, charged in wedge formation and crushed the Britons. Boudica died soon after, by poison according to Tacitus, by illness according to another source. The shock made Nero consider abandoning Britain. Later centuries made her a national heroine.

## The Great Fire of AD 64 {#great-fire-of-rome}

On the night of 18 to 19 July AD 64, a fire broke out near the Circus Maximus, Rome's huge chariot-racing stadium, probably in shops full of flammable goods. Fanned by wind, it burned for more than seven days, died down, then flared up for three more. Three of the city's fourteen districts were destroyed and seven more badly damaged.

Rumours spread that Nero had started it. Later writers claimed he wanted to clear land for his new palace, the Golden House (*Domus Aurea*), with landscaped parks and a statue of himself about 30 m tall. They said he sang about the fall of Troy, dressed for the stage, while Rome burned. The famous image of Nero fiddling is probably at least partly propaganda by his successors.

Tacitus, the senator-historian who wrote the fullest account, left the question open. He noted that Nero was at Antium, on the coast, when the fire began. Nero returned, paid to clear bodies and rubble, opened his palaces to the homeless and brought in food.

To deflect suspicion, Tacitus says, Nero blamed the Christians. Many were thrown to wild beasts, crucified or burned alive, out of cruelty rather than justice. Later Christian writers called Nero the first persecutor of their faith and placed the deaths of the apostles Peter and Paul in his reign.

Rome was rebuilt with brick houses on wider streets. To pay for it, Nero raised taxes and debased the coinage.

## The Artist Emperor's Fall {#nero-downfall}

Nero loved the arts. He sang, played the cithara, a kind of large lyre, acted in tragedies and drove chariots, all activities Romans associated with slaves and entertainers. The aristocracy was scandalised, but ordinary people loved his shows. In AD 67 he competed at the Olympic Games in Greece and won every contest, including a chariot race in which he fell out and never finished.

At home, plots multiplied. In AD 65 a conspiracy of senators and guard officers was betrayed, and its members were executed. Nero's old tutor Seneca was ordered to kill himself.

In March AD 68 the governor of part of Gaul rebelled against Nero's taxes and urged the governor of Spain to claim the throne. The rebellion in Gaul was crushed, but support for the governor of Spain grew, and even the commander of the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's bodyguard, abandoned Nero. The Senate declared Nero a public enemy (*hostis publicus*).

Nero woke at midnight to find his guards and friends gone. He fled in disguise to a freedman's villa about 6 km (four miles) outside Rome. There, ancient writers say, he muttered "What an artist the world is losing!" As horsemen approached, he stabbed himself in the throat with his secretary's help. He died on 9 June AD 68, aged 30. His dynasty died with him, and civil war followed. In the eastern provinces many believed he would return, and at least three impostors claimed to be Nero.

## Can We Trust the Ancient Historians? {#judging-the-sources}

Almost everything we know about Claudius and Nero comes from three writers: Tacitus, the senator-historian, a biographer of the emperors and a later Greek senator. All came from Rome's upper classes. Tacitus and the biographer wrote more than fifty years after Nero's death, the Greek senator more than 150. They usually took the Senate's side against the emperor.

Claudius suffered badly. His own books are lost, and his chief secretary burned his letters when Agrippina had him killed. The historians painted Claudius as a fool steered by wives and freedmen. Modern historians have revised that view and see an able, hard-working administrator.

Nero is harder to rescue. The three writers contradict one another on key events, such as the deaths of Claudius and Agrippina and the Great Fire, but agree in condemning him. Tacitus himself admitted that histories of these emperors were falsified out of fear while they lived and written in fresh hatred after they died. A Jewish historian of the time complained that some writers flattered Nero while others raved against him with lies.

There is also evidence of real popularity. Ordinary Romans mourned him, and two of his short-lived successors courted his memory. After his death many portraits were reworked or defaced, part of the condemnation of a ruler's memory (*damnatio memoriae*). Yet people went on making images of him. Some modern historians therefore doubt how tyrannical Nero really was.
