# Empire: The Year of Four Emperors and the Flavians (AD 69-96)

## The End of the First Dynasty {#fall-of-nero}

For almost a century Rome had been ruled by one family, the Julio-Claudians, heirs of the first emperor. By AD 68 the last of them, Nero, had exhausted the patience of Rome's elite with treason trials and plots.

In March of that year a governor in Gaul, a man of humble origins who did not want the throne himself, raised an army of Gallic warriors and went looking for a candidate. He found one in Galba, the elderly and childless governor of Hispania Tarraconensis, the largest province in Spain. According to an ancient biographer, Galba was the only official who did not forward the rebel's letters to the emperor. In early April 68 his troops proclaimed him emperor.

The Gallic rising was crushed by the legions (*legiones*) of the Rhine, and its leader killed himself. But in Rome the emperor's bodyguard, the Praetorian Guard (*cohortes praetoriae*), deserted him after one of its commanders promised each man 30,000 sesterces in Galba's name, roughly ten years' pay. On 8 June the Senate (*senatus*) declared Nero a public enemy (*hostis*), and the next day he took his own life. The first civil war of the Roman Empire had begun.

## Galba: The Emperor Who Made Enemies {#galba}

Galba, the elderly governor of Spain proclaimed emperor in 68, took months to reach Rome and made enemies at every step. A Roman historian called his journey "a long and bloody march". He killed officials who had opposed him, and outside the city his troops charged a new legion of former sailors who demanded he confirm their status, killing thousands. The survivors suffered decimation (*decimatio*), the ancient punishment in which one soldier in ten was put to death.

Galba also refused to pay the Praetorian Guard the reward promised in his name, and he trusted only a small circle of greedy favourites from Spain.

On 1 January 69 the legions of the lower Rhine refused to swear loyalty to him, and the next day they proclaimed their own governor, Vitellius, emperor. Panicking, Galba adopted a young senator as his heir. That choice was fatal. Otho, an ambitious nobleman who had expected the honour, bribed the Guard, and on 15 January 69 soldiers killed Galba and his heir in the Forum, Rome's great public square, and stuck their heads on poles.

## Otho: The Three-Month Emperor {#the-nobleman-emperor}

The second of the four emperors of AD 69 was Otho, an ambitious nobleman and governor of Lusitania, roughly modern Portugal. He had been one of Galba's first supporters and fully expected to be named heir to the old, childless emperor. When Galba chose a young senator instead, Otho bribed the Praetorian Guard, which murdered Galba on 15 January. The same day the Senate recognised him as emperor.

Senators greeted him with relief. He was greedy but had no record of cruelty, and he quickly won popularity by handing out favours. Yet Vitellius, governor of the lower Rhine, had already been proclaimed emperor by the Rhine legions, the finest veteran troops in the empire, and half of that army was marching on Italy.

Otho offered peace, even offering to marry Vitellius's daughter, but it was too late. On 14 April 69 his army was beaten at the First Battle of Bedriacum, near Cremona in northern Italy. Rather than drag out the war, he took his own life soon afterwards. He had reigned for little more than three months.

## Vitellius: The Glutton on the Throne {#vitellius}

Vitellius owed everything to the legions of the Rhine. When Galba sent him to govern the lower Rhine in late 68, he was best known as a penniless glutton. Yet on 2 January 69 his soldiers, who hated Galba, proclaimed him emperor. After his generals defeated the emperor in Rome at Bedriacum in April, the Senate recognised Vitellius.

His reign began under a cloud. He took the office of chief priest (*Pontifex Maximus*) on the anniversary of the Battle of the Allia, a disaster of 390 BC that Romans considered an unlucky day. Ancient writers describe what followed as an orgy of feasting: one biographer claims he held three banquets a day. Feasts and parades drove the treasury close to bankruptcy, and he reportedly had moneylenders who asked for repayment tortured and killed. He is also said to have lured rivals to the palace with promises of power, only to have them murdered.

Beyond his veterans from Germany he had little support. When the eastern armies declared for Vespasian, his position collapsed. On 20 December 69 Vespasian's soldiers caught him in the palace and killed him.

## Vespasian Wins the Civil War {#vespasian-wins}

Vespasian, the general who would end the civil war and found the Flavian dynasty, came from a family that had climbed from obscurity in four generations. His grandfather had been a centurion, an officer commanding about eighty men. Vespasian fought in the invasion of Britain in AD 43, rose to consul, Rome's highest regular office, and in 67 was sent to crush the Jewish revolt in Judaea, the Roman province around Jerusalem.

With four legions, nearly 80,000 men, and Egypt, the source of Rome's grain, within reach, he was a formidable contender. On 1 July 69 he was proclaimed emperor in Alexandria, Egypt's great port. The legions of the Danube moved first on his behalf, invading Italy and crushing Vitellius's army at the Second Battle of Bedriacum on 24 October.

Rome itself saw savage fighting. Vespasian's brother, commander of the city garrison, was besieged on the Capitoline Hill and executed, and the temple of Jupiter, Rome's holiest shrine, burned. Vespasian's younger son Domitian escaped disguised as a worshipper of the goddess Isis. Vitellius died on 20 December, and the next day the Senate proclaimed Vespasian emperor.

## Vespasian Rebuilds Rome {#vespasian-reign}

Vespasian, the general who emerged victorious from the civil war of 69, reached Rome in 70 to find an empty treasury. He raised the tribute paid by the provinces, revived old taxes and invented new ones, including a tax on urine collected from public toilets. Tradition links this tax to the famous saying "money does not smell" (*pecunia non olet*).

He rewarded loyal soldiers, punished Vitellius's supporters, and packed the Senate with allies. Power stayed in the family: his elder son Titus held seven consulships and commanded the Praetorian Guard, effectively ruling beside him.

Unable to claim descent from the first emperors, Vespasian launched a propaganda campaign presenting his rule as willed by the gods and himself as a bringer of peace. Nearly a third of the coins minted in Rome celebrated victory or peace. He built a Temple of Peace (*Templum Pacis*) and began the Flavian Amphitheatre (*Amphitheatrum Flavium*), better known as the Colosseum. He was remembered for mildness; "I will not kill a dog that barks at me," he reportedly said. He died of natural causes on 23 June 79.

## Titus and the Fall of Jerusalem {#siege-of-jerusalem}

The most dramatic campaign of the Flavian era was the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70. When Vespasian left to claim the throne, his elder son Titus took over the war against the Jewish revolt that had broken out in 66. He led at least 48,200 soldiers against a city crowded with refugees and Passover pilgrims, where three rival rebel factions had fought each other and burned their own food stores.

The Romans broke through the walls one by one and sealed the city to starve it. Famine killed many, and captured fugitives were crucified in sight of the defenders. In the summer the Second Temple, the centre of Jewish worship, burned. Josephus, a Jewish commander turned historian, watching from the Roman side, claimed Titus tried to save it. Most modern scholars doubt this. Some suspect he was protecting Titus's reputation; several see the destruction as deliberate.

The city was then razed. That historian's figure of 1.1 million dead is considered greatly exaggerated. In 71 Titus and Vespasian celebrated a triumph (*triumphus*), a victory parade through Rome, displaying the Temple's seven-branched lampstand. Jews still mourn the destruction each year on the fast of Tisha B'Av.

## Titus: A Short Reign of Disasters {#titus}

Titus, Vespasian's elder son and the conqueror of Jerusalem, became emperor in June 79. Despite earlier worries about his character, Roman writers remembered him as one of the best emperors, while Jewish tradition calls him "Titus the Wicked".

Barely two months into his reign, on 24 August 79, Vesuvius, the volcano above the Bay of Naples, erupted. Pompeii and Herculaneum were buried under metres of stone and ash, and thousands died. Titus appointed two former consuls to lead the relief effort, gave large sums from the treasury and visited twice. Pompeii stayed lost for nearly 1,700 years until it was rediscovered in 1748.

In 80 a three-day fire swept Rome, destroying the rebuilt temple of Jupiter and the Pantheon, and according to one ancient biographer a plague struck at the same time. Again Titus paid for repairs. That year he completed the Colosseum, opening it with games lasting a hundred days, with gladiators, animal fights and mock sea battles in the flooded arena. He made his dead father a god (*divus*). After barely two years he died of fever on 13 September 81.

## Domitian: The Last Flavian {#domitian}

Domitian, Vespasian's younger son, who had escaped Rome disguised as a worshipper of Isis in 69, became emperor the day after his brother Titus died in 81. He ruled for fifteen years and dropped all pretence, governing openly as a monarch from his court and sidelining the Senate.

He raised the silver content of the denarius, Rome's standard silver coin, by 12%, and built or restored around fifty structures, including a vast palace on the Palatine Hill. He added a fourth level to the Colosseum. On the frontiers he preferred defence, lining the Rhine with roads, forts and watchtowers. Against the Dacians, a people north of the Danube in modern Romania, he lost a legion and later made a peace that critics called disgraceful.

In 89 a Rhine governor rebelled; the revolt was crushed within 24 days. Executions of senators followed, and on 18 September 96 court officials assassinated him, ending the dynasty. The Senate condemned his memory (*damnatio memoriae*), and senatorial historians portrayed a cruel, paranoid tyrant. Modern historians see instead a ruthless but efficient ruler who laid the foundations for the peaceful 2nd century.
