# Empire: The Severans and the Crisis of the Third Century (AD 193-284)

## Septimius Severus Seizes the Empire {#septimius-severus}

When the last emperor of the previous dynasty was murdered, Rome plunged into the Year of the Five Emperors, a scramble for the throne in 193 that grew into four years of civil war. The winner was Septimius Severus, the general from Africa who founded a new dynasty.

Severus was born in 145 at Leptis Magna, a rich port on the coast of modern Libya. His family belonged to the equestrian order, the propertied class just below senators, and had Libyan, Punic and Italian roots. In 193 his legions in Noricum, a frontier province roughly in modern Austria, proclaimed him emperor. His last rival fell at Lugdunum, modern Lyon, in 197.

Severus then beat the Parthians, the rulers of Persia and Mesopotamia, and from 208 to 210 he campaigned in Britain and rebuilt Hadrian's Wall. The Senate (*senatus*) disliked him, but ordinary Romans and the army loved him, not least because he raised soldiers' pay. He died of natural causes in Britain in 211.

## Julia Domna and the Syrian Women {#julia-domna}

Behind the Severan emperors stood a remarkable group of women. The first was Julia Domna, a Syrian noblewoman who married Septimius Severus in 187 and bore him two sons. She held a prominent political role, carried the title of empress (*Augusta*), and tried to keep peace between her quarrelling boys.

When her elder son Caracalla ruled and went on campaign, Julia Domna travelled with him and handled much of the official correspondence herself, passing only major questions to the emperor.

Her family's power outlived her. Her wealthy sister and that sister's two daughters were also mighty empresses. After the dynasty briefly lost the throne in 217, these women used money and family claims to win over the troops and place two of Julia Domna's great-nephews on the throne in turn. Decades later, the Syrian queen Zenobia would model her coin portraits on these Severan empresses.

## Caracalla: Murder, Citizenship and Baths {#caracalla}

Caracalla, the elder son of Septimius Severus and Julia Domna, was born in 188 and named after the hooded Gallic tunic he liked to wear. His father made him co-emperor, and later raised his younger brother too. The brothers loathed each other.

Soon after their father died in 211, Caracalla had his brother, Geta, killed in their mother's apartments; the young man died clinging to Julia Domna. Caracalla bought the army's goodwill with lavish pay rises and purged his brother's supporters.

Obsessed with the Macedonian conqueror Alexander, he formed a phalanx, a block of spearmen in the old Macedonian style, and executed people who mocked him. For reasons still unclear, he granted Roman citizenship (*civitas*) to every free person in the empire. His great public baths in Rome still stand.

In April 217, heading to fight the Parthians, he was murdered near Carrhae, a town in modern south-eastern Turkey, on the orders of one of his own guard commanders.

## The Guard Commander Who Became Emperor {#praetorian-emperor}

The man behind Caracalla's murder in 217 was his praetorian prefect (*praefectus praetorio*), commander of the Praetorian Guard (*cohortes praetoriae*), the emperor's elite bodyguard. Under the Severans this post grew into a powerful advisory office, and its holders became dangerous.

The conspirator, Macrinus, born into a Moorish family in North Africa, became the first emperor not from a senatorial family. He made peace with the Parthians by paying compensation, terms his soldiers found humiliating, and angered them further by paying new recruits less than veterans. He never set foot in Rome.

In May 218, troops in Syria, won over by Julia Domna's relatives, hailed a teenage member of her family as emperor. The usurper was defeated near Antioch, the great city of Roman Syria, caught as he fled, and killed by his guards.

## Two Boy Emperors {#last-severans}

The Severans returned in 218 through two cousins, great-nephews of Julia Domna, placed on the throne by her sister, their grandmother.

The first, a 14-year-old priest of the Syrian sun god Elagabal, is remembered by his god's name, as Elagabalus. According to one account, his grandmother persuaded a legion to rebel by claiming he was Caracalla's illegitimate son. Coins and inscriptions confirm that in 220 he placed his god above Jupiter, and that he married a Vestal Virgin (*virgo Vestalis*), a priestess sworn to chastity. Ancient writers called him depraved and bloodthirsty, but modern historians think most such tales are invented. He tried to kill his cousin and heir, the troops turned on him, and in 222 his body was thrown into a sewer.

The cousin, Severus Alexander, ruled from about 14 under his mother's guidance. In 235, wintering on the Rhine, he paid off German raiders instead of punishing them, and his soldiers murdered him and his mother.

## The Age of the Barracks Emperors {#barracks-emperors}

The murder of the last Severan emperor in 235 opened the Crisis of the Third Century, half a century in which the empire nearly collapsed.

The army's new emperor, Maximinus Thrax, a legion commander of peasant origin, was the first of the barracks emperors: men raised by soldiers without political experience, noble ancestors or hereditary claim. In 238, the Year of the Six Emperors, he was killed by his own troops amid revolts backed by the Senate.

Rome had never fixed rules of succession, so any general with a big army could try his luck. About 26 men won the Senate's recognition as emperor, and over 50 claimed the title. All but one recognised emperor died violently. Goths and other peoples raided across the Rhine and Danube, and in 260 the emperor Valerian was captured by the Sasanians, a new Persian dynasty, and died a prisoner.

## Debased Coins, Plague and Broken Trade {#money-plague-trade}

Loyal soldiers were expensive. The Severans enlarged the army by a quarter and doubled legionaries' pay; Septimius Severus cut the silver in the denarius, Rome's standard coin, from 81.5% to 78.5%. Later emperors, each needing a quick bonus (*donativum*) for the troops, mixed in ever more bronze and copper. Prices spiralled, and by the century's end the denarius was nearly worthless.

The Plague of Cyprian, possibly smallpox, raged from 249 to 262 and may have cut Alexandria's population by 62%. Farms were abandoned, and harsher weather hurt harvests.

As roads became unsafe, long-distance trade broke down. Many townspeople and small farmers traded their freedom for a landowner's protection, becoming tenant farmers bound to the soil (*coloni*), an early model for medieval serfdom. Even Rome built new walls. Yet historians stress that Egypt, Africa and Spain seem to have prospered.

## Zenobia, Queen of Palmyra {#zenobia}

By 268 the empire had split in three. Gaul, Britain and briefly Spain formed a breakaway Gallic Empire. In the east, power passed to Palmyra, a rich caravan city in the Syrian desert.

Palmyra's lord, Odaenathus, had become Rome's champion after 260, beating the Persians and governing the east. When he was assassinated in 267, his widow Zenobia, then in her late twenties, took power as regent for their ten-year-old son. She ran a cultured court of philosophers and tolerated Christians, Jews and other faiths.

In 270, while Rome fought the Goths, her general seized Arabia and then Egypt. By mid-271 her realm ran from Ancyra, modern Ankara, to Upper Egypt. At first she minted coins showing the Roman emperor beside her son. But in 272, with a Roman army approaching, she declared herself empress, an open break with Rome.

## Aurelian Restores the Empire {#aurelian}

The turning point came in 268 or 269, when an emperor crushed a Gothic invasion at Naissus, modern Niš in Serbia. His cavalry commander there, Aurelian, took the throne in 270.

Aurelian, the tough soldier-emperor who reunited Rome, marched east against Zenobia in 272. He beat her army at Immae, about 40 km (25 miles) from Antioch, then near Emesa, modern Homs, where her 70,000 men nearly won before breaking ranks. She fled toward Persia, by one account on a camel faster than any horse, and was captured. Palmyra surrendered in August 272.

Aurelian then destroyed the Gallic Empire, so by late 274 the empire was whole, and he reformed the coinage. Zenobia was most likely paraded in his triumph (*triumphus*), the victory parade through Rome, and later writers said she retired to a villa at Tibur, modern Tivoli. Aurelian was assassinated in 275.

## Hostile Sources and the End of an Era {#sources-and-legacy}

Much of our evidence comes from biased writers. The Historia Augusta, a late Roman collection of imperial biographies, invented events and letters, and reshaped Zenobia to suit whichever emperor it praised. Tales of her cowardice in defeat were probably Aurelian's propaganda.

The crisis ended with Diocletian, a soldier-emperor who seized power in 284 and in 293 set up the Tetrarchy, a team of four co-emperors. The first emperors had posed as 'leading citizen' (*princeps*); rulers now called themselves 'lords' (*domini*).

Historians see the crisis as the hinge between classical antiquity and late antiquity. Zenobia, meanwhile, became a legend and is today a patriotic symbol in Syria.
