# Empire: The Five Good Emperors (AD 96-192)

## Nerva and the Adoption of Trajan {#nerva-adopts-trajan}

In September AD 96 Rome had a new emperor, Nerva, an elderly and childless senator who came to power after his predecessor was murdered. Nerva had little standing with the soldiers. Members of the Praetorian Guard (*cohortes praetoriae*), the elite bodyguard stationed in Rome, rose against him, and their commander forced him to execute the men who had killed the previous emperor.

Nerva needed the army on his side. In the summer of 97 he found a solution: he adopted a popular general as his son and heir. That general was Trajan, born in AD 53 (or perhaps 56) in Italica, a Roman town near modern Seville in southern Spain that had been settled by Italian veterans. Trajan's father had been a senator and commander, and Trajan himself had won fame fighting Germanic tribes on the Rhine.

Nerva claimed the choice rested purely on Trajan's military merit. Hints in writings of the time suggest the adoption was in fact forced on him. When Nerva died on 28 January 98, Trajan took power without trouble, but he lingered to inspect the Rhine and Danube frontiers before going to Rome. He first summoned the Guard commander who had defied Nerva and had him put to death. Some historians see his accession as less an orderly succession than a successful coup.

## Trajan, 'the Best Ruler' {#trajan-best-ruler}

When Trajan, the soldier-emperor from Spain adopted by Nerva, finally entered Rome in 99, he did so with notable modesty. He gave cash to the common people, though he halved the customary gift to the troops.

Trajan made a show of respecting the Senate, Rome's ancient council of leading men, and of being reluctant to hold power. The senators rewarded him with the title 'the best ruler' (*optimus princeps*), which appears on coins from 105. He was the only emperor officially given it. Yet a senator who praised him admitted that everything depended on the will of a single man.

At home Trajan was a generous builder and benefactor. He formalised the *alimenta*, a welfare scheme giving cash, food and schooling to orphans and poor children across Italy. Using loot from his wars, he built Rome's largest forum, the Forum of Trajan (*Forum Traiani*), with an open square about 120 m by 90 m (roughly 390 by 300 feet), a huge hall, libraries and a market. It was dedicated on 1 January 112. He also rebuilt the Circus Maximus, Rome's chariot-racing stadium, replacing wooden seats with stone and adding about 5,000 places.

When a governor in north-west Turkey asked how to treat Christians, Trajan replied that they should not be hunted down or condemned on anonymous accusations, but those who confessed and refused to give up their faith should be punished.

## The Conquest of Dacia {#dacian-wars}

Dacia, a powerful kingdom north of the lower Danube in what is now Romania, had troubled Rome for years. Under an earlier peace deal its king was recognised as a client king (*rex amicus*) and received Roman money and technical experts, an arrangement senators saw as shameful tribute. Unlike the loose Germanic tribes, Dacia was an organised state, rich in iron and gold.

Trajan attacked in 101 and won a costly battle at Tapae, a pass into the Dacian heartland. By 102 the Dacian king, Decebalus, had submitted, but he soon rearmed. In 105 Trajan returned, crossing a great stone bridge over the Danube designed by his favourite architect, Apollodorus, a Greek from Damascus. In 106 the Romans cut the water pipes of the Dacian capital, Sarmizegetusa, and the city fell. The king fled and killed himself when Roman cavalry cornered him; his severed head was displayed in Rome.

A captured nobleman revealed that the royal treasure had been buried beneath a temporarily diverted river. One modern estimate puts it at about 165,500 kg of gold and 330,000 kg of silver. Dacia became a Roman province, its gold mines worked for Rome. Trajan celebrated a triumph (*triumphus*) and 123 days of games with 10,000 gladiators. In 113 he raised Trajan's Column, whose spiral carvings tell the story of the wars. Romanians still honour him as a founder of their nation.

## Trajan's Parthian War and Death {#parthian-war}

In 113 Trajan, the emperor who had already conquered Dacia, launched his last war, against Parthia, the great Iranian empire beyond the river Euphrates. The trigger was Armenia, a mountain kingdom long shared between the two powers, where Parthia had installed a king Rome would not accept. Trajan rejected peace offers, deposed the king and made Armenia a Roman province in 114.

He then pushed into Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates. In 116 he dragged his fleet overland to the Tigris and captured Ctesiphon, the Parthian capital. Reaching the Persian Gulf, he wrote to the Senate regretting that he was too old to repeat the conquests of the Macedonian king who had once marched to India. Under Trajan the empire reached its greatest size.

It did not last. Revolts broke out across Mesopotamia, and Jewish communities rose in Egypt, Cyprus and Cyrene, a Greek city in modern Libya. Trajan failed to take Hatra, a desert fortress city, and his health collapsed. Sailing home, he died of a stroke at Selinus, on the southern coast of modern Turkey, in August 117. Tradition holds that his ashes lie in a small chamber at the base of his Column.

Why he fought is still debated: modern historians suggest control of eastern trade, safer frontiers, or simply glory.

## Hadrian's Disputed Succession {#hadrian-succession}

Hadrian, Trajan's successor, was born in 76 in Italica, the same Spanish town as Trajan, whose cousin was Hadrian's father. Orphaned at ten, he became Trajan's ward and later married Trajan's grandniece. Yet Trajan never formally adopted him.

As Trajan lay dying in 117, an adoption document appeared, signed not by the emperor but by his wife, who favoured Hadrian. Hadrian himself was far away in Syria, though Roman law expected both parties to be present at an adoption. Rumours swirled. A Greek senator who wrote a history of Rome a century later called the adoption fake; another ancient source accepted it.

Hadrian's reign began in blood. In 118 four senior former consuls, close friends of Trajan and possible rivals, were accused of conspiracy, condemned without a public trial and killed. Hadrian blamed the commander of his Guard, but the Senate never forgave him.

He also gave up Trajan's newest conquests. Mesopotamia was abandoned as too costly and distant to defend, Armenia returned to Parthian influence, and parts of Dacia were handed to a neighbouring people. Ancient writers called this jealousy of Trajan's glory. Modern historians tend to see a realistic retreat by an overstretched empire.

## Hadrian the Traveller and Builder {#hadrian-the-traveller}

Hadrian, emperor from 117 to 138, spent more than half his reign outside Italy, preferring to see the provinces for himself. He wanted a stable empire behind defensible borders, and he strengthened the frontier (*limes*) with forts, watchtowers and constant drill for the troops.

In Britain in 122 he ordered the wall that bears his name, built by ordinary soldiers 'to separate Romans from barbarians'. He never saw it finished. His true passion was Greece. Teased in his youth as the 'Greekling', he became the first emperor shown with a full beard, a style most later emperors copied. He completed the vast Temple of Olympian Zeus in Athens, under construction for more than five centuries, and created a league of Greek cities centred there. In Rome he finished the Pantheon in its familiar domed form and built a sprawling villa at Tivoli, in the hills east of the city.

His beloved companion was Antinous, a young Greek from Bithynia, in north-west Turkey. When the youth drowned in the Nile in 130, in circumstances still unknown, the grieving emperor founded a city in his honour and promoted a cult of him that spread across the empire.

## The Great Jewish Revolt under Hadrian {#jewish-revolt}

Late in his reign Hadrian, the travelling emperor devoted to Greek culture, visited Jerusalem, still in ruins after a Jewish war sixty years earlier. He may have planned to refound it as a Roman colony. A later tradition claims that a ban on circumcision provoked revolt, but modern scholars doubt it. Heavy-handed government, quarrels over land and hopes of a messiah who would rebuild the Temple probably mattered more.

In 132 a massive uprising broke out in Judaea under Bar Kokhba, a leader with messianic claims. The Romans were overwhelmed at first and lost heavily, perhaps the equivalent of a whole legion of about 4,000 men. Hadrian summoned his best general from Britain, and his report to the Senate pointedly left out the customary line that he and the legions were well.

The revolt ended in 135 with the fall of Beitar, a fortress about 10 km (six miles) south-west of Jerusalem. An ancient historian put Jewish deaths at 580,000. Judaea was renamed Syria Palaestina, and Jerusalem was rebuilt as a Greek-style city called Aelia Capitolina. Hadrian saw the war as a bitter blow to his dream of a united empire.

## Antoninus Pius, the Dutiful {#antoninus-pius}

Hadrian, ailing and childless, adopted a consul, one of Rome's two top annual magistrates, as heir in 136, but this man died on 1 January 138. Hadrian then chose Antoninus, a respected senator who had governed the province of Asia, on condition that Antoninus in turn adopt two boys: the young Marcus Aurelius and the dead heir's son. Hadrian died on 10 July 138 at Baiae, a seaside resort near Naples.

The Senate, still resentful, hesitated to declare Hadrian a god (*divus*). Antoninus threatened to refuse the throne unless it did. The senators gave way and honoured his loyalty with the name Pius, 'the dutiful'. He won them over by respecting their privileges and cancelling death sentences passed in Hadrian's last days.

His peaceful reign of twenty-three years was the longest since the first emperor. He kept Marcus at his side in Rome and married him to his daughter in 145. On 7 March 161, dying of a fever at his country estate, he handed the state to Marcus and gave the officer of the night watch his final password: 'equanimity' (*aequanimitas*).

## Marcus Aurelius, the Philosopher Emperor {#marcus-aurelius}

Marcus Aurelius, born in Rome in 121 and the adopted heir of Antoninus Pius, was a devoted Stoic, follower of a philosophy of self-control and duty. He took power in 161 with reluctance, perhaps a genuine 'fear of imperial power' (*horror imperii*), and insisted that his adoptive brother, Lucius Verus, rule beside him with equal titles. For the first time Rome had two emperors, though Marcus, who alone was chief priest (*pontifex maximus*), was clearly the senior.

Troubles followed. Parthia invaded Armenia, and Roman armies struck back, burning the palace in the Parthian capital, Ctesiphon, in 165. Returning troops brought a plague, probably smallpox, that killed five to ten million people. From 166 the Marcomanni, a Germanic people from Bohemia, and their allies crossed the Danube, and Marcus spent much of his reign fighting on the northern frontier. His co-emperor died in 169, probably of the plague.

In camp between 170 and 180 Marcus wrote, in Greek, the private notes now called the Meditations, still widely read. He died on 17 March 180 near the Danube. His own son, Commodus, succeeded him, breaking the run of adopted heirs. A historian who lived through it wrote that Rome now fell 'from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust'. Commodus ruled until 192.

## The 'Five Good Emperors': What Historians Think {#five-good-emperors-debate}

The label 'Five Good Emperors' covers Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius, who ruled from 96 to 180. It goes back to a Renaissance Florentine political thinker who praised the five from Nerva to Marcus as proof that adopting the best man beat hereditary succession. An 18th-century English historian popularised the phrase and counted their age among the happiest in human history.

The picture rests on thin evidence. The main ancient narrative survives mostly in shortened Byzantine summaries. The most glowing portrait of Trajan is a formal speech of praise by a senator. Much of what we know about Hadrian comes from the Historia Augusta, a late 4th-century collection of imperial biographies notorious for invention, though its life of Hadrian is thought fairly reliable. Coins, inscriptions and archaeology fill many gaps.

Judgements differ. Ancient writers adored Trajan, while senators feared Hadrian. The same English historian doubted Trajan's warlike reign, and a great 19th-century German scholar spoke of his 'insatiable lust for conquest'. The adoption 'system' was also partly chance: Nerva, Trajan and Hadrian had no children, and when Marcus had a son, he chose him.
