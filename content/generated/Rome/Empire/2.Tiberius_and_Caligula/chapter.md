# Empire: Tiberius and Caligula (AD 14-41)

## Tiberius Before the Throne {#tiberius-before-power}

Tiberius, Rome's second emperor, was born in 42 BC into the Claudii, one of the city's oldest aristocratic families. When he was a small child, his mother divorced his father and married Augustus, the man who would become Rome's first emperor.

Tiberius proved one of Rome's finest generals. His campaigns in the Alps, along the Danube and in Germany laid the foundations of the empire's northern frontier. His private life was unhappier. Augustus forced him to divorce the wife he loved and marry Augustus's own daughter, a match that turned bitter and scandalous.

In 6 BC, on the verge of becoming the second most powerful man in Rome, he abruptly retired to Rhodes, a Greek island off the coast of modern Turkey. His motives are still debated. Only after Augustus's two young grandsons and heirs died did the old emperor turn back to him. In AD 4 Augustus adopted Tiberius as son and heir, on condition that Tiberius in turn adopt his popular nephew, Germanicus.

## The Reluctant Emperor {#reluctant-emperor}

When Augustus died on 19 August AD 14, Tiberius was 55 and already shared the emperor's powers. In September the Senate (*senatus*), Rome's council of leading aristocrats, confirmed him as first citizen (*princeps*), the modest title Augustus had used for his position.

Tiberius seems to have taken the job with deep reluctance, perhaps feeling he could never match his charismatic stepfather. He refused the title Father of the Fatherland (*pater patriae*) and the Civic Crown (*corona civica*), an oak-leaf wreath of honour. Senators read this as hypocrisy, not humility, and relations soured. He wanted the Senate to act on its own, yet gave orders so vague that senators argued over what he meant. A later historian reports that he called them "men fit to be slaves".

Still, he was an efficient administrator. He avoided costly conquests, strengthening the frontiers with new bases and diplomacy instead. He also discouraged worship of himself, allowing just one temple in his honour, at Smyrna, a Greek city in western Turkey.

## Germanicus, the Golden Heir {#germanicus}

Germanicus, nephew and adopted son of the emperor Tiberius, was the most admired Roman of his generation: charming, popular and a gifted general. In AD 13 he took command of the eight legions on the Rhine, Rome's frontier river with Germany, about a third of the whole army.

When Augustus died in AD 14, those legions mutinied over unpaid bonuses and tried to proclaim Germanicus emperor. He refused, paid them out of his own pocket, and led them into Germany. For three years he fought the war leader who in AD 9 had wiped out three Roman legions in the Teutoburg Forest. Germanicus visited the battlefield, began burying the soldiers' remains, and recovered two of the three lost eagles (*aquilae*), the sacred standards of the legions.

On 26 May AD 17 he celebrated a triumph (*triumphus*), a victory parade through Rome. Sent east as Tiberius's expected successor, he quarrelled bitterly with the governor of Syria. In October AD 19 he fell ill at Antioch, Syria's chief city, and died convinced he had been poisoned. The governor killed himself before his trial ended, and rumours that Tiberius was behind it all never faded.

## Sejanus and the Retreat to Capri {#sejanus}

Sejanus became praetorian prefect (*praefectus praetorio*) in AD 15, commanding the Praetorian Guard (*cohortes praetoriae*), the emperor's bodyguard. When the emperor Tiberius moved the Guard from camps outside Rome into the city itself, Sejanus gained between 6,000 and 9,000 soldiers inside the capital.

After Tiberius's own son died in mysterious circumstances in AD 23, the emperor leaned ever harder on Sejanus, calling him "partner of my labours". In AD 26 Tiberius left Rome for good, retiring to his villas on Capri, an island in the Bay of Naples. Sejanus ran the state and controlled the messages passing between emperor and capital.

He used treason trials to destroy rivals. The widow of the popular general Germanicus and her two eldest sons were exiled and later died in suspicious circumstances. In AD 31, apparently plotting to seize power himself, Sejanus was summoned to the Senate, where a letter from Tiberius was read out denouncing him. He was executed within the week, and a wave of trials swept up anyone linked to him.

## The Death of Tiberius {#death-of-tiberius}

Tiberius died on 16 March AD 37 at Misenum, a naval base on the Bay of Naples, aged 77. Ancient writers agree on when and where, but not how. One says the old man seemed to stop breathing, courtiers began hailing his heir Caligula, and then Tiberius stirred, so the new praetorian prefect smothered him with his bedclothes. Others blame Caligula himself. Some writers who lived at the time simply describe a natural death.

Rome did not mourn. The Senate refused him the divine honours given to Augustus, and crowds chanted "To the Tiber with Tiberius!", because criminals' bodies were thrown into the river. Even so, his ashes were laid in Augustus's mausoleum.

His record was better than his reputation. A Roman writer called him "the gloomiest of men", yet he left the treasury nearly 3 billion sesterces, a common Roman coin, and a stable, consolidated empire. Had he died before AD 23, one modern view holds, he might be remembered as a model ruler.

## Little Boot Becomes Emperor {#caligula-rise}

Caligula, Rome's third emperor, was born Gaius in AD 12, son of the beloved general Germanicus. As a toddler on campaign in Germany he wore a miniature uniform with little army boots (*caligae*), and the soldiers nicknamed him Caligula, "little boot".

His childhood ended in disaster. His father died in AD 19. His mother feuded with the emperor Tiberius, and she and Caligula's two older brothers died after being exiled. From AD 31 Caligula lived under Tiberius's eye on Capri. He hid his feelings so well that it was said there had never been "a better slave or a worse master", and Tiberius's praetorian prefect, commander of the imperial bodyguard, protected him.

When Tiberius died in AD 37, Caligula, aged 24 and without political or military experience, received all of an emperor's powers in a single day. His start was dazzling. He doubled the Guard's bonus, gave cash to every citizen and staged lavish games. A Jewish writer who later visited his court called the first seven months a "Golden Age".

## Illness, Purges and Empty Coffers {#caligula-turn}

In the autumn of AD 37, only months into his reign, the emperor Caligula fell gravely ill and hovered near death for about a month. After he recovered, the killings began. His former father-in-law, an eminent senator, and Tiberius's teenage grandson, Caligula's co-heir, were forced to kill themselves. In early AD 38 the praetorian prefect who had protected him met the same fate. That year his beloved sister died, and he made her a goddess, the first Roman woman so honoured.

In AD 39 his relations with the Senate collapsed. Caligula had publicly burned Tiberius's files on treason (*maiestas*) trials, claiming he had not read them. That proved false. Now he reopened old cases and told the senators that since they hated him, it was better they feared him.

Money became an obsession. Ancient writers say he squandered a fortune, then seized rich men's estates and taxed everything from weddings to prostitutes, with the Guard as collectors. Yet his successor seems to have inherited a solvent treasury, and many of the taxes outlived him.

## A Horse, a Bridge and a God {#horse-bridge-god}

The most famous story about Caligula concerns his racehorse Incitatus, "Speedy". Ancient writers say he planned to make it a consul, one of Rome's two highest annual officials. Some modern historians read this as a joke aimed at senators who craved that honour; others disagree.

In AD 39 or 40 he built a floating bridge of ships more than 3 km (two miles) long, from Baiae, a seaside resort near Naples, to the nearby port of Puteoli. Wearing a breastplate said to have belonged to the great Macedonian conqueror, he rode Incitatus across. Its pointlessness may have been the point: a display of total power.

He enjoyed dressing as gods, including Jupiter, Rome's chief god. When a shoemaker from Gaul called him "a complete idiot", Caligula seemed to appreciate the honesty. More dangerously, he ordered his statue set up in the Temple in Jerusalem, a blasphemy for Jews. The governor of Syria stalled, and it was never installed.

Abroad, a planned invasion of Britain was abandoned, while Mauretania, a kingdom in north-west Africa, was annexed after Caligula executed its king.

## The Murder of Caligula {#assassination}

On 24 January AD 41, the day before he was due to leave for Alexandria, Egypt's great city, the emperor Caligula was stabbed in a narrow passage beneath his palace during a festival of games. The killers were officers of his own Praetorian Guard. The first blow came from an officer whom Caligula had repeatedly mocked for his high voice. By the time the emperor's German bodyguards arrived he was dead, and in their fury they killed several bystanders.

The conspirators also murdered Caligula's wife and young daughter. In the Senate, some spoke of restoring the Republic, government without emperors. The soldiers had other plans. According to tradition, a guardsman found Caligula's uncle hiding behind a palace curtain; the Praetorians proclaimed him emperor and the Senate gave way.

Ordinary Romans, with whom Caligula had been popular, were shocked. The new emperor executed the assassins and refused to declare his nephew a public enemy (*hostis*) or condemn his memory (*damnatio memoriae*). Still, many of Caligula's statues were quietly recarved with other emperors' faces.

## Gloomy Tyrant, Mad Emperor? What Historians Think {#what-historians-think}

Nearly everything we know about Tiberius and Caligula comes from senators and officials writing long afterwards, men with little love for emperors. The fullest account of Tiberius, by a senator-historian born in AD 56, paints him as a hypocrite whose character darkened by stages after his son's death in AD 23. A later biographer delights in scandal. An officer who served under Tiberius praised him lavishly, though whether from admiration or prudence is unclear.

Modern scholars push back. One counted no more than 52 people accused of treason in Tiberius's 22-year reign, nearly half of whom escaped conviction.

Caligula's case is harder still. His main biographer wrote 80 years after his death, another historian over 180 years after, and no surviving source is friendly. There is no reliable evidence of his mental state, and every ancient writer had reasons to condemn him. Many historians now see not a lunatic but a young man handed near-limitless power, with a cruel sense of humour and open contempt for the Senate.
