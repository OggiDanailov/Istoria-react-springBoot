# Empire: The Fall of the West (AD 395-476)

## An Empire Split in Two (395) {#a-divided-empire}

In January 395 the last emperor to rule the whole Roman world died and left it to his two young sons, Arcadius and Honorius. One took the East, governed from Constantinople (*Constantinopolis*), the great city on the Bosphorus. The other took the West, which included Italy, Spain and Gaul (*Gallia*), roughly modern France. Neither son ever showed real ability, and both became puppets of rival ministers who distrusted each other.

The timing was terrible. Since 376, huge numbers of Goths, a Germanic people fleeing the Huns, had been living inside the empire. The Huns were nomadic horsemen from the Eurasian steppe. Corrupt officials exploited the refugees, and the Goths rebelled. At Adrianople, in modern European Turkey, on 9 August 378 they destroyed an eastern army and killed its emperor.

Rome could neither crush nor expel them, as it normally would have done. Instead they were settled as allies (*foederati*), peoples who kept their own leaders and fought for Rome in return. By 395 the Goths were an armed power that could threaten either half of the empire, while the western army had been bled white by two civil wars.

## Alaric and the Half-Vandal General {#alaric-and-the-general}

Among the Goths a leader rose who would become infamous: Alaric, the chieftain who would one day sack Rome. Angry at being denied high Roman command, he revolted in 395 and plundered Greece.

Real power in the West lay with its commander-in-chief (*magister militum*), Stilicho, a general whose father was a Vandal (the Vandals were a Germanic people), who acted as guardian to the boy emperor. Twice he cornered Alaric in Greece and twice let him escape, and historians still argue why. In 401 Alaric invaded Italy. The general fought him to a draw at Pollentia in 402 and then beat him at Verona. Even so, the frightened court moved its capital from Milan to Ravenna, a town near the Adriatic coast shielded by marshes and with an escape route by sea.

More blows followed. Another Gothic horde ravaged northern Italy in 405 and 406. Then perhaps 100,000 Vandals, Alans (nomadic horsemen from the steppe) and others crossed the Rhine (*Rhenus*) into Gaul. In 408 rivals at court accused the general of treason. Lured out of a church where he had taken sanctuary, he was executed on 22 August. Roman soldiers then massacred the families of barbarian troops, and thousands fled to join Alaric.

## Alaric Besieges Rome (408-410) {#sieges-of-rome}

Rome was no longer the capital, but it was still the Eternal City (*Urbs Aeterna*), the symbolic heart of the empire, with perhaps 800,000 inhabitants. In late 408 Alaric marched on it unopposed and blocked the grain ships coming up the Tiber. Rations fell to a third, and corpses lay unburied in the streets.

Envoys from the Senate (*senatus*) boasted that the Romans were ready to fight. Alaric reportedly laughed: “The thickest grass is easier to cut than the thinnest.” Asked what he would leave the citizens, he replied: “Their lives.” The city paid him about 1,650 kg (5,000 pounds) of gold and nearly 10 tonnes (30,000 pounds) of silver. Pagan statues were melted down to make up the sum.

Alaric's real demands were modest: land and grain for his people. Yet the emperor in Ravenna refused even those and answered with insults. Alaric besieged Rome again in 409 and set up a puppet emperor, then deposed him a few months later. When a Gothic rival ambushed him during peace talks in 410, Alaric gave up on negotiating and marched on Rome for the third time.

## The Sack of Rome (410) {#sack-of-rome-410}

On 24 August 410 the Goths entered Rome through the Salarian Gate. Some said traitors had opened it, others that hunger had. For three days the Goths plundered the city. It was the first time in almost 800 years that Rome had fallen to a foreign enemy.

The great tombs of earlier emperors were ransacked and their ashes scattered, and fires gutted buildings near the old Senate house. Citizens were tortured to reveal hidden valuables, and the emperor's own sister was carried off as a hostage. Yet by the standards of the age the sack was restrained. There was no general massacre, most monuments survived, and the city's two great basilicas served as sanctuaries.

The shock echoed across the Roman world. “If Rome can perish, what can be safe?” wrote Jerome, a famous scholar-monk living in Bethlehem. A 6th-century Greek historian later told a mocking story. When the emperor in Ravenna heard that “Roma had perished”, he was horrified, then relieved to learn it was only the city. He had feared for his favourite chicken, named Roma.

Alaric headed south to cross to Africa, but storms wrecked his ships and he died of fever within months.

## Who Was to Blame? Pagans, Christians and the Sack {#who-was-to-blame}

In 410 the empire was still divided between Christians and worshippers of the old gods, and both sides used Alaric's sack of Rome as ammunition. Pagans argued that Rome had lost divine protection by abandoning the ancient sacrifices. One pagan historian blamed Christianity for sapping the empire's old virtues. Some Christians replied that the disaster was God's punishment of a proud city, softened only by his mercy. Others thought the end of the world was near.

The most lasting answer came from Augustine, a bishop in North Africa, who wrote a vast book called The City of God. He rejected the idea, shared by pagans and Christians alike, that true religion should bring worldly success. The real home of Christians, he argued, was a heavenly city that no earthly disaster could touch. His book became one of the foundations of Christian thought.

## Barbarian Kingdoms Inside the Empire (410-439) {#kingdoms-inside-the-empire}

After Alaric's death his followers, known today as the Visigoths, moved into Gaul. In 418 Rome gave them land to farm in Aquitaine, in south-western France. It made lasting what had begun with the Goths in the Balkans: a whole people living inside the empire under its own king and army, only loosely obeying Roman authority.

The empire was shrinking everywhere. In 410 the towns of Britain (*Britannia*) were told to see to their own defence, and no Roman government ever ruled there again. Spain slipped into the hands of the Sueves, another Germanic people, the Vandals and local strongmen, while the Franks became the main power in north-eastern Gaul.

The worst loss came in Africa. In the late 420s the Vandals and their allies crossed from Spain under their ferocious king, Gaiseric. In 439 they captured Carthage (*Carthago*), the great port in modern Tunisia, and made it the capital of an independent kingdom with a powerful navy. Africa had been rich, easy to defend and the main source of Rome's grain. Its lost taxes could have paid for nearly 40,000 infantry. The government raised taxes on peasants who could pay no more, while the wealthy avoided them.

## Aetius Stops Attila (451) {#aetius-and-attila}

For two decades the West was held together by Aetius, the tireless general who fought Goths, Franks and rebels, often with Hunnic mercenaries. Then the Huns themselves turned west. Since 444 they had been united under Attila, a king who ruled many Germanic peoples and had squeezed vast sums of gold out of the eastern empire. His excuse came from Honoria, the emperor's sister. Trapped in an engagement she hated, she sent him her ring and a plea for help. Attila claimed her as his bride, with half the western empire as her dowry, and in 451 crossed the Rhine.

Aetius built a coalition of old enemies: Visigoths, Alans, Franks, Burgundians and what Roman troops remained. The Alan defenders of Orléans held out against Attila's siege, and the Huns fell back. Around 20 June 451 the armies clashed on the Catalaunian Plains near Troyes, in north-eastern France, fighting over a ridge until dark. The Visigothic king was killed, and Attila withdrew to his camp of wagons. Aetius let him go home.

An English historian of the 18th century called it the last victory won in the name of the Western Roman Empire. Modern historians are cooler. Ancient death tolls of up to 300,000 are impossible, the main account is distorted, and many think the real turning point was the defence of Orléans.

## Murder in the Palace and the Vandal Sack (452-455) {#murder-and-vandal-sack}

Attila returned in 452, invading Italy and razing Aquileia, a city at the head of the Adriatic. Disease and hunger in his army, and perhaps an embassy led by the pope, persuaded him to turn back. He died suddenly the next year, and his empire soon fell apart.

In 454 the western emperor, Valentinian III, stabbed his great general Aetius to death with his own hands. Some historians think the emperor simply wanted to rule for himself rather than through a powerful manager. A year later Aetius's supporters murdered the emperor in revenge.

A rich senator who had encouraged both killings seized the throne. He broke off a planned royal marriage with the Vandals of Carthage, which amounted to declaring war. When a Vandal fleet reached Italy, the new emperor tried to flee and was torn apart by a Roman mob. The Vandals then plundered Rome for two weeks, far more thoroughly than the Goths in 410, and sailed away with huge treasure and an imperial princess.

## Romulus Augustulus, the Last Emperor (476) {#the-last-emperor}

After 455 western emperors rose and fell in quick succession, most of them puppets of generals of barbarian birth. One capable emperor rebuilt the army and planned to retake Africa, but traitors burned his fleet and his own general had him executed in 461. A huge eastern expedition in 468 was wrecked by Vandal fireships off North Africa.

In 475 Orestes, a general who had once been Attila's secretary, drove out the reigning emperor and crowned his own young son. The boy was named Romulus Augustus, after Rome's founder and its first emperor, but rivals mocked him as Romulus Augustulus, “little Augustus”. He was never recognised outside Italy.

When the general broke his promise of land to his Germanic mercenaries, they rebelled under Odoacer, a Germanic officer, and killed him. On 4 September 476 Odoacer forced Romulus Augustulus to give up the throne. Said to have pitied the 16-year-old, Odoacer spared his life, granted him a yearly pension of 6,000 gold coins (*solidi*) and sent him to live with relatives in Campania, the region around Naples. Odoacer then ruled Italy himself and sent the imperial regalia to the emperor in Constantinople.

## Why Did the West Fall? What Historians Think {#why-the-west-fell}

By convention, the Western Roman Empire ended on 4 September 476, when Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus. Historians have long questioned that neat date. A deposed emperor still claimed the throne from Dalmatia, on the Adriatic coast, until 480, and a Roman enclave in northern Gaul lasted until 486. The Senate kept meeting in Rome for more than a century, and the eastern empire survived for centuries.

Edward Gibbon, an English historian whose Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire began appearing in 1776, blamed barbarians, Christians and the Romans' own quarrels. One modern scholar has counted 210 different theories. Today historians point to plague, a worsening climate, mass migration, civil wars, crushing taxes and a rich elite unwilling to pay for defence.

Many of these weaknesses affected the East too. Its advantage was geography: Constantinople's walls and the sea shielded its heartland, while the West had some 2,000 km of Rhine and Danube frontier to guard. Many scholars now speak of “late antiquity”, a long transformation in which Roman culture lived on after Roman power ended.
