# Empire: From Constantine's Sons to Theodosius (AD 337-395)

## A Family Massacre and Three Brothers {#sons-of-constantine}

When Rome's first Christian emperor died in AD 337, his three sons were proclaimed joint emperors and divided the Roman world between them. First, though, the family was thinned out. Soldiers murdered most of the old emperor's other male relatives, a purge that ancient writers blamed on the middle son, Constantius II, the brother who would outlast them all.

Only two boys survived from the rival branch of the family: Julian, then about six, and his older half-brother. They were probably spared because they were so young. Julian's own father was among the dead. Years later Julian claimed that Constantius was tormented by guilt over the killings.

The brothers soon destroyed one another. The eldest was killed in 340 while attacking the youngest. The youngest, Constans, fell in 350 fighting a usurper, a general who had seized power in the west. That left Constantius as sole emperor. He crushed the usurper in 351, but he needed a relative to share the burden of government. So he made Julian's half-brother, Gallus, his deputy in the east. The young man ruled by terror and was executed in 354. Julian, now the last male of his line, was summoned to court at Milan (*Mediolanum*) and held for a year under suspicion of treason.

## Julian's Secret Conversion {#julian-the-philosopher}

Julian was born in Constantinople, probably in AD 331, and grew up speaking Greek. His mother died soon after his birth. As nephew of the first Christian emperor, he was raised a Christian, kept under close guard and away from public life. He was taught by a bishop and by a Gothic tutor he later remembered fondly, then sent to a remote imperial estate in Cappadocia, in central Anatolia. There a bishop lent him books of classical Greek literature. Julian even served as a reader in church, and his later writings show he knew the Bible well.

Around the age of 20, he quietly abandoned Christianity. He later wrote that he had spent twenty years as a Christian and twelve following Helios, the sun god. He studied Neoplatonism, a mystical philosophy built on the ideas of Plato. One teacher warned him about a rival philosopher who practised theurgy, magical rituals meant to call down the gods, and who had supposedly made a statue of the goddess Hecate smile and laugh. Julian was not put off. He sought that man out as his new mentor.

After his year of detention, the empress spoke up for him and he was allowed to study in Athens. There he was initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries, ancient secret rites held at Eleusis near Athens.

## Julian Defends Gaul {#julian-in-gaul}

In November AD 355, Constantius II made his young cousin Julian a Caesar, a junior emperor, and married him to his sister. He sent Julian to Gaul, roughly modern France and Belgium, with a small staff. The idea was that Julian would be a figurehead while experienced officials ran things. Instead the bookish young man taught himself to command an army.

Germanic peoples had broken across the Rhine frontier. In 356 Julian recovered Cologne from the Franks, a Germanic people of the lower Rhine. That winter, however, he was trapped by Frankish raiders in a small town near Verdun for months.

His great moment came in 357 at Strasbourg (*Argentoratum*). An army of the Alamanni, a confederation of tribes from the upper Rhine, attacked him. The Romans were heavily outnumbered, and 600 horsemen deserted mid-battle, yet they won overwhelmingly. The enemy was driven into the river and their king captured. The soldiers tried to hail Julian as Augustus, senior emperor, but he rebuked them. He then crossed the Rhine and forced three local kingdoms to submit.

Julian also took on civil government. He blocked a tax increase by Gaul's top civil official, the praetorian prefect (*praefectus praetorio*), and clashed with him over corruption.

## Mutiny in Paris and the Road to Civil War {#proclaimed-in-paris}

Around AD 359 the king of Persia invaded Roman Mesopotamia and took the fortress city of Amida after a 73-day siege. In February 360, Constantius II ordered more than half of Julian's army in Gaul to march east. The order went straight to the commanders, bypassing Julian.

The soldiers had no wish to leave Gaul. They mutinied and proclaimed Julian Augustus, senior emperor, at Paris (*Lutetia*). Whether Julian secretly encouraged the revolt is unclear, but some evidence suggests he did. By November he was openly using the title and issuing coins with it.

In 361 Julian marched east, splitting his army into three columns. He led one himself, sailing down the Danube in boats. The gamble nearly failed when troops loyal to Constantius seized Aquileia, a city at the head of the Adriatic, threatening to cut him off. Julian could only wait at Naissus in the Balkans, writing letters to Greek cities to justify himself.

Then fate intervened. Constantius died on 3 November 361, and some sources say he named Julian his heir on his deathbed. On 11 December Julian entered Constantinople as sole emperor. His first act was to lead the Christian funeral of his cousin, a public show that he was the lawful successor.

## The Last Pagan Emperor {#julian-pagan-revival}

Julian, remembered by Christians as "the Apostate", was the last non-Christian to rule the Roman Empire. He believed Rome could only be saved by returning to its ancient values.

He began with the palace. Thousands of servants, eunuchs and officials were dismissed, and a special court tried corrupt ministers of Constantius; several were executed. Julian wanted to be first among equals (*primus inter pares*), and he joined debates in the Senate (*senatus*) as an ordinary member. He handed power back to the city councils (*curiae*) and cancelled unpaid land taxes.

His religious programme was bolder. He reopened temples and stripped bishops of their privileges. His School Edict stopped Christians from teaching the classics: "If they want to learn literature, they have Luke and Mark," it said. He let bishops exiled as heretics return home, perhaps to set Christians quarrelling. He also backed rebuilding the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem, most historians think to disprove a Christian prophecy. Fires, perhaps caused by an earthquake, stopped the work.

Julian wanted pagan priests to be moral models who ran charities, like Christian clergy. Historians note the irony: in competing with Christianity, he made paganism look more like it. His revival died with him.

## Disaster in Persia and Julian's Death {#persian-campaign}

After a prickly nine-month stay in Antioch, the great city of Roman Syria, where citizens mocked his philosopher's beard and he answered with a satire called the "Beard Hater", Julian set out against Persia in March AD 363. Persia was then ruled by the Sasanian dynasty, which controlled Iran and Iraq. His army of perhaps 65,000 to 90,000 men was the largest Rome ever sent against the Persians, supplied by a fleet of over 1,000 ships on the Euphrates.

By May he reached Ctesiphon, the Persian capital on the Tigris near modern Baghdad. The Romans won a battle before its gates but did not take the city. His generals advised against a siege, so Julian burned his fleet and pushed inland. It was a fatal mistake. The Persians burned the crops in his path and flooded the land behind him, forcing a retreat.

In late June, during a Persian raid, Julian rushed into the fight without his armour. A spear pierced his liver, and despite his doctor's efforts he died. Legend says his last words were "You have won, Galilean" (*Vicisti, Galilaee*), "the Galilean" being his scornful name for Jesus, but this is considered invented. Who threw the spear was disputed: a Christian soldier, an Arab fighting for Persia, or, in later Christian legend, a saint. His successor, Jovian, a guard officer, had to surrender territory to save the trapped army, including the fortress city of Nisibis.

## Catastrophe at Adrianople {#battle-of-adrianople}

In AD 376 the Goths, a Germanic people driven from their lands by the Huns, nomadic horsemen from the steppes, asked to settle inside the empire. Valens, emperor of the eastern half, let them cross the Danube as allies (*foederati*), hoping for farmers and soldiers. Instead, cheating Roman officials pushed them into revolt, and two years of inconclusive fighting followed.

In 378 Valens came to finish the job himself. The young western emperor, Gratian, was marching to help and asked him to wait, but Valens wanted a victory of his own. His scouts counted about 10,000 Goths, missing the cavalry who were away gathering food.

On 9 August, Valens marched about 12 km (eight Roman miles) from Adrianople, a city in Thrace (modern Edirne in European Turkey), over rough ground in summer heat. His tired, thirsty troops found the Goths in a ring of wagons on a hilltop. The Gothic leader, Fritigern, set fields on fire and stalled with peace talks. Some Roman units attacked without orders and were thrown back. Then the Gothic cavalry returned and surrounded the Romans, who were packed together and unable to move. The rout lasted until nightfall.

Only a third of the army escaped. Valens' body was never found; one story says he died in a burning farmhouse. Historians often see the battle as the beginning of the end for the western empire.

## Theodosius: Peace with the Goths, War on Heresy {#theodosius-goths-and-faith}

With Valens dead and Rome's leadership shattered, the generals turned to Theodosius, a commander from Spain whose father, a famous general, had been executed in murky circumstances. He was made emperor in the east in January AD 379.

His army was so depleted that he conscripted farmers and miners and recruited barbarians, even Gothic deserters. He could not drive the Goths out. A treaty of 3 October 382 let them settle south of the Danube under their own leaders, fighting for Rome as a separate force. It brought peace in his lifetime but created a semi-independent people inside the empire that troubled later emperors.

Theodosius was a devout Christian, baptised during a serious illness in 380. That year he issued the Edict of Thessalonica (*edictum*). It declared that only Christians who believed the Father, Son and Holy Spirit were of one divine substance could call themselves "catholic". Opponents, notably the Arians, who taught that the Son was created by the Father and lesser than him, were branded heretics. In 381 a council of bishops at Constantinople confirmed this belief. Modern historians stress that the edict was aimed only at Constantinople and did not make Christianity the state religion.

## The Massacre of Thessalonica and Bishop Ambrose {#massacre-of-thessalonica}

In about April AD 390, a riot broke out in Thessalonica, a great city in northern Greece. According to the most trusted account, a general had jailed a popular charioteer, and the crowd demanded his release. When the general refused, the mob killed him.

Roman soldiers then took revenge in the city's racetrack, killing thousands of civilians. The emperor Theodosius was far away in Milan. Historians still argue over his role. Some think he ordered the slaughter in a fit of rage. Others believe a few planned executions spiralled out of control when the soldiers panicked, and that Theodosius took the blame to hide his failure to control his troops.

Ambrose, the forceful bishop of Milan, wrote to the emperor refusing him communion until he did penance. Theodosius agreed, attending church without his imperial robes until Christmas. A famous later story has Ambrose physically barring the cathedral door against the emperor. Modern scholars call this a pious fiction. For centuries the episode was held up as proof that the Church could command the state, but historians now see Ambrose as just one adviser among many, offering the emperor a way to save face.

## The Last Ruler of a United Empire {#theodosius-legacy}

Theodosius fought two civil wars to defend his family's claim on the west. In AD 388 he defeated a usurper who had killed the western emperor. In 392 the young western emperor he had restored was found hanged, and Arbogast, a powerful Frankish general and the western master of soldiers (*magister militum*), put a puppet emperor on the throne. In September 394, at the river Frigidus in today's Slovenia, a fierce wind blew into the enemy's faces and gave Theodosius victory. He now ruled the whole empire, the last man ever to do so.

He died at Milan in January 395, and his bishop, Ambrose, praised him at his funeral. His two young sons, Arcadius and Honorius, split the empire into eastern and western courts, a division that lasted until the west fell in the late 5th century.

Later Christian writers hailed Theodosius as the emperor who crushed paganism. Modern historians doubt it. He banned animal sacrifice and issued harsh laws in 391 and 392, but he kept many temples open and appointed pagans to high office. Of 43 temple destructions reported in written sources, archaeologists have confirmed only four. The old gods, it seems, faded slowly rather than being overthrown.
