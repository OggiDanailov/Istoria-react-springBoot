# Late Republic: Antony, Octavian and the End of the Republic (44-27 BCE)

## After the Ides of March {#after-caesars-murder}

On 15 March 44 BC, Julius Caesar, the dictator who had made himself master of Rome, was stabbed to death by a band of senators. His killers called themselves the liberators (*liberatores*). At first an uneasy compromise held. The assassins were pardoned, Caesar's decisions were confirmed, and the office of dictator was abolished.

The man best placed to profit was Mark Antony, Caesar's most important general and one of the two consuls (*consules*), the elected heads of state, that year. In June he pushed through an illegal law giving himself the provinces of Gaul, roughly modern France and northern Italy. From there he could menace Rome itself.

He had a young rival. Octavian, Caesar's great-nephew and adopted heir, lured Caesar's veterans away from Antony and raised a private army. Rome's greatest orator, an ex-consul who loathed Antony, persuaded the Senate (*senatus*) to back the young man. On 21 April 43 BC the Senate's forces beat Antony at Mutina, a town in northern Italy, and declared him a public enemy. But both consuls died in the fighting, and Octavian marched on Rome to claim power for himself.

## Three Men Take Over the Republic {#forming-the-triumvirate}

Octavian reached Rome on 19 August 43 BC and had himself elected consul. He confirmed his adoption as Caesar's son, set up courts to condemn the assassins in their absence, and quietly cancelled Antony's status as a public enemy.

Then, rather than fight Antony, he made a deal with him. Urged on by their soldiers, the two met in northern Italy under the protection of Lepidus, a Caesarian general commanding in the west. The three men named themselves the board of three for restoring the republic (*triumviri rei publicae constituendae*). A friendly tribune passed a law, the *lex Titia*, on 27 November 43 BC, giving them this power for five years.

It was close to absolute rule. The three could make binding decrees, overrule any governor, punish without right of appeal, and appoint every magistrate. Official records listed them above the consuls. They also divided the west between them: Gaul to Antony, southern Gaul and Spain to Lepidus, Africa and the islands to Octavian, then the junior partner.

We call this the Second Triumvirate, but that label is modern, first used in the 17th century, and many historians now avoid it as misleading.

## The Proscriptions {#proscriptions}

The triumvirs' first act was a massacre. They issued a declaration that Caesar's famous mercy (*clementia*) had been a mistake, and attached a list of men to be killed. This was a proscription (*proscriptio*): anyone named could be murdered on sight, and his property was seized and sold. Bounty hunters and informers were paid cash for each death.

How many died is disputed. Ancient writers give figures ranging from about 130 senators to 300 men, plus many knights (*equites*), the wealthy class just below the senators. A larger figure of 300 senators and 2,000 knights probably counts everyone killed or stripped of property over several years. One modern study settles on about 300 names, split evenly between senators and knights. Historians who think the triumvirs mainly wanted money tend to favour the higher numbers.

Friends were sacrificed alongside enemies, as the three traded names to get their own foes on the lists. The orator who had attacked Antony in a series of blistering speeches was killed, along with his brother, nephew and son. Some victims escaped by sea. Still short of cash, the triumvirs seized the land of eighteen rich Italian towns for their veterans.

## Philippi: Revenge for Caesar {#battle-of-philippi}

In January 42 BC Julius Caesar was officially declared a god. His avengers then went to war. While Lepidus guarded Italy, Antony and Octavian took roughly forty legions (*legiones*) across the Adriatic against Caesar's two leading assassins, who had spent the year squeezing money from the eastern provinces to pay their troops.

The armies met at Philippi, a town in Macedonia in northern Greece. In the first battle, one of the assassins' armies stormed Octavian's camp and destroyed three of his legions. Octavian, it was said, spent the fight hiding in a marsh. But Antony broke into the other assassin's camp, and that leader, believing all was lost, killed himself.

Three weeks later, on 23 October 42 BC, the surviving assassin offered battle again, fearing desertions. This time the combined Caesarian armies crushed him, and he too took his own life. Antony was the real victor of Philippi.

Later Romans saw it as a turning point. A Roman historian writing over a century afterwards dated the end of the republic to this battle, after which, he wrote, the state had no armies left to defend it.

## Antony Turns East {#antony-and-cleopatra}

After Philippi the victors carved up the Roman world again. Antony took the rich eastern provinces and kept Gaul. Octavian got the thankless jobs of settling veterans in Italy and fighting a son of Caesar's old enemy Pompey, who ruled the seas from Sicily. Lepidus was left with Africa alone.

Antony toured the east, favouring great cities and propping up loyal local rulers. He summoned Cleopatra, the Greek-speaking queen of Egypt, to meet him in Cilicia, on the southern coast of modern Turkey. They soon began an affair, and Antony helped her secure her throne by having her sister killed. Ancient writers claimed the queen was manipulating him. Modern historians think he was simply strengthening a useful ally, in line with his policy of backing strong client monarchs.

The affair would matter more than either expected. Antony later acknowledged twins she bore him in 40 BC and fathered a son with her, and he gave her territory in Cyprus and Cilicia, whose timber could build a fleet. In Italy, the relationship was deeply unpopular.

## Perusia and an Uneasy Peace {#perusia-and-brundisium}

While Antony was away, Italy boiled over. Veterans demanded land in the middle of a famine, made worse by an enemy naval blockade from Sicily. Antony's brother, a consul in 41 BC, and Antony's wife stirred up the discontent against Octavian and raised an army.

Octavian trapped them in Perusia, modern Perugia in central Italy. When the town fell, he let Antony's family go and spared their soldiers, but massacred the town's councillors and burned it to the ground.

Antony hurried back with an army, and in 40 BC the two rivals faced each other at Brundisium, Italy's main port for Greece. Their soldiers refused to fight one another and pushed them to negotiate. The treaty confirmed Antony as master of the east, gave Octavian the west, and left Lepidus in Africa. It was sealed by a marriage: Antony wed Octavian's sister, a recent widow. Italy celebrated wildly. In 39 BC the triumvirs also made peace with their enemy in Sicily, freeing his runaway slaves and giving his soldiers veterans' rewards.

## Disaster in Parthia, Victory in Sicily {#parthia-sicily-lepidus}

The peace frayed quickly. In 37 BC Antony and Octavian met at Tarentum in southern Italy and renewed the triumvirate, which had quietly lapsed, for five more years. Antony lent ships for the war in Sicily in exchange for promised troops.

In 36 BC both men went to war. Antony invaded Parthia, the great Iranian empire on Rome's eastern border, demanding the return of Roman standards lost in a catastrophic defeat seventeen years earlier. With sixteen legions he raced ahead of his slow siege engines, which the enemy then destroyed. His Armenian ally deserted him, and Parthian horse archers harried his army through a gruelling 27-day retreat. He lost about a third of his men, and his military reputation never recovered.

Octavian fared better. His closest friend and admiral won the decisive sea battle off Naulochus, in Sicily, on 3 September 36 BC. Lepidus, who had landed in Sicily too, then tried to win over Octavian's troops. Octavian walked into his camp almost alone, and the soldiers switched sides. Lepidus was expelled from the triumvirate and exiled, keeping only his life, his property and his title of chief priest (*pontifex maximus*).

## The War of Words {#propaganda-war}

With Lepidus gone, two men remained. Antony gave Octavian plenty of ammunition. In 34 BC he tricked the king of Armenia into captivity and paraded him through Alexandria, Egypt's capital, in a procession honouring the god Dionysus. Then came the Donations of Alexandria, a ceremony in which he crowned Cleopatra's children as rulers of eastern lands. Her son by Julius Caesar was hailed "King of Kings", a direct challenge to Octavian's claim to be Caesar's only heir.

Octavian painted Antony as a Roman corrupted by an eastern queen. Antony's camp hit back, calling Octavian a coward and a land-grabber. When the triumvirate expired at the end of 33 BC, both consuls and hundreds of senators fled Rome to join Antony.

Then a defector told Octavian about Antony's will, kept by the Vestal Virgins (*virgines Vestales*), the priestesses of Rome's sacred hearth. Octavian seized and read it: Antony allegedly wanted burial in Alexandria and left lands to Cleopatra's children. Historians suspect Octavian invented some of it. The outrage worked anyway. In 32 BC the Senate declared war on Cleopatra, a war everyone understood to be against Antony.

## The Battle of Actium {#battle-of-actium}

Antony gathered a huge force in western Greece, camping at Actium, a headland at the mouth of a bay on the Ionian coast. But Octavian's admiral raided the Greek coast and cut his supply lines. Trapped, Antony's camp was ravaged by malaria and desertion.

On 2 September 31 BC Antony's fleet rowed out to break the blockade. Ship counts vary widely between sources. His galleys were huge and armoured, but slow and short of rowers. Octavian's smaller, nimble ships, crewed by veterans, darted in to shower his decks with arrows and stones. A defecting general had already handed over Antony's battle plan.

The fighting raged all afternoon. Then Cleopatra's squadron, waiting behind the line, slipped through a gap to the open sea, and Antony followed in a smaller ship. Ancient writers called it panic or betrayal. Some modern historians argue the breakout was the plan all along. Most of the fleet was left behind, burned or captured, and within about a week Antony's leaderless army surrendered.

Octavian later founded Nicopolis, "City of Victory", nearby, and displayed the bronze rams of captured warships in a hilltop memorial.

## Alexandria and the Birth of an Empire {#alexandria-and-augustus}

Octavian marched on Egypt overland in 30 BC as Antony's allies deserted him. On 1 August he took Alexandria. Antony, falsely told that Cleopatra was dead, stabbed himself. According to ancient accounts, he was carried to the tomb where she was hiding and died in her arms.

Cleopatra tried to bargain, but learned she was destined to be paraded in Octavian's victory procession (*triumphus*). On 12 August 30 BC she killed herself, by a snake's bite or poisoned ointment according to a writer alive at the time. Octavian had her son by Julius Caesar killed, supposedly remarking that "two Caesars are one too many". Egypt became a Roman province.

Octavian now controlled the whole Mediterranean, which Romans called "Our Sea" (*Mare Nostrum*). He celebrated a triple triumph and, in 27 BC, the Senate gave him the name Augustus, meaning "revered". He ruled as first citizen (*princeps*) while keeping the outward forms of the republic, much as the triumvirs had. Historians see this as the end of the Roman Republic and the start of the empire, and of the long Roman Peace (*Pax Romana*).
