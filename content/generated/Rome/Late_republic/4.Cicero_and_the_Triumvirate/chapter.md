# Late Republic: Cicero, Catiline and the Triumvirate (63-53 BCE)

## Catiline: An Aristocrat Who Kept Losing {#catiline}

In 63 BC one of the angriest men in Rome was Catiline, an aristocrat from an ancient family whose career had stalled. None of his ancestors had held the consulship, Rome's top office, shared each year by two consuls (*consules*), for some four centuries. Catiline had stood for it repeatedly and been rejected by the voters every time.

He was no outsider. When he was prosecuted in 65 and 64 BC, former consuls spoke in his defence and he was acquitted. Even Cicero, the brilliant lawyer who would become his nemesis, once considered running for office alongside him.

After yet another defeat in the elections of autumn 63, Catiline decided to seize by force what the voters had denied him. He gathered a ragged coalition of senators expelled for immorality or bribery, failed candidates, and men from fading noble families. The plot's real muscle came from farmers who had lost their land in the civil wars of the 80s BC, and from veterans settled on farms who had sunk into debt after bad harvests.

Ancient writers blamed crushing debts. Modern historians add wounded pride, the shame of failing to match one's ancestors. Despite warnings of a slave revolt, the evidence suggests Catiline wanted a coup that would put him in charge, not a social revolution.

## Cicero Exposes the Plot {#cicero-exposes-plot}

In autumn 63 BC the consul Cicero, a self-made orator from outside Rome's old nobility, began hearing rumours of a conspiracy from a woman informant. The first hard evidence came on 18 or 19 October from Crassus, the richest man in Rome, who handed over anonymous letters describing plans to massacre leading citizens.

Reports followed of armed men gathering in Etruria, the region north of Rome. The Senate (*senatus*) declared an emergency and passed its so-called final decree (*senatus consultum ultimum*), urging the consuls to do whatever it took to protect the state. By 27 October a former army officer had taken up arms near Faesulae, a hill town near modern Florence.

Catiline stayed in Rome and was charged with public violence in early November. The plotters met, probably on 6 November, and two volunteers agreed to murder Cicero. The attempt failed the next morning.

Cicero summoned the Senate and delivered a blistering speech denouncing Catiline to his face. Catiline tried to reply, sneering at Cicero's humble ancestry, but was shouted down. He left the city to join the rebel army, where he put on the trappings of a consul. The Senate declared him a public enemy (*hostis*) and sent Cicero's fellow consul to fight him.

## The Debate Over the Prisoners {#execution-debate}

With Catiline gone, Cicero hunted his allies inside Rome. A senior magistrate among the plotters had tried to recruit the Allobroges, a Gallic tribe from what is now south-eastern France. Their envoys revealed the scheme, and Cicero used them as double agents. On 2 or 3 December five conspirators were arrested and confessed.

On 5 December the Senate met in the Temple of Concord to decide their fate. The emergency decree did not give Cicero legal immunity, so he sought the Senate's advice, probably to share responsibility for whatever followed.

The senior senators all called for death. Then Caesar, a rising politician due to take high office the next year, proposed life imprisonment or custody until trial. His milder plan won many over, though it was also illegal. The mood turned only when a stern young senator famous for old-fashioned morals spoke. He hinted that Caesar sympathised with the plotters and claimed Catiline's army was almost at the gates.

The Senate backed execution without trial. When the men had been strangled, Cicero announced it with a single grim word, "they have lived" (*vixerunt*), meaning they were dead. Senators hailed him as "father of the fatherland" (*pater patriae*).

## Catiline's End and Cicero's Reckoning {#pistoria-and-exile}

After the executions in Rome, support for Catiline melted away. In early January 62 BC his remaining force, at least 3,000 men, met the government army near Pistoria, modern Pistoia in Tuscany. Their commander, Cicero's former fellow consul, claimed to be ill and left a deputy in command. Catiline's army was destroyed, and Catiline himself died fighting.

For Cicero, glory quickly soured. To his dismay, he had to share the credit with the stern senator whose speech had swung the vote. At the end of his year in office, two tribunes of the plebs (*tribuni plebis*), officials elected to protect ordinary citizens and armed with a veto, blocked his farewell speech. One tried to prosecute him for killing citizens without trial, until the Senate threatened anyone who did so.

His enemies waited. In 58 BC a rabble-rousing tribune passed a law banishing anyone who had executed a citizen without trial. Cicero fled to Greece and was recalled only the following year, thanks to Pompey, Rome's greatest general.

Modern verdicts are mixed. Many accept that Cicero acted out of necessity. One modern historian argues he saved the republic only "by circumventing due process and the civil rights of citizens."

## How Dangerous Was Catiline Really? {#catiline-historians}

Almost everything we know about Catiline comes from his enemies. The first source is Cicero, the consul who crushed the plot. He published his speeches against Catiline around 60 BC, partly to defend himself against charges of killing citizens without trial. He also wrote a memoir and a poem celebrating his own consulship.

The second is a short history written about twenty years later by a Roman politician who had been abroad in 63 BC. He leaned heavily on Cicero and portrayed Catiline as a villain bent on destroying Rome from the start, a symbol of the moral decay he believed was ruining the republic.

Some critics have gone further, arguing that Cicero invented or provoked the whole affair. Two well-known studies from the 1970s made versions of this case, but most scholars reject them. The consensus is that the plot was real, but its danger was exaggerated.

Older theories that Catiline was a puppet of Caesar or Crassus are now discredited. One scholar called the affair "one of the best known and least significant episodes in Roman history." Others reply that, while it never threatened to topple the government, it exposed real hardship, which soon prompted a grain law and land reforms.

## Three Frustrated Men Join Forces {#alliance-formed}

By 60 BC three powerful Romans were stuck. Each wanted something the Senate's conservatives would not give.

Pompey, Rome's greatest general, had returned from a long war in the east against the king of Pontus, a realm on the Black Sea. He wanted his peace arrangements there approved and land for his veterans, but opponents tied up his proposals in endless review.

Crassus, one of the richest men in Rome, had grown wealthy buying up confiscated property during the civil wars. He championed the businessmen who bid for the right to collect taxes. They had badly overbid for the province of Asia, in western Turkey, and wanted their payments cut. That too was blocked.

Caesar, back from governing southern Spain, gave up a triumph (*triumphus*), the grand victory parade every general craved, so he could stand for consul. His enemies saddled him with a hostile fellow consul.

Pompey and Crassus were personal rivals who could only cooperate through a go-between, and Caesar was that man. Sometime between July 60 and January 59 BC the three struck a secret pact to back one another. They would probably have called it simply friendship (*amicitia*), the normal Roman word for a political alliance.

## Caesar's Stormy Consulship, 59 BC {#caesar-consulship}

Caesar began his year as consul politely. He drafted a carefully balanced land law (*lex agraria*) to settle Pompey's veterans and poor citizens, and offered the Senate changes to win its support. A leading conservative senator talked endlessly to block it. Caesar had him dragged off to jail, then quickly released him when senators protested.

Caesar took the law to the people. His fellow consul, an opponent, declared: "You will not have this law this year, not even should you all want it!" Caesar then revealed his secret allies. Pompey said that if anyone raised a sword against the law, he would bring a shield.

When the fellow consul tried to block the vote, a mob threw him from the speakers' platform and broke his fasces, the bundle of rods around an axe carried before a consul as a symbol of his authority. The law passed. Caesar then cut the tax companies' debt by a third for Crassus and approved Pompey's eastern settlement. Pompey married Caesar's daughter to seal the bond, and Caesar received a five-year command in Gaul.

His colleague shut himself at home, declaring bad omens. By summer, public opinion had turned against the three men.

## Renewal at Luca and the Consulship of 55 BC {#luca-renewal}

By 56 BC the alliance was fraying. In Rome, Pompey was harassed by a rabble-rousing tribune and frozen out by the nobility. In Gaul, Caesar was winning record honours for his conquests, but enemies threatened to strip him of his command.

Caesar met Crassus at Ravenna and Pompey at Luca, modern Lucca, then the southernmost town of Caesar's province in northern Italy. Some two hundred senators flocked there to curry favour. The deal suited all three: Pompey and Crassus would share the consulship again, take rich provinces, and extend Caesar's command.

Winning the election was not easy. An allied tribune vetoed elections until the year had run out. When they were finally held in 55 BC, Caesar's soldiers were in Rome on leave, rival candidates were driven off by force, and bribes flowed freely.

As consuls, Pompey and Crassus passed a law giving themselves five-year commands, Spain for Pompey and Syria for Crassus. Caesar's command was extended by five years.

The naked use of force backfired, confirming critics' warnings of tyranny. When Crassus left for Syria on 14 November 55 BC, a tribune solemnly cursed him at the city gate.

## Disaster at Carrhae, 53 BC {#carrhae}

Crassus, now about 62, craved military glory to match Pompey and Caesar. His target was the Parthian Empire, a power ruling Iran and Mesopotamia. Critics objected that Parthia had given Rome no reason for war.

According to an ancient biographer, Crassus raised seven legions, some 28,000 to 35,000 heavy infantry, plus light troops and 4,000 cavalry, including Gallic horsemen brought by his son. He twice refused the Armenian king's offer of a safer route through Armenia, and after early successes spent the winter in Syria, losing the advantage of surprise.

In spring 53 BC he marched into Mesopotamia. Near Carrhae, modern Harran in south-eastern Turkey, he met a Parthian army of about 10,000 cavalry, on 9 June according to a Roman poet. Drums thundered and armoured horsemen revealed gleaming steel. Mounted archers rained arrows on the Roman square, resupplied by a train of camels.

Crassus's son led a breakout, was lured away and killed; his head was paraded on a spear. The Romans retreated, abandoning 4,000 wounded. At truce talks a scuffle broke out and Crassus was killed. Legend says molten gold was poured down his throat. About 20,000 Romans died and 10,000 were captured, along with the legions' sacred eagles (*aquilae*).

## A "Triumvirate" That Never Was {#gang-of-three}

The alliance of Pompey, Crassus and Caesar is famous as the "First Triumvirate", from a Latin word for a board of three officials. But no ancient writer called it that, and it was never an official post. The term is first recorded in 1681 and many modern historians now avoid it as misleading.

Contemporaries had their own labels. Cicero, the orator who had crushed Catiline, complained of "three men" holding kingly power. A satirist called the trio a "three-headed monster". One modern popular historian prefers "Gang of Three".

How much did they really control? Since ancient times, some have seen their pact as the moment the free republic died: never before had three men had the power to impose private arrangements on the state. Others point out that the allies often lost elections, quarrelled constantly, and won big only when fully united, as in 59 and 55 BC.

Crassus's death at Carrhae in 53 BC turned a balanced trio into a two-man rivalry. Caesar, enriched by his Gallic victories, was no longer a junior partner. As Pompey sought new allies among Caesar's enemies, trust collapsed, and in January 49 BC the rivalry exploded into civil war.
