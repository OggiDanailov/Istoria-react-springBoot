# Late Republic: The Social War and Sulla (91-78 BCE)

## Rome and Its Italian Allies {#rome-and-its-allies}

By the early first century BC, Rome dominated the Italian peninsula without ruling it directly. Dozens of city-states and peoples were tied to Rome by separate treaties. These allies (*socii*) kept their land and ran their own affairs. In return, they had to send soldiers whenever Rome went to war.

That bargain made the allies indispensable. By 218 BC, Rome's armies held three allied soldiers for every two Romans. The allies shared in the plunder, and Rome often backed local elites against popular uprisings. Yet they still saw themselves as separate peoples. Among them were the Samnites, a tough mountain people of south-central Italy who had fought Rome for generations.

Tension grew after 133 BC, when Roman reformers began handing out public land to poor citizens. Much of that land had been claimed by Rome centuries earlier but left in allied hands. Italian communities now complained that Roman officials were seizing property their treaties guaranteed. Several proposals to offer the allies Roman citizenship (*civitas*) failed. Even that offer was complicated, because a person could hold only one citizenship. Becoming Roman meant giving up one's own community.

## The Revolt Begins, 91 BC {#outbreak-at-asculum}

The crisis came to a head in 91 BC. Drusus, a reforming tribune of the plebs (*tribunus plebis*), an official elected to champion ordinary citizens, pushed a complicated package of laws. At some point it included citizenship for the allies. His measures collapsed, and Drusus was murdered by an unknown assassin.

The allies had already been preparing in secret, exchanging hostages to guarantee each other's loyalty. Late in 91 or early in 90 BC, a Roman magistrate heard that Asculum, a town in Picenum near Italy's Adriatic coast, was swapping hostages. He rushed there and threatened the townspeople. Terrified of discovery, they killed him and every Roman in the town. With blood openly spilled, the allies rose together.

The rebels built a rival state they called Italia. According to an ancient summary most scholars accept, its capital was Corfinium, a town in the central Apennine mountains. It had a forum, a senate of 500 members, two consuls and twelve generals. Romans called the conflict the Marsic War, after the Marsi, a mountain people east of Rome. Our name comes from the later Latin *bellum sociale*, the "war of the allies."

## Italians Against Romans, 90–89 BC {#fighting-90-89-bc}

According to ancient writers, the rebels raised some 100,000 men. Rome answered with an estimated 140,000 in fourteen legions. The Latins, Rome's oldest allies, stayed loyal. Manpower was so short that freed slaves were enlisted for the first time.

The Italians struck first and hard. They knew Roman tactics intimately, and many Roman soldiers changed sides. At Nola, a town near Naples, most of the captured garrison joined the rebels. On 11 June 90 BC, a Roman consul was killed while leading raw recruits across the Tolenus River against the Marsi. So many corpses came home that the Senate (*senatus*) ordered future war dead buried on the battlefield.

The tide turned through old soldiers. Marius, the veteran general who had saved Italy from Germanic invaders, saw bodies floating downstream, forced the river and won. With help from Sulla, an ambitious aristocratic officer, he beat the Marsi again and split the rebels into northern and southern halves.

In 89 BC, Asculum fell after a long siege. In the south, Sulla took Pompeii and won the grass crown (*corona graminea*), Rome's highest military honour, near Nola. He then captured the rebels' new capital at Bovianum.

## Winning the War by Giving Way {#citizenship-laws}

Rome did not win by the sword alone. In October 90 BC, a consul passed the Julian Law (*lex Iulia*). It let any Italian community that had stayed loyal, or quickly laid down its arms, take up Roman citizenship. In 89 BC, a second law extended the offer to almost everyone. Only the Samnites and Lucanians, a people of the far south, still in the field were excluded. Support for the rebellion drained away.

The last organised rebel army was crushed in 88 BC, and its Marsic commander was killed. A stubborn band of Samnites and Lucanians fought on until 87 BC. Then Rome's own civil strife let them win citizenship by bargaining with one faction.

Citizenship brought a new fight. Romans voted in thirty-five voting tribes (*tribus*), and each tribe cast one vote regardless of size. Early plans packed the new citizens into a few new tribes, limiting their influence. Registration dragged on for decades and was completed only under Rome's first emperor. Meanwhile, Italy was scarred. Rome minted more silver coins than ever before to pay its troops, and the central mountains lay devastated.

## Why Did the Allies Fight? {#what-the-allies-wanted}

Historians still argue about what the rebels actually wanted. The fullest surviving narrative was written by a Greek historian more than two centuries later. He tells a clear story: the allies demanded Roman citizenship, were refused, and went to war to win it. In his words, they wanted to be "partners in rule rather than subjects." Most ancient writers agreed.

Since 1998, an influential modern historian has challenged this. He argues that writers living under the emperors, when citizenship was a prized privilege, projected their own values onto the past. Before the war there is little sign of Italians clamouring for citizenship, which would have cost them their independence. Instead, he suggests, the allies were angered by Roman land seizures and rose to throw off Roman domination altogether.

Some evidence supports him. The rebels set up their own state, and another ancient source speaks of allies risking their lives to win their own dominance. Yet his view has not won everyone over. One recent summary blends both sides: whether seeking citizenship or independence, the allies wanted equality and freedom, inside or outside Rome's system. No Italian account survives, apart from a few coins.

## Sulla: The Poor Aristocrat {#sulla-early-career}

Sulla was born in 138 BC into one of Rome's oldest noble families, but a family that had sunk into poverty. With almost no money, he spent his youth among actors, musicians and dancers, even writing comic farces. His later fortune, according to ancient writers, came from two inheritances: one from a loving stepmother and one from an older mistress.

Sulla had red-gold hair, blue eyes and a pale face blotched with red. He was charming and easy with ordinary people, yet capable of startling cruelty. A Roman historian called him "devoted to pleasure but more devoted to glory."

His career began in 107 BC as a junior officer under Marius, then fighting a rebellious king in Numidia, a kingdom in North Africa. Sulla won fame by persuading a neighbouring ruler to betray the king into Roman hands. Marius took the credit, and the two men's friendship soured into rivalry. Sulla served again in the wars against Germanic invaders and later governed a province in Asia Minor, modern Turkey. There he became the first Roman official to meet an envoy of Parthia, the great power beyond the Euphrates.

## The First March on Rome, 88 BC {#march-on-rome}

Fresh from victory in the Social War, Sulla was elected consul for 88 BC. The Senate gave him a rich and glorious prize: command against Mithridates, king of Pontus on the Black Sea's southern shore. Mithridates had overrun Rome's province in Asia and arranged the massacre of some 80,000 Romans and Italians living there.

Then a tribune proposed spreading the new Italian citizens across all thirty-five voting tribes. Sulla opposed him. The tribune struck a secret deal with Marius, who craved one more command. Marius would back the voting law, and in return the tribune would transfer the war against Mithridates to Marius. Armed gangs filled the streets, and Sulla was even forced to shelter in his rival's house.

The new law stripped Sulla of his command. He went to his army near Nola, warned the soldiers they would lose the plunder of the East, and led them on Rome. No Roman general had ever done this. His officers deserted him, and citizens hurled stones from the rooftops. But Sulla took the city and had Marius and eleven others declared public enemies (*hostes*). Marius fled to Africa. One modern historian calls the march a continuation of the Social War, now between citizens.

## War in the East and Civil War at Home, 87–82 BC {#east-and-civil-war}

In 87 BC, Sulla sailed for Greece. Behind him, Cinna, an enemy of Sulla elected consul for that year, joined forces with the returning Marius. Late in 87 BC they seized Rome, killed their opponents and declared Sulla an outlaw. Marius died soon after taking up another consulship, leaving Cinna in control.

Sulla ignored them. On 1 March 86 BC, he stormed and sacked Athens, which had sided with Mithridates. That summer he crushed huge armies of Mithridates at Chaeronea and Orchomenus in central Greece. In 85 BC he offered Mithridates a generous peace. Ancient critics called it a betrayal, made so Sulla could hurry home for revenge.

Cinna was killed by mutinous troops before he could stop him. In spring 83 BC, Sulla landed at Brundisium, a port in Italy's heel, with five legions of veterans. Many aristocrats flocked to him, and enemy armies melted away through desertion. The decisive battle came on 1 November 82 BC at the Colline Gate, on Rome's north-eastern edge, against a large Samnite and Lucanian army. Sulla's own wing broke, but his right wing won. The next day, while he addressed the Senate, thousands of prisoners were butchered within earshot.

## Dictator: Proscriptions and Reforms {#dictatorship-and-proscriptions}

Victorious, Sulla revived the office of dictator, unused for more than a century. This time the office came with no time limit and almost unlimited power. It also carried immunity for everything he had done or would do.

His most infamous weapon was the proscription (*proscriptio*), a public list of men condemned to death without trial. The first list held about 80 names, and hundreds more followed. Anyone could kill a listed man and claim a reward. His property went to the state, and his sons and grandsons were barred from office. Ancient writers say some 1,500 nobles died, and perhaps 9,000 people in all. Many were killed simply for their estates, which were auctioned cheaply to Sulla's friends.

Sulla then rebuilt the state in the Senate's favour. Tribunes lost the power to propose laws, and former tribunes could hold no further office. The Senate grew from 300 to 600 members, and senators alone staffed the jury courts. He tightened the treason law (*maiestas*) to keep provincial governors in line.

Then, around the start of 80 BC, he laid down the dictatorship. He dismissed his guards and walked unprotected through the Forum, offering to answer any citizen.

## Death and Legacy {#death-and-legacy}

After a final consulship in 80 BC, Sulla retired to his villa near Puteoli, a port on the Bay of Naples. There he wrote his memoirs and kept company with actors and musicians. He died in 78 BC. According to ancient writers, he burst a blood vessel while shouting for a corrupt local official to be strangled. Rome gave him a funeral grander than any for generations. His self-written epitaph boasted that no friend had ever served him, and no enemy wronged him, without being repaid in full.

His reforms crumbled quickly. Within a decade, the tribunes had regained their powers. His example outlasted his laws. He had shown that a general with a loyal army could seize Rome, and a later general reportedly asked, "If Sulla could, why can't I?"

Many historians trace this danger back to the Social War. It brought large armies back onto Italian soil for the first time in a century and taught soldiers to fight fellow Italians. It also blurred the line between Roman and enemy. One historian calls Rome's victory "a Pyrrhic one at best," since it produced both Sulla and an army willing to march on Rome.
