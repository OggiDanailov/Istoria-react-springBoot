# Late Republic: Caesar's Civil War and the Ides of March (49-44 BCE)

## The Road to War {#road-to-war}

For almost ten years Julius Caesar had been conquering Gaul, the vast land roughly matching modern France. By late 50 BC he was rich, famous and backed by devoted legions. The question gripping Rome was what would happen when his command ended.

Caesar had once been allied with Pompey, Rome's most celebrated general, but their partnership had frayed. As gang violence engulfed Rome, the Senate (*senatus*), the council of Rome's leading men, drew close to Pompey. Caesar wanted a second term as consul, Rome's highest office, before giving up his army. Pompey and the hardliners demanded he disarm first.

On 1 December 50 BC the Senate voted 370 to 22 that both men should lay down their commands, but Pompey and a consul rejected the vote. In January 49 BC the Senate passed its emergency final decree (*senatus consultum ultimum*). Pro-Caesar tribunes (*tribuni plebis*), officials elected to protect ordinary citizens, fled to his camp.

Caesar said he was defending his personal honour (*dignitas*). Modern historians doubt ancient claims that he feared prosecution. Pride on both sides seems to have tipped Rome into war.

## Crossing the Rubicon {#crossing-the-rubicon}

On 10 or 11 January 49 BC, Julius Caesar led his troops across the Rubicon, a small river marking the boundary between his province and Italy proper. That single act made him a rebel.

According to one ancient biographer, he declared "the die is cast" (*alea iacta est*). Another ancient writer says he quoted a Greek poet instead, and Caesar's own account never mentions the river at all.

Italy was unprepared, and towns opened their gates. Pompey, Caesar's former ally and now the Senate's champion, abandoned Rome with many senators. At Corfinium, a town in central Italy, a garrison surrendered after a week. Caesar freed some fifty senators and noblemen and even returned the soldiers' pay.

This mercy (*clementia*) was shrewd. Unlike earlier civil-war leaders, Caesar took no revenge and his troops did not plunder, so Italy did not turn on him. Pompey sailed from Brundisium, a port on the heel of Italy, to raise an army in the east. Caesar arrived on 9 March, too late to stop him.

## The Treasury, Spain and a Brief Dictatorship {#spain-and-the-treasury}

With his rival Pompey gone east, Julius Caesar turned west in spring 49 BC. First he stopped in Rome. When a tribune vetoed his plan to seize the state treasury, Caesar threatened the man's life until he gave way. The haul included some 15,000 gold bars and 30,000 silver bars.

Caesar left the episode out of his own account of the war. Some scholars see it as proof that his claim to be defending tribunes' rights was hollow.

Leaving Mark Antony, his trusted lieutenant, in charge of Italy, Caesar marched to Spain. In June he defeated Pompey's commanders at Ilerda, in north-east Spain, and the peninsula soon fell to him.

Back in Rome that December, he had himself named dictator, an emergency office with sweeping powers. He ran elections, won a second consulship, and resigned the dictatorship after just eleven days before setting off after Pompey.

## Dyrrachium and Pharsalus {#battle-of-pharsalus}

In January 48 BC Julius Caesar crossed the Adriatic, the sea between Italy and Greece. At Dyrrachium, a port in modern Albania, he tried to wall in the army of his rival Pompey. After months of skirmishing, Pompey broke out and forced him to retreat into Thessaly, in central Greece.

Pompey's overconfident allies pushed him to finish the war. On 9 August 48 BC the armies met near Pharsalus. By Caesar's count he had 22,000 legionaries against some 45,000, and Pompey had 5,000 to 7,000 cavalry to Caesar's 1,000.

Pompey planned for his infantry to hold while his cavalry swept round Caesar's flank. But Caesar had hidden a fourth line behind his right. When the horsemen charged, these men leapt up and jabbed their javelins at them. The cavalry fled, Pompey's left wing collapsed, and Pompey himself escaped.

Caesar claimed to have lost 200 men and killed 60,000; another ancient historian gives a likelier 1,200 and 6,000. Among those who sought pardon was Brutus, a young aristocrat whose mother had been Caesar's lover. Caesar welcomed him warmly.

## Pompey's Death and Cleopatra {#pompey-and-cleopatra}

After his defeat at Pharsalus, Pompey, once Rome's greatest general, fled to Egypt. A boat came out carrying Egyptian officials and two Roman officers who had served under him. Once aboard, he was stabbed to death in sight of his wife, on the orders of Egypt's boy king.

Julius Caesar reached Alexandria, Egypt's capital, three days later and was presented with Pompey's head and signet ring. He wept at the ring and recoiled from the head, though historians debate whether his grief was genuine.

The king was feuding with his sister Cleopatra, named his co-ruler in their father's will. Caesar demanded money to pay his troops and offered to settle the quarrel. The king's regent answered by besieging him in the palace quarter. Cleopatra slipped secretly into the palace and became Caesar's lover.

When reinforcements arrived, Caesar crushed the Egyptians, and the king drowned in flight. Caesar made Cleopatra queen alongside a younger brother and cruised the Nile with her for three months. In 47 BC she bore a son, and Caesar, believing the boy was his, let him carry the Caesar name.

## I Came, I Saw, I Conquered: Zela and Thapsus {#zela-and-thapsus}

In mid-47 BC Julius Caesar left Egypt to punish a king of Pontus, a realm on the Black Sea coast of modern Turkey, who had seized Roman-held lands. Near the hilltop town of Zela the king attacked while Caesar's men were digging camp, but they rallied and routed him. Caesar summed up the brief campaign in a letter: "I came, I saw, I conquered" (*veni, vidi, vici*).

A new Pompeian army had gathered in North Africa, backed by the king of Numidia, roughly modern Algeria, with some 120 war elephants. According to an anecdote, Caesar tripped on landing and laughed off the bad omen by grabbing sand and crying, "I have hold of you, Africa!"

At Thapsus, in modern Tunisia, in 46 BC, Caesar's veterans charged before he gave the order; one ancient writer says he was having an epileptic fit. Some 10,000 Pompeians died, against about 50 of Caesar's men.

In nearby Utica, Caesar's most uncompromising enemy in the Senate stabbed himself. When a doctor stitched the wound, he tore it open and died. Caesar regretted losing the chance to pardon him.

## Triumphs, Munda and Dictator for Life {#munda-and-honours}

Back in Rome in 46 BC, Julius Caesar celebrated four triumphs (*triumphi*), the grand victory parades of Roman generals. Seventy-two attendants (*lictores*) marched before him carrying the fasces, bundles of rods around an axe that symbolised the power to punish. That was three times a dictator's usual number.

Then came rebellion in Spain, led by the sons of his dead rival Pompey. In 45 BC the armies met on a ridge near Munda, in southern Spain. Caesar's men wavered and he rushed to the front himself. One ancient writer says he had often fought for victory, but now for the first time for his life. He won, losing around 1,000 men, and Pompey's elder son was caught and beheaded.

A flattering Senate heaped on honours: fifty days of thanksgiving, the title "Father of his Country" (*parens patriae*), and the renaming of his birth month, Quinctilis, which became July. Early in 44 BC he was made dictator for life (*dictator perpetuo*).

## Three Last Straws {#three-last-straws}

Ancient writers picked out three incidents of 45 and 44 BC as the last straws that turned senators against Julius Caesar.

First, a delegation of senators came to present him with new honours. Etiquette demanded he stand; he stayed seated and joked that his honours should be cut back. It looked as if he no longer cared about the Senate.

Second, in January 44 BC, a diadem, a symbol of kingship, appeared on Caesar's statue in the Forum, Rome's main public square, and two tribunes removed it. Soon after, some in a crowd hailed him as king (*rex*). He punned, "I am not Rex, but Caesar," since Rex was also a Roman family name. When the tribunes arrested the first man to shout, Caesar had them stripped of office, angering ordinary Romans.

Third, at the Lupercalia, a February festival, Mark Antony, Caesar's loyal lieutenant and fellow consul, twice placed a diadem on his head. The crowd fell silent. Caesar set it aside, declaring that Jupiter alone was king of the Romans, and the crowd cheered. Many suspected he was testing support for a crown.

## The Ides of March {#ides-of-march}

On 22 February 44 BC, Brutus, the aristocrat Caesar had pardoned after Pharsalus, met his brother-in-law, a fellow senator. They agreed that Julius Caesar must be stopped from becoming king. About sixty men joined them, many of them Caesar's own officers.

Some wanted to kill Mark Antony, Caesar's fellow consul, too. Brutus refused, arguing that killing only Caesar would look like removing a tyrant rather than a purge. They chose a Senate meeting, where Caesar's friends could not protect him. The last one before he left to fight the Parthians, a great eastern empire, fell on the Ides of March (*Idus Martiae*), 15 March.

According to tradition, a seer had warned Caesar of danger by the Ides, and his wife begged him to stay home after a nightmare. He went anyway, to a hall in the Theatre of Pompey. As one plotter pulled down his toga, the rest closed in. Stabbed 23 times, he fell near Pompey's statue.

His last words are disputed. Some ancient writers say he spoke none; others report "You too, child?" in Greek. One says he covered his face on seeing Brutus.

## Aftermath and the Fall of the Republic {#aftermath-and-debate}

The killers of Julius Caesar marched through Rome proclaiming freedom, but citizens hid indoors, and angry crowds later burned the hall where he died. Mark Antony, Caesar's fellow consul, brokered a compromise: the assassins would go unpunished, but Caesar's appointments would stand.

Caesar's will named as heir his 18-year-old grandnephew, who took the Caesar name. In 43 BC he joined Mark Antony and another Caesarian general in the Second Triumvirate, a three-man ruling alliance. They declared Caesar a god, revived murderous purges of their enemies, and defeated Brutus at Philippi, in northern Greece, in 42 BC.

The alliance collapsed. Antony, who had married Cleopatra, queen of Egypt and Caesar's former lover, was beaten at Actium, off western Greece, in 31 BC, and both took their own lives. In 27 BC the heir became Rome's first emperor.

Historians still debate the war's role. Some see it as the blow that broke the Republic, triggered by miscalculation rather than design. Others call it a symptom of a Republic already unable to reform itself.
