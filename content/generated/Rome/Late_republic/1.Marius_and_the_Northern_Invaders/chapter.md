# Late Republic: Marius and the Northern Invaders (121-100 BCE)

## Gaius Marius: The Outsider from Arpinum {#marius-origins}

Gaius Marius, the outsider who would be elected to Rome's highest office more often than any man before him, was born around 157 BC near Arpinum, a hill town south-east of Rome. His hometown had only gained full Roman citizenship in 188 BC. One ancient biographer claimed his father was a humble labourer, but modern historians reject this. The family was locally prominent and wealthy, probably from large landholdings, and could afford to launch two sons into politics.

In Rome, though, Marius was a "new man" (*novus homo*), the first of his family to reach high office, and the old aristocracy never let him forget it. He first made his name as a young officer in 134 BC at the siege of Numantia, a Celtic stronghold in Spain. According to a later story, someone asked the commander there who could ever replace him. He tapped Marius on the shoulder and said, "Perhaps this is the man."

His climb was bumpy. In 119 BC he served as a tribune of the plebs (*tribunus plebis*), an official elected to protect ordinary citizens. He passed a law narrowing the walkways where voters cast their ballots, so that the rich could no longer peer at their choices. Later he lost one election and scraped into a senior judicial post in last place. He was then charged with electoral bribery (*ambitus*), though he was acquitted. A governorship in Spain made him rich, and marriage into an ancient but faded aristocratic family gave him respectability.

## Jugurtha and the Price of Rome {#jugurtha}

Jugurtha, the Numidian prince who believed every Roman had a price, started the war that made Marius famous. Numidia was a kingdom on the north African coast, roughly modern Algeria, and a loyal ally of Rome since the wars against Carthage (*Carthago*). Its old king died in 118 BC. He left the realm jointly to his two sons and to his nephew and adopted son, Jugurtha, who had served with Roman officers in Spain and knew how Romans thought.

Jugurtha had one cousin assassinated and defeated the other, who fled to Rome to beg the Senate (*senatus*), Rome's council of elder statesmen, for help. A Roman commission divided the kingdom, but Jugurtha bribed its members into giving him the richer western half. In 113 BC he attacked again and besieged his cousin in the city of Cirta. When it surrendered, he killed his cousin along with the Italian traders who had helped defend it.

Under popular pressure, Rome declared war in 111 BC. The results were humiliating. A consul, one of the two chief magistrates (*consules*) elected each year, accepted a lenient peace that reeked of bribery. Then another Roman army was trapped and forced to pass under the yoke (*iugum*), a frame of spears that beaten soldiers had to stoop beneath. Many Romans concluded that their aristocratic leaders could simply be bought.

## Metellus and the Battle of the Muthul {#metellus-and-the-muthul}

After years of disgrace, the Senate sent Metellus, an aristocrat with a reputation for honesty and courage, to take over the war against Jugurtha, the defiant king of Numidia in north Africa. Metellus arrived in 109 BC, retrained a slack and undisciplined army, and chose his officers for ability rather than rank. His senior officer and right-hand man was Gaius Marius, the ambitious newcomer from Arpinum, who joined partly to boost his own chances of becoming consul.

That summer Jugurtha laid a clever trap near the Muthul River. He cut the Romans off from the water they needed and caught them on open desert, where his nimble light cavalry had every advantage. The Numidian horsemen broke the Roman army into isolated groups, each fighting for its life.

Marius kept his head. He gathered several scattered detachments and led a column of about 2,000 men through the enemy to link up with Metellus. Together they seized a hill held by Numidian infantry, then struck the enemy cavalry from behind. The Numidians withdrew, and Marius's actions probably saved the army from destruction.

Yet the victory settled nothing. Jugurtha escaped, dismissed his weaker troops and switched to guerrilla warfare. Metellus took town after town and even stormed a desert fortress after a forty-day siege, but the king slipped away every time.

## Marius Seizes the Consulship and the Command {#marius-becomes-consul}

By 108 BC Gaius Marius, the new man serving under the aristocrat Metellus in the war against Jugurtha, wanted to go home and run for consul, the highest office in Rome. Metellus refused. Allegedly he sneered that Marius should wait and stand alongside Metellus's own son, who was then about twenty. Ancient writers say a fortune-teller in the African city of Utica urged Marius to trust the gods and test his luck as often as he could.

Marius campaigned anyway, from the camp. He ate with the ordinary soldiers and shared their labours. He told Italian merchants in Africa that with half the army he could capture Jugurtha within days. Soldiers and traders alike wrote home praising him and grumbling about Metellus's slow, methodical strategy. Eventually Metellus let him go.

Marius was elected consul for 107 BC. The Senate tried to keep Metellus in charge by extending his command, but a tribune allied to Marius took the question to the Assembly of the plebs (*concilium plebis*), the popular voting body. It gave the war to Marius instead. Metellus sailed home in fury and received a victory parade and the honorary name Numidicus.

Historians see this as a turning point. Using popular votes to overrule the Senate on military commands became a habit, and ambitious generals after Marius copied it, fuelling bitter personal rivalries.

## Sulla and the Capture of Jugurtha {#capture-of-jugurtha}

Gaius Marius, now consul and commander against the Numidian king Jugurtha, needed fresh troops in 107 BC. Normally only men who owned property could serve in the legions. Marius instead appealed for volunteers, including veterans and men with no property at all, the so-called "head count" (*capite censi*). His cavalry was entrusted to his new financial officer, Sulla, a young aristocrat with a playboy reputation who would one day become Marius's deadliest rival.

Winning proved harder than Marius had boasted. He made a dangerous desert march to the town of Capsa in the far south and, after it surrendered, killed its adult men and enslaved the rest. In 106 BC, near Serif, he was ambushed by Jugurtha and his father-in-law, the king of Mauretania, a realm in what is now Morocco. The Romans held two hills until nightfall, then routed the enemy with a surprise attack on their camp at dawn.

Fighting alone could not catch Jugurtha, so Marius turned to diplomacy. Sulla bravely entered the Mauretanian king's camp, and the king handed over Jugurtha in chains. In 104 BC Jugurtha was paraded in Marius's triumph (*triumphus*), the grand victory procession through Rome, and later died in an underground prison. But Sulla wore a ring showing the capture, and an ancient biographer called the quarrel over credit "the first seed" of their hatred.

## The Cimbri and the Disaster at Arausio {#cimbri-and-arausio}

While Rome fought Jugurtha in Africa, a far greater danger was gathering in the north. The Cimbri, a Germanic people from the Jutland peninsula in modern Denmark, were on the move with their neighbours the Teutones. Some Roman writers said floods drove them from their homes around 120 to 115 BC, though an ancient geographer thought this unlikely.

In 113 BC the migrants reached Noricum, an Alpine region in modern Austria. A Roman consul ordered them to leave and they began to comply, until they discovered he had laid an ambush. Enraged, they annihilated his army at Noreia. Then they turned west into Gaul, beating a Roman army there in 109 BC. In 107 BC their allies the Tigurini, a Celtic tribe, destroyed another army and killed a consul.

In 105 BC Rome gathered more than 80,000 soldiers, possibly the largest force it had ever sent into battle, near Arausio, modern Orange in southern France. The two commanders loathed each other and camped on opposite banks of the Rhône. One attacked alone and was wiped out; the other's isolated army was then destroyed. Only a few hundred Romans escaped.

Arausio was Rome's worst defeat since Hannibal's victory at Cannae over a century earlier. Panic swept the city, and Romans spoke of the "Cimbrian terror" (*terror cimbricus*), expecting the barbarians at the gates at any moment.

## Five Consulships in a Row {#consul-again-and-again}

After the catastrophe at Arausio, Rome turned to the one general who had recently won a war: Gaius Marius, the conqueror of Jugurtha. He was still in Africa when voters elected him consul for 104 BC. Standing in absence, and so soon after his first term, broke the usual rules, but the Assembly simply set them aside.

Marius had to rebuild the army in Gaul almost from scratch. Again waiving the property requirement, he raised about 30,000 Romans and 40,000 Italian allies and auxiliaries. He based them near Aquae Sextiae, modern Aix-en-Provence, and drilled them hard. He also built a network of spies and negotiated with the Gallic tribes along the frontier. His former officer Sulla served on his staff, a sign that the two men were not yet enemies.

Luck helped. Instead of marching on Italy, the Cimbri wandered into Spain, giving Marius precious time. Rome kept re-electing him, partly to avoid another clash between rival commanders like the one that had doomed Arausio. Re-election for 102 BC was not certain, but a public appeal from Saturninus, a young tribune and rabble-rousing champion of the poor, helped carry him.

In the end Marius held five consulships in a row, from 104 to 100 BC, on top of his first in 107 BC. Seen as necessary at the time, they placed unprecedented power in one man's hands for an unheard-of length of time.

## Victory at Aquae Sextiae and Vercellae {#aquae-sextiae-and-vercellae}

In 102 BC the northern tribes finally moved on Italy along separate routes. Gaius Marius, Rome's repeatedly elected consul, went west to meet the Teutones and their allies the Ambrones, while his fellow consul guarded the Alps.

Marius refused battle and beat off attacks on his fortified camp. The Teutones then simply marched past. Ancient writers claimed their column took six days to file by, the warriors jeering and asking what messages the Romans wanted delivered to their wives. Marius shadowed them. Near Aquae Sextiae a scuffle between Roman servants fetching water and bathing Ambrones exploded into battle, and the Ambrones were crushed. Days later the Teutones charged uphill at the Roman position, only to be hit from behind by about 3,000 men hidden nearby. Ancient estimates of those killed or captured ran from 100,000 to 200,000.

Meanwhile the Cimbri forced the Alpine passes and spread across northern Italy. In 101 BC Marius joined his colleague at Vercellae, on the Raudine Plain. Sulla's cavalry surprised the Cimbri, Roman infantry pinned them, and Marius struck their flank. More than 120,000 reportedly died, and many of their women killed themselves and their children rather than face slavery.

Afterwards Marius granted citizenship to about a thousand Italian allied soldiers without asking the Senate. An ancient biographer says he was hailed as Rome's "third founder", though modern historians doubt the story.

## Saturninus and the Bloody Year 100 BC {#saturninus-and-the-sixth-consulship}

In 100 BC Gaius Marius, saviour of Rome from the northern tribes, held his sixth consulship. He wanted land for his veterans and proper credit for his victories. His partner was Saturninus, a radical tribune serving his second term, who pushed laws offering cheaper state grain, farmland for veterans, including Italian allies, and exile for Marius's old enemy Metellus, his former commander in Africa.

Marius helped pass the land law and drive Metellus out, then grew uneasy with his allies' methods. When Saturninus and an ally had a rival candidate for consul murdered during the elections, the Senate passed its emergency decree (*senatus consultum ultimum*), telling the magistrates to do whatever was needed to restore order. For the first time, such a decree targeted serving officials rather than private citizens.

Marius rejected the idea of bringing an army near the city. Instead he rallied volunteers and veterans, cut off the water supply to the Capitoline Hill where the rebels held out, and forced their surrender. He locked them in the Senate house to await trial, but a mob climbed onto the roof, tore off the tiles and stoned the prisoners to death, possibly with Marius's tacit approval.

The episode won Marius little. Metellus's relatives followed him around in mourning clothes demanding the exile's recall, and Marius drifted into semi-retirement as an elder statesman.

## Did Marius Really Reform the Roman Army? {#marian-reforms-debate}

For generations, textbooks credited Gaius Marius, the general who defeated Jugurtha and the northern tribes, with the "Marian reforms". The idea began with German scholars in the 1840s. Supposedly Marius replaced citizen militias with professional soldiers drawn from the poorest class (*proletarii*). These men looked to their generals for loot and land, and eventually helped them overthrow the Republic.

Today most specialists call this "a construct of modern scholarship". His recruitment of propertyless volunteers in 107 BC seems to have been a one-off. Those men were discharged after Africa, and the army Marius took over against the Cimbri had been raised in the traditional way. Rome kept relying mainly on conscription, and late Republican armies still came mostly from the countryside.

Other changes credited to him fare no better. Citizen cavalry still appears in later evidence, and eagle standards shared the field with wolf and ox standards for decades. If Marius redesigned the heavy javelin, archaeology suggests his design was soon dropped, and no ancient source says he introduced the larger infantry units called cohorts. Making soldiers carry their own gear, the famous "Marius's mules" (*muli Mariani*), was routine for Roman generals.

Much of the old picture rests on two ancient authors, a Roman historian of the war and a later Greek biographer, both moralising critics. Some modern historians argue instead that brutal civil fighting with Italian allies, not Marius, taught Roman soldiers to turn their swords on fellow Romans.
