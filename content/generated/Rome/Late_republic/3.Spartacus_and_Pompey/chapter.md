# Late Republic: Spartacus and Pompey (78-63 BCE)

## Rome's Slave Economy {#roman-slavery}

In the 2nd and 1st centuries BC, Rome's wars of conquest flooded Italy with captives. Tens or even hundreds of thousands of enslaved people could arrive at once. A few became servants or craftsmen, but most were sent to the mines or to the vast farming estates of Sicily and southern Italy, where they did back-breaking work for life.

Under the law of the Republic, a slave was property, not a person. An owner could beat, maim or even kill his own slaves without any legal consequence.

Such misery bred revolt. In 135 and 104 BC, great uprisings broke out on Sicily, the large island off the toe of Italy. These became known as the First and Second Servile Wars, from the Latin word for slave (*servus*). Small rebel bands quickly gathered tens of thousands of followers, and it took Rome years of fighting to crush them. Still, the Senate (*senatus*), Rome's governing council, never saw them as a real danger to the Republic.

Italy itself had never seen a slave rising, and nobody imagined slaves threatening the city of Rome. That changed in 73 BC with the third and last of these wars, which ancient writers also called the Gladiator War, or the War of Spartacus.

## The Escape from Capua {#escape-from-capua}

Gladiator fights were among Rome's favourite entertainments. Prisoners of war and condemned criminals were trained to fight in special schools (*ludi*). In 73 BC, about 200 gladiators at a school in Capua, a wealthy city south of Rome, plotted to escape. The plot was betrayed, but some 70 men grabbed kitchen choppers and roasting spits, fought their way out and seized wagons loaded with gladiators' weapons.

They chose three leaders: two Gauls and Spartacus, the man history remembers. Ancient writers called him a Thracian, from the Balkan lands north of Greece. Some said he had served Rome as an auxiliary soldier before being enslaved; others that he was a captive. Since "Thracian" was also a style of gladiator, the label may describe how he fought rather than where he came from. His name, though, closely matches those of Thracian kings and means "renowned by the spear".

The runaways beat a small force from Capua, plundered the countryside and retreated to Mount Vesuvius, the volcano above the Bay of Naples. A praetor, a senior magistrate, blocked the only path down with 3,000 hastily gathered militiamen and waited for hunger to do his work. Instead, the rebels wove ropes and ladders from wild vines, climbed down the cliffs on the far side, and wiped out his force from behind. A second praetor's army fared no better. Herdsmen and shepherds flocked to the rebels, who soon numbered some 70,000.

## Humiliating the Consuls {#consular-defeats}

By spring 72 BC, Spartacus' slave army was heading north towards Cisalpine Gaul, the Roman province in Italy's far north below the Alps. The alarmed Senate sent both consuls (*consules*), the Republic's two highest elected officials, each at the head of trained legions.

At first the Romans did well. One consul caught a breakaway band of about 30,000 rebels near Mount Garganus in south-eastern Italy, led by one of the Gallic gladiators who had escaped with Spartacus. Two-thirds of them died, including their leader.

After that, our two main ancient accounts tell different stories. One historian says Spartacus beat both consular armies in turn, then forced 300 Roman prisoners to fight each other to the death as gladiators to avenge his fallen comrade. Burning his baggage and killing his pack animals to move faster, he marched north with some 120,000 followers and beat the consuls again. Then, this writer claims, he thought about attacking Rome but decided his army was not ready, and turned back south to raid and trade plunder for bronze and iron to make weapons.

The other account, by a Greek biographer, says Spartacus defeated one consul and then a governor's army of 10,000 near Mutina, modern Modena. He adds that the Senate then recalled both consuls.

## What Did Spartacus Want? {#spartacus-aims}

Because the revolt failed, no rebel ever wrote down what Spartacus and his followers hoped to achieve. Everything we know comes from Roman and Greek writers, and they disagree.

One ancient biographer believed Spartacus simply wanted to cross the Alps and send his men home to their own lands. Two other ancient historians wrote that he meant to march on Rome itself. Modern historians suspect that this may reflect Roman fears more than rebel plans. And if Spartacus really aimed for the Alps, nobody explains why he turned back south after reaching northern Italy.

Many popular modern tellings describe a split between Spartacus, who wanted freedom beyond the mountains, and a Gallic leader who preferred to stay and plunder Italy. The ancient sources do not contradict this, but there is no direct evidence for it either.

Films and novels often show the rebels as freedom fighters out to abolish slavery. No ancient writer says this was their goal, and nothing the leaders did seems aimed at ending slavery as such. It is not even certain that Spartacus was the single commander. Other chiefs are named, and they may have been his equals, leading their own bands alongside his.

## Crassus Takes Command {#crassus-takes-command}

By 71 BC the Senate turned in desperation to Crassus, the immensely rich politician who now took on the slave army. He had commanded troops for the dictator who seized Rome in a civil war in 82 BC. Crassus received eight legions (*legiones*), perhaps 32,000 to 48,000 trained soldiers.

He ruled them with terror. He revived decimation (*decimatio*), an ancient punishment in which one soldier in every ten was chosen by lot and executed. Accounts differ on scale. One writer says 50 men of a single unit died after an officer disobeyed orders and lost a battle; another suggests up to 4,000 may have been killed. Either way, his men learned that Crassus was more dangerous to them than the enemy.

The tide turned. Crassus won several battles and drove the rebels south to the narrow strait facing Sicily. According to tradition, Spartacus paid pirates from Cilicia, on the southern coast of modern Turkey, to ferry 2,000 men to the island to start a new slave rising there. The pirates took the money and sailed away. Crassus then built fortifications right across the peninsula near Rhegium, the city at Italy's toe, trapping the rebels and cutting off their supplies.

## The Last Battle and the Crosses {#silarius-and-aftermath}

Trapped by Crassus' fortifications at the toe of Italy, Spartacus heard that Pompey, Rome's most celebrated young general, was marching home from Spain with his legions. More troops were landing at Brundisium, the port on Italy's heel. Spartacus offered to negotiate. Crassus, desperate not to share the glory, refused. The rebels smashed through his lines and fled north, but they were exhausted and their discipline was collapsing.

Spartacus turned and threw his whole army at the legions in a final battle by the Silarius River in southern Italy. The rebels were routed, and most died on the field. Every ancient historian says Spartacus fell fighting, though his body was never found.

About 6,000 captured survivors were crucified along the Appian Way (*Via Appia*), the great road from Rome to Capua. Pompey's troops caught and killed 5,000 fugitives, and he then told the Senate that while Crassus had beaten the slaves, he himself had ended the war. Crassus never forgave him.

Both generals camped their armies outside Rome and stood for the consulship. Pompey was legally too young, but both were elected for 70 BC, helped by the unspoken threat of their soldiers. Some historians think that, out of fear, Romans afterwards began treating their slaves less harshly.

## Pompey, the Teenage Butcher {#young-pompey}

Pompey, the general who stole Crassus' glory in the Spartacus war, was born in 106 BC in Picenum, a region on Italy's Adriatic coast. In 83 BC, still in his early twenties, he raised a private legion from his father's veterans and joined the general who went on to make himself dictator of Rome.

Pompey won battles in Italy and then hunted down the dictator's enemies in Sicily and North Africa. His execution of a captured former consul earned him the nickname "teenage butcher" (*adulescentulus carnifex*). His own soldiers, however, hailed him as "the Great" (*Magnus*), after Alexander the Great, and he made the name officially his. He even forced the dictator to grant him a triumph (*triumphus*), Rome's grand victory parade, at an unheard-of young age.

Normally a Roman climbed a fixed ladder of elected offices (*cursus honorum*) before commanding armies. Pompey skipped it. In 77 BC he helped crush a rebel consul's march on Rome. He was then sent to Spain to fight a brilliant renegade Roman general who had united local tribes against Rome. That rebel beat Pompey more than once, but after five years of grinding warfare the rebellion collapsed, and Pompey returned to Italy just in time for Spartacus' final defeat in 71 BC.

## Clearing the Seas of Pirates {#war-on-the-pirates}

By the 60s BC, pirate fleets roamed the whole Mediterranean, many of them based in Cilicia, on the southern coast of modern Turkey. They sometimes allied with Rome's enemies, and some historians argue they were closer to an organised hostile power than to mere outlaws. In 68 BC they raided Ostia, Rome's own port, and kidnapped two senators.

In 67 BC a tribune of the plebs (*tribunus plebis*), an official elected to defend ordinary citizens, proposed the Gabinian Law (*lex Gabinia*) at Pompey's urging. It gave Pompey sweeping command for three years over every province within about 75 km (50 miles) of the Mediterranean, with the right to appoint deputies and spend huge sums. The Senate feared giving one man such power, but the people voted it through.

Pompey divided the sea into zones, each patrolled by a deputy, so the pirates had nowhere to flee. He cleared the western Mediterranean in 40 days, then drove the pirates back to their Cilician strongholds and stormed the last of them. The whole war took just three months.

Most pirates surrendered without a fight, trusting his reputation for mercy. Pompey resettled them in towns ruined by war, where they stayed loyal to him for years.

## Lucullus and the War with Mithridates {#lucullus-and-mithridates}

Mithridates, king of Pontus, a realm on the southern shore of the Black Sea, was Rome's most stubborn enemy in the East. Rome had already fought him twice. In 88 BC he had ordered the massacre of Roman and Italian settlers across Asia Minor, modern Turkey; ancient writers claim some 80,000 died.

The third and longest war, lasting from 73 to 63 BC, began when the king of Bithynia, in north-western Asia Minor, left his kingdom to Rome at his death. Mithridates invaded at once. Rome sent the consul Lucullus, a skilled general and lover of Greek culture.

Lucullus trapped the king's huge army as it besieged the city of Cyzicus, and famine, disease and Roman attacks destroyed it. Ancient writers say only 20,000 of perhaps 300,000 men survived. Lucullus then invaded Pontus, routed Mithridates at Cabira, and chased him into Armenia, ruled by the king's son-in-law. In 69 BC Lucullus crushed the Armenian army outside its new capital, Tigranocerta, despite being outnumbered more than two to one.

But his weary soldiers grumbled that only their general was getting rich. They mutinied and refused to march against Mithridates, who recovered his kingdom. Rome decided Lucullus had to be replaced.

## Pompey Conquers the East {#pompey-in-the-east}

In 66 BC a tribune passed the Manilian Law (*lex Manilia*), handing Pompey, fresh from his triumph over the pirates, supreme command against Mithridates, the defiant king of Pontus. The bitter Lucullus, the general he replaced, called Pompey a vulture who fed on other men's work, mocking his claim to have finished off Spartacus as well.

Pompey was a superb organiser. He persuaded the king of Parthia, the great empire beyond the Euphrates, to attack Armenia, then cornered Mithridates at the river Lycus and defeated him. The king of Armenia surrendered, and his kingdom became a dependent ally of Rome. Mithridates fled to the Crimea and planned yet another war, but in 63 BC his own son led a revolt against him. According to tradition, his poison failed because he had long taken small doses to build immunity, so he had his bodyguard kill him with a sword.

Meanwhile Pompey turned Syria into a Roman province in 64 BC and in 63 BC captured Jerusalem after a three-month siege. He entered the Temple's most sacred chamber, which only the high priest could enter, an act Jews saw as desecration, though he left its treasures untouched. Pompey returned to Rome with an enormous fortune and unmatched prestige.
