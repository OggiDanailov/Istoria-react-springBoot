# Late Republic: Caesar in Gaul (58-50 BCE)

## Caesar's Road to Gaul {#caesar-goes-to-gaul}

In 59 BC Julius Caesar served as consul, the Roman Republic's highest office, and finished his year deep in debt. He belonged to an informal power-sharing pact, later called the First Triumvirate. His partners were Pompey, Rome's most celebrated general, and Crassus, its richest man.

Their backing won Caesar the governorship (*proconsul*) of three regions. These were northern Italy, Illyricum on the eastern Adriatic coast, and Transalpine Gaul, a Roman province along France's Mediterranean shore. His term ran for an unusual five years instead of one. That was long enough to fight a real war.

Beyond his province lay the rest of Gaul, roughly modern France, Belgium and Switzerland. Its tribes were prosperous. They minted coins and traded grain, iron and slaves for Roman wine. Yet Romans had never forgotten the Gauls' sack of their own city in 390 BC.

Historians agree that Caesar wanted conquest mainly for plunder and glory. In 58 BC a migrating Gallic people handed him the justification for war (*casus belli*) he needed.

## Legions Against Warriors {#legions-and-warriors}

Rome's army was a disciplined, permanent force of heavy infantry. It was organised into legions (*legiones*) of around 5,000 men. Each legion was split into ten cohorts (*cohortes*) of 480 soldiers. The cohort had replaced the smaller maniple (*manipulus*), and it put rich and poor side by side in the same unit.

The Gauls were a warrior culture that prized personal bravery. Men supplied their own gear, and raids on neighbours kept their skills sharp. Some fought in ornate armour, others even naked. Usually taller than the Romans, they carried longer swords and had far better cavalry. United, their armies could match Rome's. The trouble was that the tribes rarely united, and Caesar exploited their rivalries throughout the war.

Rome's weak point was supply. Each soldier carried a few days' rations. Even so, a legion needed around a thousand pack animals, its baggage train (*impedimenta*), which stretched some 4 km (2.5 miles) on the march. Again and again, hunger dictated Caesar's moves.

## 58 BC: The Helvetii and the Suebi {#helvetii-and-suebi}

The Helvetii, a group of related tribes on the Swiss plateau, were being squeezed by Germanic peoples. On 28 March 58 BC they set off westward with their families and herds. They burned their villages so there could be no turning back.

Julius Caesar, the Roman governor, refused them passage through his province, so they turned north, away from Roman land. He followed and attacked them anyway. He slaughtered those still crossing the river Saône, then met the main force near Bibracte, chief town of the Aedui, a Gallic tribe allied to Rome. When the Romans were surrounded, Caesar had his rear line turn about and fight on two fronts, and he won. His men then killed fighters, women and children in the enemy camps.

Next Caesar turned on the king of the Suebi, a Germanic people from across the Rhine. Only the year before, the Senate (*senatus*) had named this king a friend of Rome. Gallic allies complained of the king's conquests. Caesar beat him, helped by a cavalry charge led by the son of Crassus, and drove him back over the Rhine.

## 57-56 BC: The Belgae, the Nervii and the Veneti {#belgae-nervii-veneti}

Caesar's victories convinced many Gauls, correctly, that he meant to take everything. In 57 BC he marched with 32,000 to 40,000 men against the Belgae, a league of tribes in roughly modern Belgium. Their army, short of food, broke up and went home. The mere sight of Roman siege works, unknown in Gaul, made several tribes surrender without a fight.

The Nervii, a Belgic tribe, fought back. They ambushed the Romans on the river Sambre while the legions were building camp. Two legions were still about 15 km (9 miles) behind. On the Roman right wing the men were packed too tightly to swing their swords, and many officers fell. Caesar grabbed a shield and joined the front line until reinforcements turned the battle. The Senate later voted him a 15-day public thanksgiving (*supplicatio*), the longest yet.

In 56 BC the Veneti, seafaring tribes of Brittany, seized Roman officers sent to collect grain. Their sturdy oak sailing ships ruled the Atlantic until a battle near Quiberon Bay. There Roman grappling hooks shredded their rigging, and then the wind died, leaving them helpless against oared Roman galleys. Caesar executed their elders and sold the rest into slavery.

## 55-54 BC: Across the Rhine and into Britain {#rhine-and-britain}

In 56 BC Caesar renewed his pact with Pompey and Crassus. He got five more years in Gaul, and his two partners got the consulship for 55 BC. With these rivals in the spotlight, Caesar needed headlines. He chose to do what no Roman army had done before: cross both the Rhine and the English Channel.

First, two peoples driven from their lands by the Germanic Suebi crossed the Rhine in search of land and defeated a Roman cavalry force. Caesar then fell on their undefended camp, killing men, women and children. His claim of 430,000 dead is impossible. Still, the massacre was bad enough that his enemies in the Senate wanted to prosecute him. Next he bridged the Rhine with timber in ten days, raided for 18 days, and burned the bridge behind him.

In late August he landed in Britain with two legions. His men hesitated before the waiting Britons. Then the standard bearer (*signifer*) of the Tenth Legion leapt into the sea, and the soldiers followed rather than lose their standard. Caesar gained little, yet Rome voted him a 20-day thanksgiving. In 54 BC he returned with five legions, defeated the Britons and imposed tribute.

## 54-53 BC: Ambiorix and the Winter Revolt {#ambiorix-revolt}

Harvests failed in 54 BC, but Caesar still kept his legions in Gaul over the winter and made the tribes feed them. To spread the burden he scattered their camps, which left each one isolated.

Ambiorix, the shrewd leader of the Eburones, a tribe of north-eastern Gaul, saw his chance. He told the commanders of one camp that all of Gaul was rising and the Germans were invading. He promised them safe passage if they left. They believed him and were ambushed in a steep valley. About a legion and a half was wiped out.

Rebels then besieged a second camp for more than two weeks, using siege towers built on Roman models. Caesar rescued it after forced marches of about 32 km (20 miles) a day. By then more than 90 per cent of its defenders had been killed or wounded.

In 53 BC Caesar answered with terror. He burned villages, seized livestock and took captives until tribe after tribe surrendered, Ambiorix's Eburones among them. Northern Gaul was left flattened, and Gallic hatred deepened.

## Vercingetorix Unites Gaul {#vercingetorix}

Vercingetorix was born around 82 BC at Gergovia, chief town of the Arverni, a powerful tribe of central Gaul. His father, a nobleman, had been killed by his own people for trying to make himself king. The name Vercingetorix means roughly "supreme king of warriors" and was probably a title. His real name is lost.

In 53 BC Caesar declared that Gaul would live under Roman law and religion. The Gauls feared for their sacred heartland, where druids, their priests, met each year to settle quarrels between tribes. Early in 52 BC pro-Roman nobles exiled Vercingetorix for urging revolt. He returned, drove them out and was proclaimed king. That winter he built an alliance of tribes from most of Gaul, something never seen before.

His plan was to starve the Romans by raiding their foragers and burning towns he could not hold. Caesar stormed Avaricum, a fortified town of central Gaul, and claimed 40,000 killed. Yet the alliance held. At Gergovia, Vercingetorix inflicted a clear defeat. Caesar admitted 700 dead, including 46 centurions, the army's veteran officers. Historians suspect the real losses were much higher.

## The Siege of Alesia {#siege-of-alesia}

In September 52 BC Vercingetorix fell back on Alesia, a hilltop fortified town (*oppidum*) of the Mandubii tribe. It almost certainly stood on Mont Auxois, above the modern village of Alise-Sainte-Reine in eastern France. Some 80,000 warriors were inside. Caesar chose to starve them out rather than storm the walls. Vercingetorix sent riders out by night to summon a relief army.

Caesar's answer became a classic of siege warfare. An inner ring about 16 km (eleven Roman miles) long penned the Gauls in. An outer ring about 21 km (14 Roman miles) long faced outward against the relievers. In front of the ditches, ramparts and towers lay hidden pits with fire-hardened stakes and iron hooks buried in the ground. Archaeology suggests the lines were less continuous than Caesar claimed, but they held.

As food ran out, the town's women, children, old and sick were sent out. Caesar refused to let them through, and they starved between the lines. Then the relief army arrived. Caesar says it was a quarter of a million strong, but historians suggest 50,000 to 100,000. Attacks from inside and out failed, and the relievers melted away. Vercingetorix surrendered.

## Mopping Up and the Road to Empire {#aftermath}

The victory at Alesia ended organised Gallic resistance. In 51 BC a few holdouts defended Uxellodunum, a hill fort in south-western Gaul. Roman tunnels diverted its spring, and the defenders, believing the gods had dried it up, surrendered. Caesar cut off their hands as a warning to others. In 50 BC the legions were withdrawn for a looming civil war. Gaul became formal provinces only in 27 BC, under Rome's first emperor, and its Latin later grew into French.

The war made Caesar rich and famous. The Senate decreed 20 days of thanksgiving for Alesia. For political reasons, though, it refused him a triumph (*triumphus*), the parade that crowned a general's career. In 49 BC he crossed the Rubicon, the river marking Italy's border, and won the civil war that followed. In 44 BC he was named dictator for life (*dictator perpetuus*).

Vercingetorix, the Gallic war leader, was held captive for about six years. In 46 BC he was paraded in Caesar's triumph and then strangled in the Tullianum, Rome's prison. Since the 19th century he has been celebrated as a French national hero.

## Caesar's Own Story: Can We Trust It? {#caesars-account}

Almost everything known about the war comes from Julius Caesar himself. His Commentaries on the Gallic War (*Commentarii de Bello Gallico*) reached Rome in instalments and kept his exploits before the public. The Gauls wrote no histories, so their side of the story is lost. The book's plain, clear Latin still makes it a classic school text.

Until the 20th century scholars largely trusted it. Since the Second World War they have grown sceptical. Caesar claims over a million Gauls killed and almost no Roman dead. He says a census written in Greek, found in the camp of the Helvetii, counted 368,000 people. Modern estimates for the Helvetii and their allies run from 20,000 to 50,000. One historian calls Caesar one of history's earliest "spin doctors".

Caesar presented the war as defence against barbarians. Historians agree it served mainly his career and his debts, though Gallic and Germanic raids on Roman land were real. Some call his massacre near the Rhine genocide. Others argue that by ancient standards he was not unusually brutal, and that his account holds more truth than critics allow.
